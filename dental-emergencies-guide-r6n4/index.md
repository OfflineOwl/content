# Dental Emergencies

> **A knocked-out adult tooth is on a clock measured in minutes.** Most dental problems can wait a few hours for a dentist. A few cannot: a tooth out of its socket, swelling that is spreading, bleeding that will not stop. This guide helps you tell them apart and hold things steady until you reach care.

## Do this now

**If an adult (permanent) tooth has been knocked out:**

1. **Find the tooth and pick it up by the crown,** the white part you chew with. Do not touch the root.
2. **If it is dirty, rinse it gently** in milk, saline, or the person's own saliva. A few seconds of clean water is the fallback. Do not scrub it, scrape it, dry it, or wrap it in tissue.
3. **Put it back in the socket right away** if the person is alert and cooperative. Line it up with the neighboring teeth, push it in gently, then have them bite softly on gauze or a clean cloth to hold it.
4. **Cannot get it back in? Keep it wet.** Put it in a small container of milk, or in the person's saliva (have them spit into a cup). An older child or adult can hold it inside the cheek, taking care not to swallow it. Do not store it in tap water and never let it dry out.
5. **Get to a dentist or emergency room immediately,** within 30 minutes if you can. Go even if more time has passed: teeth have been saved after an hour or more out of the mouth.

**If it is a baby tooth: do not put it back.** Pressing it in can damage the adult tooth growing underneath. Control the bleeding with gauze and call a dentist.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) or go to an emergency room, not a dental office, if someone has:

- **Trouble breathing, swallowing, or speaking**
- **Swelling that is spreading** to the eye, under the jaw, or down the neck, or a lot of swelling inside the mouth
- **A swollen or painful eye, or a sudden change in eyesight**
- **A mouth that will not open properly** along with tooth pain or swelling
- **Fever and feeling very unwell** with a toothache or swollen face
- **Bleeding from the mouth that does not stop** with firm, steady pressure
- **A blow to the face or head** followed by passing out, confusion, or repeated vomiting
- **Teeth that no longer meet,** or a jaw that will not close: the jaw may be broken

Do not drive yourself if you are having trouble breathing or swallowing. Outside the US, use your country's emergency number (112 in the EU, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia).

**See a dentist the same day** for a knocked-out or displaced tooth, a broken tooth that hurts or shows a pink or red spot in the middle, a throbbing toothache that will not let up, or a pimple-like bump on the gum.

## How urgent is it?

| Problem | How fast | First move |
|---------|----------|------------|
| Adult tooth knocked out | Minutes | Replant it, or keep it in milk, and go |
| Face or neck swelling, fever, trouble swallowing | Now: 911 or ER | Do not wait for a dentist |
| Bleeding that will not stop | Now: ER | Firm pressure with gauze on the way |
| Tooth pushed out of position or very loose | Same day | Leave it alone, soft food only |
| Broken tooth with pain or a red center | Same day | Rinse, cold pack on the cheek, pain relief |
| Throbbing toothache, bump on the gum | Same day | Pain relief, watch for red flags |
| Lost filling or crown, small painless chip | Days | Keep it clean, avoid chewing on that side |

## Knocked-out teeth: why the rules are so strict

The root of a tooth is coated in living cells that reattach it to the jaw. They are the whole game. They die when they dry out, they are scrubbed off by rubbing, and they cannot tolerate tap water for long. Milk happens to be gentle on them, which is why it beats water.

| Where the tooth waits | How good |
|-----------------------|----------|
| Back in its own socket | Best |
| Milk, saline, or a tooth preservation kit with the ADA Seal | Good |
| The person's saliva, in a cup or inside the cheek | Good |
| Tap water | Poor: avoid |
| Dry, in a tissue or a pocket | Worst |

- **Do not clean the tooth with soap, alcohol, or peroxide.**
- **Even if you cannot find the tooth, see a dentist** as soon as possible so the socket and the neighboring teeth can be checked.
- **Baby tooth or adult tooth?** Children start losing baby teeth at around age 6. If you are not sure which it is, do not replant it. Put it in milk and let the dentist decide.

## Broken, chipped, or loose teeth

1. Rinse the mouth with warm water to clean the area.
2. Hold a cold compress against the cheek to keep swelling down.
3. Press gauze on any bleeding spot.
4. Save any pieces you can find and bring them with you.
5. A sharp edge can be covered with dental (orthodontic) wax so it does not cut the tongue.

A small chip that does not hurt is not an emergency, though it should still be repaired. A break that shows a pink or red spot, bleeds from the middle of the tooth, or hurts with air and cold has reached the nerve: see a dentist the same day.

**A loose or pushed-in tooth:** do not wiggle it or force it back. Eat soft food, keep it clean, and get it seen the same day.

## Toothache

A toothache with no injury usually means decay, a crack, or an infection starting. Home care buys comfort and time. It does not fix the cause.

- **Rinse with warm salt water:** about half a teaspoon of salt in a cup (8 oz / 240 mL) of warm water. Swish and spit.
- **Floss gently** around the tooth. Trapped food is a common cause of pain.
- **Take an over-the-counter pain reliever** as the label directs (see below).
- **Cold compress on the cheek** if there is swelling.
- **Eat soft food** and avoid very hot or very cold food and drink.

**Do not put aspirin on the tooth or gum.** It irritates the tissue and causes mouth ulcers. Do not poke into a cavity with anything sharp.

**Clove oil is not the cure it is made out to be.** The US National Library of Medicine finds no good scientific evidence that it works for toothache, it can irritate and damage the gums, and swallowing even small amounts can cause seizures and liver damage in children. Leave it out of the kit.

**Numbing gels (benzocaine):** adults can use them sparingly and briefly. The FDA warns against benzocaine products for teething and for children under 2, because they can cause a rare, dangerous blood condition that starves the body of oxygen.

## Pain relief within the label

For dental pain, the American Dental Association puts anti-inflammatory pain relievers such as ibuprofen first, alone or together with acetaminophen. The two work differently, so they can be taken at the same time.

| Medicine | Adult label dose | 24-hour limit |
|----------|------------------|---------------|
| Ibuprofen 200 mg tablets | 1 tablet every 4 to 6 hours; 2 tablets if 1 is not enough | 6 tablets (1,200 mg) unless a doctor directs otherwise |
| Acetaminophen | As your product's label directs | Never more than 4,000 mg from all products combined (FDA); many labels set a lower limit |

- **Write down each dose and the time.** Pain makes people lose count, and more than the label dose does not work better.
- **Acetaminophen hides in other products,** including many cold, flu, and sleep medicines. Count all of it. Too much can cause liver failure. The risk is higher with three or more alcoholic drinks a day.
- **Ask a doctor or pharmacist before taking ibuprofen** if you have had stomach ulcers or bleeding, have kidney disease, heart disease, or high blood pressure, take blood thinners or steroids, have asthma, or are 60 or older. Do not take it at 20 weeks of pregnancy or later unless a doctor tells you to.
- **Children:** use children's products and the dosing chart on the package. Do not give adult tablets to children. Never give aspirin to children or teenagers.
- **If label doses do not control the pain,** that is a reason to be seen sooner, not to take more.

## Dental abscess

An abscess is a pocket of pus from a bacterial infection in a tooth or the gum. Signs include a severe, constant, throbbing toothache, pain when chewing, a bad taste, swollen gums or a pimple-like bump on the gum, swollen glands in the neck, and sometimes fever.

It matters because the infection can spread to the jaw, the neck, and the bloodstream. Swelling of the jaw or face is a serious sign.

**Until you are seen:**

- Rinse with warm salt water and take pain relievers within the label.
- Eat soft food and keep drinking fluids.
- Check twice a day for the red flags in **Get emergency help if** above. Any of them means an emergency room now.

**Do not:**

- **Lance, squeeze, or drain it yourself.** That is a job for a clinician with sterile instruments.
- **Take leftover or stockpiled antibiotics.** The ADA's guideline is that most dental pain and swelling should be treated with dental work and pain relievers, not antibiotics. Antibiotics are added when there are signs the infection is spreading through the body, such as fever or feeling ill, and that is exactly when you need a clinician, not a pill bottle. They also do nothing about the source: the tooth still needs drainage, a root canal, or removal.
- **Try to pull the tooth.** Extraction needs a dentist with anesthetic, instruments, and X-rays. A home attempt can break the tooth and cause bleeding you cannot control.

## Lost fillings, crowns, braces, and stuck objects

- **Lost filling or crown:** this is rarely urgent. Keep the crown and bring it to the dentist. Keep the tooth clean, chew on the other side, and avoid very hot, cold, or hard food. If the tooth is sensitive, dab toothpaste for sensitive teeth onto it and leave it on. Pharmacies sell temporary filling material: if you use it, follow the package and treat it as cover for a few days, not a repair. Never use household glue in the mouth.
- **Something stuck between teeth:** work it out gently with dental floss. Never use a pin, knife, or other sharp tool.
- **Bitten tongue or lip:** clean it gently with water and hold a cold compress on it. Bleeding that will not stop needs an emergency room.
- **Braces wire poking the cheek:** cover the end with orthodontic wax and call the orthodontist.

## When no dentist is available for days

After a disaster, dental offices may be closed for a while. The honest position: you can manage pain and watch for danger, but you cannot treat the tooth.

- **You can:** control pain within the label, rinse with salt water, eat soft food, keep the mouth clean, and check for red flags twice a day.
- **You cannot safely:** pull teeth, drain an abscess, or dose yourself with antibiotics.
- **An emergency room cannot fix a tooth, but it can treat a spreading infection, a broken jaw, and bleeding.** Go for the red flags even when you know there is no dentist there.
- **Keep brushing** twice a day with fluoride toothpaste. Under a boil-water notice, brush with bottled, boiled, or treated water. See the Water Purification guide.
- **In a shelter,** tell the medical staff about any swelling or fever so they can decide whether you need a hospital.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a tooth rescue with a pebble or a dried bean standing in for the tooth. Start a timer. Pick the "tooth" up by one end only, find a small lidded container, pour in milk (or note where the shelf-stable milk is), drop the tooth in, and find your dentist's after-hours number. Aim for under two minutes. Then save the dentist's number and the nearest emergency room in every phone in the house, and put a gauze pad and the container where you found the gap.

**What would you do?**

1. *Your nine-year-old takes a swing to the mouth at the playground. A front tooth is lying in the wood chips. Another parent hands you a tissue to wrap it in, and the dentist is 40 minutes away.*

   **Answer:** Skip the tissue: a dry tooth is a dying tooth. At nine, a front tooth is almost certainly a permanent one. Pick it up by the crown, rinse it for a few seconds in milk or saline (or water if that is all there is), and push it back into the socket, then have your child bite on a clean cloth and drive to the dentist. If you cannot get it in, carry it in milk or in your child's saliva. If your child was knocked out or is confused, that is an emergency room first.

2. *Your partner has had a toothache for three days. This morning the cheek is swollen, the swelling has reached under the jaw, and swallowing hurts. There is half a course of amoxicillin in the cabinet from last year.*

   **Answer:** Emergency room now, or 911 if breathing or swallowing gets harder. Swelling that is spreading under the jaw with trouble swallowing means the infection is moving into the neck, where it can close the airway. Old antibiotics are the tempting move and the wrong one: a leftover part-course was never prescribed for this, it delays care, and it does not drain the infection.

3. *Your three-year-old trips and knocks out a top front tooth. It is clean and whole, and it would slide right back in.*

   **Answer:** Do not put it back. A baby tooth pushed into the socket can damage the adult tooth forming above it. Have your child bite on gauze to stop the bleeding, use a cold compress on the lip, and call the dentist that day so the gum can be checked. If the bleeding will not stop or your child was dazed by the fall, go to an emergency room.
