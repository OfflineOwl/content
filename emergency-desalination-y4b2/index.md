# Emergency Desalination Methods

> **Never drink seawater.** Your kidneys can only make urine that is less salty than the sea, so every mouthful costs you more water than it gives. Salt can be removed, but every improvised method is slow and hungry for fuel or sun. Look for fresh water first, and start desalting early, before you are desperate.

## Do this now

1. **Don't drink seawater, not even a little, and don't mix it into fresh water to "stretch" it.** Don't drink urine either. Both speed up dehydration.
2. **Find fresh water before you make any.** Stored and bottled water, the water heater tank, rain caught in any clean container, and relief distribution points all beat desalination. See the Finding Safe Water Sources guide.
3. **Slow your losses.** Stay in the shade, rest through the hottest hours, and skip salty food, which makes you thirstier.
4. **If you have a pot, a lid, a cup, and heat: distill.** This is the only reliable home method for salt water. Steps are below.
5. **If you have sun and plastic sheeting but no fuel: build solar stills now, several of them.** They take hours to produce anything, and one still will not keep one person alive.
6. **Taste every batch before you rely on it.** Any salty taste means seawater splashed in. Run it again.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number), signal for rescue, or get to medical care if anyone:

- Is confused, very drowsy, faints, or cannot stand without dizziness
- Has passed little or no urine for many hours, or only small amounts of very dark urine
- Cannot keep fluids down because of vomiting
- Has drunk seawater and is vomiting, has diarrhea, or is acting strangely
- Is a baby, small child, older adult, or pregnant woman showing any of these signs: they dehydrate faster

At sea, use your marine radio's distress channel, an emergency beacon, or flares. Thirst is a late sign. The US Army survival manual lists it last, because you are already short of water by the time you feel it.

## Why seawater kills

Ocean water carries about 35,000 parts per million of dissolved salt, which is 3.5 percent, or roughly 35 grams in every liter (about 1.2 ounces per quart). Fresh water has less than 1,000 parts per million.

The US Geological Survey sorts water like this:

| Type | Dissolved salt (parts per million) |
|------|------------------------------------|
| Fresh water | Under 1,000 |
| Slightly saline | 1,000 to 3,000 |
| Moderately saline | 3,000 to 10,000 |
| Highly saline | 10,000 to 35,000 |
| Ocean water | About 35,000 |

Your kidneys get rid of salt by dissolving it in urine, and human urine is always less salty than seawater. To flush the salt from one cup of seawater, your body has to give up more than one cup of water. NOAA puts it bluntly: you die of dehydration even as you become thirstier.

Brackish water from estuaries, tidal rivers, and storm-surge flooding is less salty than the open sea, but you have no way to measure how much less, and USGS warns that water that is too salty is dangerous to drink. **If water tastes salty, treat it as salt water.**

## What does not remove salt

| Tempting idea | Why it fails |
|---------------|--------------|
| Boiling it | Boiling kills germs, but the salt stays in the pot and gets more concentrated as water steams away. Only the captured steam is fresh. |
| Bleach, iodine, or purification tablets | They kill germs. EPA notes that boiling and disinfection do not remove salts. |
| A backpacking or pitcher filter | Salt is dissolved, not suspended. It passes straight through cloth, sand, charcoal, ceramic, and hollow-fiber filters. |
| Small sips, or diluting seawater with fresh | Every bit of added salt has to be flushed out with water you can't spare. |
| Drinking urine | It returns the salt and waste your body just worked to remove. |

Only two processes take salt out: **distillation** (boil or evaporate the water, then catch the vapor) and **reverse osmosis** (force water through a membrane too fine for salt).

## Method 1: Pot distillation

This is FEMA's method, and it works on any stove, grill, or fire.

**You need:** a pot with a lid that has a handle or knob in the center, a heat-safe cup (metal, ceramic, or glass), string or wire, and a heat source.

1. Fill the pot about halfway with salt water.
2. Tie the cup to the lid's handle so the cup hangs right-side up when the lid is turned upside down.
3. Put the lid on upside down. Check that the cup hangs level and **does not touch the water**.
4. Bring the water to a boil and keep it boiling gently for 20 minutes. Steam condenses on the lid, runs to the handle at the low point, and drips into the cup.
5. Turn off the heat. Let everything cool before you lift the lid: the steam and the cup will scald.
6. Taste a drop. If it is fresh, pour it into a clean, covered container, then top up the pot and repeat.

**Make it work better:**

- **Simmer, don't rage.** A hard boil spits salt water into the cup.
- **Keep the lid cool.** Steam only turns back into water on a surface cooler than itself. A wet cloth laid on top of the upturned lid, re-wetted as it warms, helps. Don't let that cooling water run under the lid.
- **Never boil the pot dry.** Refill it before the level gets low. When the leftover brine turns thick and crusty, dump it and start with fresh seawater.
- **Use food-safe cookware.** No galvanized buckets, paint cans, or containers that held chemicals.

**The limit is fuel.** Turning boiling water into steam takes several times more heat than bringing it to a boil in the first place, and a lid-and-cup rig catches only part of that steam. Expect a small amount of water for each load of fuel. That is why distilling is for salt water only. If your water is fresh but dirty, a 1-minute rolling boil (3 minutes above 5,000 ft or 1,500 m) makes it safe for a fraction of the fuel. See the Complete Water Purification Guide.

**What it removes:** germs, salt, heavy metals, and most chemicals. It does not reliably remove chemicals that evaporate easily, such as fuels, solvents, and some pesticides, because they travel over with the steam. Don't distill harbor or flood water that has an oily sheen or a fuel smell.

## Method 2: Solar still

A solar still uses the sun to do the same job slowly: water evaporates under a plastic sheet, condenses on the underside, and drips into a container.

**You need:** a clear plastic sheet about 6 ft (2 m) square, a clean cup or bowl, a digging tool, and a small rock.

1. Pick a spot that gets sun most of the day. Dig in the cool of early morning or evening, not at midday.
2. Dig a bowl-shaped hole about 3 ft (1 m) across and 2 ft (60 cm) deep.
3. Set the container upright in the center of the hole.
4. For salt water, dig a narrow trench outside the hole, about 10 in (25 cm) from its lip, and pour the seawater into the trench. The soil carries the moisture into the hole and holds the salt back. Damp beach sand works the same way with no trench at all. Don't spill salt water on the rim where the plastic will sit.
5. Lay the plastic over the hole and bury its edges with soil so no air leaks out.
6. Put the rock in the center of the sheet so the plastic sags into a cone about 16 in (40 cm) deep, with the point directly over the container. The plastic must not touch the sides of the hole, or the soil will soak the water back up.
7. Leave it sealed. Every time you open it, you let the moist air out. A length of tubing run from the cup to the outside lets you drink without opening it.

**Be honest about the yield.** The US Army survival manual says one person needs **at least three** of these stills to cover a day's drinking water, and that assumes good sun and moist ground. USGS describes even purpose-built floating stills as producing "small amounts." Digging one in the heat can cost you more sweat than it returns. Build stills early, build several, and treat them as a supplement to rain and stored water.

A normally active adult needs about 3/4 gallon (3 liters) of fluid a day, and needs can double in very hot weather.

**Is still water safe?** Evaporation leaves salt and germs behind, but a pit still never gets hot enough to sterilize the cup, the plastic, or dirt that falls in. If the water looks dirty or the cup was not clean, disinfect it.

## Method 3: Reverse osmosis

Reverse osmosis (RO) pushes water through a membrane with pores of about 0.0001 micron. CDC lists RO as removing parasites, bacteria, viruses, and salt.

- **Hand-pump desalinators** are small reverse-osmosis units made for life rafts and boats. They are expensive, and pumping is slow, hard work, but they need no fuel and no sun. If you sail, or live somewhere a storm could cut you off beside the sea, this is the tool to buy ahead of time. Follow the maker's instructions for storage and flushing, or the membrane will not work when you need it.
- **Check the label.** A unit must say it is rated for seawater. Under-sink home RO systems, pitcher filters, and backpacking filters are built for fresh water and are not.

## Rain beats everything

On the coast or at sea, rain is the best fresh water you will get. Spread a tarp, sail, poncho, or plastic sheet with a low point over a container, and put out every pot and bucket you have. If the tarp is crusted with dried salt, rinse it in the sea first: the Army manual notes that the little seawater left clinging to it is hardly noticeable in the catch. Drink your fill while it rains and store the rest. On land, treat roof-caught rain before drinking. See the Rainwater Collection Basics guide.

## Common questions

**Can I dig a "beach well"?** Water that seeps into a hole dug behind the beach is sometimes fresh and often brackish, and you can't judge by looking. Taste a drop. Any saltiness means distill it like seawater. If it tastes fresh, it is still untreated ground water: boil or disinfect it.

**Does distilled water need treating?** Not if the cup and storage container were clean. It tastes flat. Pouring it back and forth between two clean containers improves it.

**Is the leftover brine useful?** Not for drinking or cooking. Pour it out away from your still and your garden.

## People who need extra care

- **Babies:** use ready-to-feed formula if you have it. Properly distilled water is fine for mixing formula. Never use salty or brackish water.
- **Children, older adults, pregnant and nursing women, and anyone sick** dehydrate faster. Give them fresh water first.
- **Pets** can't drink seawater either. Count them in your water plan.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Rig the pot still cold. Take your largest pot, its lid, a mug, and a piece of string, and get the mug hanging level under the upturned lid without touching the bottom. That fiddly knot is the hard part, and it is much easier to learn at the kitchen counter than over a fire. If you have another half hour, stir a tablespoon of salt into a quart (liter) of tap water, run the still for 20 minutes with an adult at the stove, let it cool, then taste and measure what you made. Seeing how little comes out is the lesson.

**What would you do?**

1. *A hurricane has cut off your barrier island. It is day two, the taps are dry, and three of you have two gallons of bottled water left. A neighbor says sailors mix one part seawater into four parts fresh to make it last.*

   **Answer:** Don't. Every gram of salt you swallow has to be flushed out with fresh water, so the mix leaves you worse off than drinking the fresh water alone. Close your main valve and drain the water heater (often 30 to 80 gallons), put out containers for rain, stay shaded and still, and start distilling with whatever fuel you have.

2. *You have one small canister of camp-stove fuel. In the yard is a rain barrel of murky roof water, and 50 yards away is the sea. You've just built a pot still.*

   **Answer:** Use the barrel. Fresh water that is dirty only needs clearing through a cloth and a 1-minute rolling boil, which uses a fraction of the fuel that distilling does. Save the still for the day the fresh water is gone.

3. *Your first cup from the pot still tastes faintly salty. Everyone is thirsty and it took 20 minutes to make.*

   **Answer:** Don't drink it. Salt water boiled over or splashed into the cup, or the cup was dipping in the pot. Pour the cup back, lower the water level, shorten the string, turn the heat down to a gentle boil, and run it again. A salty batch costs you water; a second run only costs fuel.
