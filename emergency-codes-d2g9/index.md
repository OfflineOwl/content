# Emergency Codes & Signals

> **"Code Yellow" has meant ten different things in the hospitals of a single US state.** Emergency codes are local habits, not a shared language. This guide explains what the common hospital, school and public-alert terms usually mean, which few are truly standard, and how to talk on a phone or radio so that nobody has to decode you.

## Start Here

1. LEARN THE ONE RULE: a code means whatever that building says it means. If you hear one you do not know, ask a staff member or follow what staff are doing. Never guess.
2. LEARN THE TERMS THAT ARE STANDARD: weather WATCH versus WARNING, and the sound your phone makes for an emergency alert (below).
3. ASK YOUR CHILD'S SCHOOL which words it uses (lockdown, secure, hold, shelter) and what each one means for parents.
4. PRACTICE SPELLING your surname and street with the phonetic alphabet (Alfa, Bravo, Charlie...).
5. AGREE ON ONE FAMILY PASSWORD for pick-ups and for checking that a caller is who they say they are.

---

## Get Emergency Help If

Someone is in danger. Call 911 (or your local emergency number) and speak plainly. Dispatchers do not want codes from you. Say:

- WHAT is happening ("my father has collapsed and is not breathing")
- WHERE (address, floor, room, nearest cross street)
- WHO you are and the number you are calling from

In a hospital, school, store or office, tell the nearest staff member the same way, in plain words. Do not try to announce a code yourself. For a fire, use the fire alarm pull station.

Outside the US the emergency number differs; 112 works across the European Union.

---

## Why Codes Cannot Be Trusted

Color codes were invented so staff could be called to an emergency without alarming the public. Each hospital made up its own, and they never converged.

- A review of Pennsylvania healthcare facilities found 80 different emergency codes covering 37 kinds of event. "Code Yellow" had 10 different meanings and "Code Orange" had 9.
- California hospitals were once found to be using 47 different codes for an infant abduction and 61 for a combative person.
- "Code Green" has meant an oxygen system failure in one hospital and a violent incident in another.
- A nurse working in several Washington State hospitals called a "Code Blue" for a patient who had stopped breathing. In that hospital, Code Blue summoned armed police.

There is no national standard in the US. More than 25 state hospital associations have recommended standard code lists for their own states, and those lists differ from each other. The Emergency Nurses Association, federal incident-management doctrine and at least ten state hospital associations now support replacing codes with PLAIN LANGUAGE: "Medical emergency, cardiac arrest, room 123."

## Hospital Codes You May Hear

The list below is ONE widely copied set, recommended by the Hospital Association of Southern California. Treat it as "what this often means," never as fact for the building you are in.

```
  Code Blue     Adult medical emergency, such as cardiac arrest
  Code White    Child medical emergency
  Code Red      Fire
  Code Pink     Infant abduction
  Code Purple   Child abduction
  Code Gray     Combative person
  Code Silver   Person with a weapon, active shooter or hostage
                situation
  Code Orange   Hazardous material spill or release
  Code Green    Missing patient
  Code Yellow   Bomb threat
```

How reliable is each line?

- CODE BLUE is the most widely and consistently used hospital code in the US, and CODE RED for fire is common. Even these have exceptions, as the story above shows.
- Everything else varies. Yellow, Orange, Purple, White, Silver and Green have each carried several different meanings.
- Some hospitals use numbers, letters or names instead ("Code 99", "Dr. Quick").

What a patient or visitor should do:

- Stay in the room, out of the corridor, unless staff tell you otherwise. Corridors fill with people and equipment moving fast.
- Ask the nearest staff member: "Do I need to do anything?"
- If the announcement is in plain language, do what it says. A typical one: move away from the named location if it is safe to; if not, shelter where you are.
- At admission, ask: "What will I hear if there is a fire or an evacuation, and what should I do?"

## School Terms

Many US schools use the Standard Response Protocol from the "I Love U Guys" Foundation. Many others use their own words. The five SRP actions and the instruction that goes with each:

```
  HOLD      "In your room or area. Clear the halls."
            Hallways need to be kept empty.
  SECURE    "Get inside. Lock outside doors."
            A hazard or threat is OUTSIDE the building. People
            are brought in and the outer doors are locked.
  LOCKDOWN  "Locks, lights, out of sight."
            A threat is INSIDE the building. Rooms are locked,
            people stay quiet and hidden.
  EVACUATE  To an announced location.
  SHELTER   For a named hazard, with the safety action stated
            (for example, a tornado or a hazardous spill).
```

Two points catch parents out:

- SECURE IS NOT LOCKDOWN. Schools using this protocol dropped the old word "lockout" in 2021 because people confused it with "lockdown."
- YOUR SCHOOL MAY USE DIFFERENT WORDS, or the same words with different meanings ("shelter in place," "soft lockdown," "lockout"). Ask for its emergency guide for parents and keep it with your family plan.

Ask the school now how it will tell you what is happening and how children are released afterward. During an incident, follow its instructions on whether and where to come. See the Emergency Communications Plan guide.

## Stores and Public Buildings

CODE ADAM is a missing-child procedure from the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children, used in many stores, parks and government buildings. It was created in memory of six-year-old Adam Walsh. If your child goes missing in a building, tell the nearest employee immediately, in plain words, with a description of the child and their clothing. You do not need to know the code.

Other overhead pages in stores and offices vary by company.

## Public Alerts: The Terms That Are Standard

Unlike building codes, these mean the same thing across the US.

WEATHER (National Weather Service)

```
  WATCH      Be prepared. The hazard is possible in and near
             the area.
  WARNING    Take action. The hazard is happening or about to.
  EMERGENCY  The highest level (for example, Tornado
             Emergency). Severe threat to life. Shelter now.
```

Memory aid: a watch means watch the sky and your radio. A warning means go.

PHONE AND BROADCAST ALERTS

- WIRELESS EMERGENCY ALERT (WEA): looks like a text, with a unique sound and vibration repeated twice. No sign-up is needed, and it is not affected by network congestion.
- EMERGENCY ALERT SYSTEM (EAS): the interruption on radio and TV.
- AMBER ALERT: an abducted child. The name stands for America's Missing: Broadcast Emergency Response.
- BLUE ALERT: a suspect who has killed or seriously injured a law enforcement officer, or an officer missing in the line of duty. Run by states; 37 states have a plan.
- SILVER ALERT (name varies by state): typically a missing older adult with dementia or a similar condition. Rules differ from state to state.

WHAT IS NOT STANDARD

- OUTDOOR SIRENS. The meaning of a siren pattern is set by each community. Treat any siren as "go inside and check the radio or your phone."
- EVACUATION WORDING. Levels, zones and "warning" versus "order" differ by state and county. Look up your county's terms before you need them.

The Radio Use for Emergency Updates guide has more on alerts.

## Plain Language: The Habit to Copy

US emergency services run on a shared doctrine called NIMS. It tells responders to use "plain language and clear text, not codes," and calls that a matter of public safety. The reason is the same as in hospitals: police "ten-codes" and agency jargon are not the same from one agency to the next.

For a family or a neighborhood:

- Say what you mean. "Tree on the power line at number 20," not "we have a situation."
- If you use color status words such as the Green / Yellow / Red / Black check-in in the Emergency Communications Plan guide, write the meanings on every card. They are a family convention and mean nothing to outsiders.
- On GMRS radio, FCC rules do not allow coded messages or messages with hidden meanings.
- "Privacy codes" on walkie-talkies do not hide your words from anyone.

## Talking on a Two-Way Radio

Only one person can talk at a time, and nobody hears you while they are talking. Good habits fix most problems:

1. LISTEN first. Wait for the channel to be clear.
2. THINK about what you will say. Keep it short.
3. PRESS, PAUSE, SPEAK. Hold the button, wait a beat so the first word is not cut off, then talk across the microphone in a normal voice. Shouting distorts.
4. SAY WHO YOU ARE CALLING, THEN WHO YOU ARE: "Maria, this is Dad, over."
5. READ BACK anything that matters (addresses, numbers, times) and ask the sender to confirm.

Words worth knowing:

```
  THIS IS      Identifies who is speaking
  OVER         I have finished, your turn to reply
  OUT          I have finished, no reply expected
               (never "over and out": it is one or the other)
  GO AHEAD     I am listening, send your message
  ROGER        I received all of that (it does not mean "yes")
  AFFIRMATIVE  Yes
  NEGATIVE     No
  SAY AGAIN    Repeat that
  STAND BY     Wait a moment
  WILCO        Received, understood, and I will do it
```

DISTRESS WORDS: "MAYDAY," said three times, is the aviation and marine call for a life-threatening emergency, and "PAN-PAN" is the call for an urgent problem that is not yet life-threatening. They belong on aircraft and boat radios. On a family walkie-talkie, say "EMERGENCY, EMERGENCY," then what and where. (GMRS rules reserve "Mayday" for a ship, aircraft or vehicle in immediate danger.) Everyone else on the channel should stop and listen.

For whistle, light and ground signals, see the Signal Flags, Whistles & Visual Signals guide.

## The Phonetic Alphabet

B, D, E, P and T sound alike on a bad line. The international (ICAO) alphabet replaces each letter with a word that cannot be mistaken. Use it on a radio or on the phone with 911, an insurer or a pharmacy, for names, streets and license plates.

```
  A  Alfa        J  Juliett     S  Sierra
  B  Bravo       K  Kilo        T  Tango
  C  Charlie     L  Lima        U  Uniform
  D  Delta       M  Mike        V  Victor
  E  Echo        N  November    W  Whiskey
  F  Foxtrot     O  Oscar       X  Xray
  G  Golf        P  Papa        Y  Yankee
  H  Hotel       Q  Quebec      Z  Zulu
  I  India       R  Romeo
```

("Alfa" and "Juliett" are the official spellings.)

Numbers are said one digit at a time. Three are pronounced oddly on purpose: 3 is "tree," 5 is "fife," 9 is "niner." So 59 Oak Street is "fife niner Oak Street."

Example: "Last name Nguyen. I spell: November, Golf, Uniform, Yankee, Echo, November."

If you forget a word, any clear word works ("N as in Nancy"). The goal is to be understood, not to pass a test.

## A Family Password

One private word does two jobs:

- PICK-UPS. A child does not go with anyone, including someone they know, who cannot say the password. "No password, no go." Then find a teacher or another trusted adult.
- PHONE CALLS. If a caller says they are a relative in trouble and needs money or a sudden change of plan, ask for the password. No password: hang up and call the person back on the number you already have.

Make it easy to remember and hard to guess, not a pet's name that is on social media. Change it after it has been used with someone outside the family.

Optional, for teenagers: one agreed phrase or text that means "come and get me, no questions now." Keep it to that. Long lists of secret phrases are forgotten when they are needed.

## People Who Need Extra Thought

- CHILDREN: teach the alert sound on a phone, the school's words, and the family password. Tell them an adult who really was sent by you will know it.
- DEAF OR HARD OF HEARING: overhead announcements will be missed. In a hospital, ask staff at admission how you will be told about an emergency. Keep phone alerts and vibration on.
- PEOPLE WHO SPEAK LITTLE ENGLISH: plain-language announcements help, codes do not. Ask for the facility's emergency information in your language.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Phonetic roll call. Each person spells their full name and your street out loud using the alphabet above, then reads a made-up phone number digit by digit ("fife, fife, fife, zero, one, niner, tree"). The listener writes down what they hear and reads it back. Swap roles. Then pick one building you use every week (school, workplace, clinic) and find out what its emergency announcements say.

### What Would You Do?

1. You are visiting your father on ward 4 West when the overhead speaker says, "Code Gray, 4 West." Years ago you worked in a hospital where Code Gray meant severe weather, so you step into the corridor to look out of the window.

   **Answer:** Step back into the room. You do not know what Gray means in this building, and in many hospitals it means a combative person, on the very ward you are standing in. Stay with your father, keep the doorway clear, and ask the first staff member you see whether you need to do anything.

2. A text from your child's school reads: "The school is in SECURE status. Students are safe and classes are continuing." Your instinct is to drive there and bring your child home.

   **Answer:** Do not go yet. In the protocol many schools use, Secure means a hazard outside the building: the outer doors are locked and coming and going is stopped or tightly controlled. You may not be able to collect your child, and you would be heading toward whatever the hazard is. Wait for the school's next message and follow its release instructions. If you are not sure what your school means by the word, that is the question to ask this week.

3. During a blackout your street is using walkie-talkies. A neighbor comes on: "Uh, we've got a situation at the Hendersons', it looks pretty bad, can somebody come?"

   **Answer:** Nobody can act on that. Ask for plain facts and read them back: "This is number 12. Say again: what has happened, and what is the address?" The useful version is: "Emergency. Mr. Henderson has fallen from a ladder at 31 Oak. Tree one Oak. He is awake but cannot move his leg. We need someone to call 911. Over." Then one person confirms they are making the call.
