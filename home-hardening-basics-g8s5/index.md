# Home Hardening Basics

> **Most homes fail at a few cheap weak points.** A door frame held by short screws, a garage door that buckles in wind, a gutter full of dry leaves. Fix those first. A box of long screws and a slow walk around the house do more than any gadget.

## Start here

Six jobs you can do today, most of them free:

1. **Walk around the outside like a stranger.** Which door or window is hidden from the street? What is loose enough to blow away, dry enough to burn, or handy enough to climb on?
2. **Put 3-inch (7.5 cm) screws in the strike plates** of every exterior door, so they bite into the wall framing and not just the thin door jamb.
3. **Check every lock.** Each exterior door needs a deadbolt. Each sliding door needs a stick or dowel lying in its track.
4. **Find and tag your shutoffs:** the main water valve, the electrical panel and the gas meter valve.
5. **Test the smoke alarms,** and confirm that every door and window you might escape through opens from the inside without a key.
6. **Film every room** on your phone for insurance, and store a copy away from home.

## Get emergency help if

- **You smell gas or hear a hissing or blowing sound.** Open a window, get everyone out, and call the gas company or 911 (or your local emergency number) from outside.
- **You come home to a forced door or a broken window.** Do not go in. Go to a safe place and call 911.
- **Someone is breaking in while you are home.** Get out if you can do it safely. If not, lock yourself in a room and call 911.
- **A power line is down on or near the house.** Stay well back and call the utility or 911.

## The rule that comes first: you must be able to get out

Ready.gov says a home fire can become life-threatening in about two minutes. Every security upgrade has to pass one test: can a sleepy child or a guest still get out, in the dark, without a key?

- **Use single-cylinder deadbolts,** with a key outside and a thumb turn inside. A deadbolt keyed on both sides can trap you in a fire, and some building codes ban them in homes.
- **Window bars and grilles need a quick-release latch** on the inside. Check it regularly so it does not rust shut.
- **Pin windows with a removable nail or pin,** never a screw or bolt.
- **Keep two ways out of every room,** and smoke alarms on every level.

## Doors

Police who survey homes describe the typical burglar as low to medium skilled, working with a rock, a screwdriver or a pair of pliers, and getting through weak doors and windows in well under a minute. The goal is not a fortress. It is a door that takes long enough, and makes enough noise, that the easy target is somewhere else.

A lock is only as strong as the wood it is screwed to. Many strike plates are held to the soft door jamb by short screws, so one kick splits the jamb and the lock comes with it. Long screws anchor the plate to the wall framing behind.

| Part | What to aim for | Why |
|------|-----------------|-----|
| **Door** | Metal or solid wood, at least 1 3/4 inches (4.4 cm) thick. Replace hollow-core or rotted exterior doors. | A hollow door breaks before the lock does. |
| **Deadbolt** | Single-cylinder, bolt throw of at least 1 inch (2.5 cm), ANSI Grade 1 or 2 | The latch in a doorknob can be slipped or forced. |
| **Strike plate** | Heavy-duty, four screws, each at least 3 inches (7.5 cm) long | Ties the lock to the wall framing. |
| **Hinges** | Swap one screw in each hinge for a 2 1/2-inch (6 cm) screw. On doors that open outward, use hinges with non-removable pins. | Stops the door being forced from the hinge side. |
| **Viewer** | A wide-angle peephole, or a camera | You can see who is there without opening. A chain does not hold. |
| **Glass near the lock** | A grille or security film over glass within reach of the thumb turn | Stops someone breaking the pane and reaching in. |

**Strike plate upgrade, 15 minutes:** remove the old plate, hold the heavy-duty one in the same position, drill pilot holes, and drive the long screws. Check that the bolt still slides fully home. Wear eye protection, and make sure no wire or pipe runs where you drill. Renters: ask the owner first.

**Sliding glass doors** are held by a latch, not a lock. Lay a wooden dowel or a hinged security bar in the bottom track. Add round-head screws in the top track, set so the door just slides under them, so it cannot be lifted out. A pin through both frames adds a third layer.

**Garage:** lock the door between the garage and the house every time. Do not leave the remote in a car parked outside. On a long trip, unplug the opener or lock the track.

**Keys:** no spare under the mat, in a planter or over the door frame. Burglars check those places. Leave one with a neighbor you trust, and re-key the locks when you move in.

**Pet doors** bigger than about 9 by 10 inches (23 by 25 cm) are big enough to be used as a way in. Fit one that locks.

## Windows

- Lock them, including upstairs windows that can be reached from a porch roof, a tree or a ladder.
- **Double-hung windows:** drill a small hole at a downward angle through the inner sash into the outer one and slide in a nail. Drill a second hole a few inches up if you want to lock it slightly open for air.
- **Sliding windows:** treat them like sliding doors, with a dowel in the track and screws above.
- **Window air conditioners:** brace them from the inside so they cannot be pushed in or pulled out.
- **Basement windows:** grilles are fine if they open from the inside.
- Put ladders and tools away. Left in the yard, they are a burglar's kit.

## Light and sightlines

Burglars want to work unseen. Take that away.

- **Trim shrubs** to about 3 feet (0.9 m) and below windowsill height. **Prune tree limbs** up to about 6 feet (1.8 m) from the ground. A person standing at any door or window should be visible from the street or a neighbor's house.
- **Light every entry** all night with dusk-to-dawn fixtures, or use motion lights. An ordinary porch bulb is enough. Aim lights down so they do not glare into the street or the house next door.
- **Lock gates.** A locked gate does not stop a climber, but it makes anyone climbing it look wrong, and it is hard to carry a television over.
- **Make your house number easy to read** from the street at night, at least 4 to 5 inches (10 to 13 cm) high. Responders cannot help at a house they cannot find.
- **When you are away:** lights on timers in different rooms, mail held or collected, and a neighbor who knows. A light burning 24 hours a day signals an empty house. Do not post travel plans online.
- **Know your neighbors.** People who recognize what is normal on the street, and who will call it in, beat any device.

An alarm system is optional. Police crime prevention units suggest matching it to your actual risk and not buying one by reflex.

## Weather: harden for the hazards you actually have

Find out which hazards apply to your address from your local emergency management office and the flood maps. Then pick the matching rows.

| Hazard | Weak points | What to do |
|--------|-------------|------------|
| **High wind** (hurricane, tornado, severe storm) | Roof, garage door, windows, loose objects | Fix loose shingles. Brace or replace a weak garage door. Fit storm shutters, impact glass or shatter-resistant film. Bring in or tie down furniture, bins and toys. Cut out dead and damaged limbs. Clear drains and gutters. |
| **Flood** | Low floors, drains, uninsured contents | Buy flood insurance early. Ask a plumber about check valves and a sump pump with battery backup. Move valuables up, and keep documents in a waterproof container. |
| **Earthquake** | Tall furniture, water heater, items on walls and shelves | Anchor bookcases, refrigerators, water heaters and televisions. Keep heavy and breakable things on low shelves. Ask a professional about structural weak points. |
| **Wildfire** | Embers on the roof, in gutters and vents, and against the walls | Keep the first 5 feet (1.5 m) around the house free of anything that burns. Clean the roof and gutters. Cover vents with 1/8-inch (3 mm) metal mesh. |

A few points worth knowing:

- **Wind.** Garage doors and double doors are common failure points. FEMA suggests a heavy-duty deadbolt, slide bolts on the inactive leaf of a double door, and longer hinge screws. That is the same work that slows a burglar. Roof straps and garage door bracing are jobs for a contractor.
- **A house built to code is not a tornado shelter.** FEMA is blunt that even wind-resistant homes do not give reliable protection in strong tornadoes. Choose your refuge spot now. See the Tornado Safety and Safe Room Setup guides.
- **Flood.** Homeowners insurance does not cover flooding, and a new flood policy typically takes 30 days to start. The night before the storm is too late.
- **Earthquake.** Standard homeowners policies do not cover earthquake damage either. See the Earthquake Response guide.
- **Wildfire.** Most of the risk sits within 100 feet (30 m) of the house, and embers do much of the damage. Work outward: 0 to 5 feet, then 5 to 30 feet (1.5 to 9 m), then 30 to 100 feet. Cut back limbs within 10 feet (3 m) of the chimney or over the roof, and keep grass mowed to about 4 inches (10 cm). See the Wildfire Preparedness guide.
- **Trees near power lines** are the utility's job, not yours. Call them.

## Utilities: find, tag, practice

Every adult and teenager in the home should be able to find these in the dark.

- **Gas:** the main valve is at the meter. Shut it off only if you smell gas or hear it escaping, and only after everyone is out. Do not turn it off for practice. Once it is off, only a qualified professional should turn it back on.
- **Water:** find the main valve where the line enters the house and tag it. Close and reopen it once to prove it works. A valve that is rusted open should be replaced. Closing it also traps the clean water in your heater and pipes. See the Household Water Shutoff and Safety guide.
- **Electricity:** at the panel, switch off the individual circuits first, then the main. Never touch the panel with wet hands or while standing in water. Call the utility.

## Think twice

- **Deadbolts keyed on both sides,** and bars with no release: they keep you in as well as others out.
- **Door chains:** they do not hold against a shove. Use the viewer and keep the door shut.
- **Hidden spare keys.**
- **Traps or hazards meant for intruders.** The people most likely to meet them are your own family and the firefighters coming to help you.
- **Blocking every exit** when you feel threatened. Always keep two ways out.

## Renters and apartments

- Ask the owner in writing for a deadbolt, a viewer and re-keyed locks. Offer to do the long-screw upgrade yourself.
- A dowel in a sliding door track needs no permission.
- Report broken locks, dark hallways and garage gates that do not close.
- Do not buzz in people you don't know.
- Buy renters insurance and make the same room-by-room inventory.

## Record what you own

An inventory speeds up any claim, whether the cause was a storm or a thief.

- Film or photograph every room. Open the closets and drawers.
- Capture serial numbers on electronics and appliances.
- Include the garage and shed: tools, bikes, lawn and sports equipment.
- Store a copy away from home, in cloud storage, in your email or with a relative.
- Update it once a year and after big purchases. Ask your insurer whether jewelry, collections and similar items need extra coverage.

## Weekend checklist

- [ ] Long screws in every exterior strike plate and hinge
- [ ] Single-cylinder deadbolt on every exterior door
- [ ] Dowel and anti-lift screws on sliding doors and windows
- [ ] Door from garage to house locked, remote out of the car
- [ ] No hidden keys, spare with a neighbor
- [ ] Shrubs and limbs trimmed, entries lit, house number visible
- [ ] Shutoffs found, tagged and tested (not the gas)
- [ ] Smoke alarms tested, two exits from every room
- [ ] Loose items outside secured, gutters clear
- [ ] Tall furniture and water heater anchored (earthquake areas)
- [ ] First 5 feet around the house clear of fuel (wildfire areas)
- [ ] Insurance checked, including flood or earthquake if needed
- [ ] Video inventory made and stored off-site

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Do the screw test. Open your front door and take one screw out of the strike plate on the frame. Measure it. If it is shorter than 3 inches, you have found this week's job, so put it back and write "long screws" on the shopping list. Then turn off the lights and have each person in the household unlock and open every exit door by feel. Any lock that needs a key from the inside, or that a child cannot work, goes on the list too.

**What would you do?**

1. *Your front door has a glass panel beside the lock. A friend says to fit a deadbolt that needs a key on both sides, so nobody can break the glass and reach the thumb turn.*

   **Answer:** Protect the glass, not the lock. A deadbolt keyed on both sides means that in a fire, at night, everyone's escape depends on finding one key, and some building codes prohibit them for that reason. Keep the thumb turn, and cover the glass with a security grille or security film so it cannot be broken and reached through.

2. *A hurricane is forecast to reach your area in two days. You have never had flood insurance and you can see a few loose shingles. You are thinking of buying a policy online tonight and getting on the roof tomorrow.*

   **Answer:** Neither will help this time. A new flood policy usually takes 30 days to start, and roof repairs under time pressure are how people fall. Do the fast, low-risk work: bring in or tie down everything loose, clear drains and gutters, close shutters, move valuables up, put documents in a waterproof container, and film every room. Then follow any evacuation order. Put the policy and the roofer on the list for after the storm.

3. *You get back from a weekend away and find the back door ajar with the frame split. Your dog is somewhere inside.*

   **Answer:** Do not go in, even for the dog. Whoever forced the door may still be there. Go to your car or a neighbor's house, call 911, and wait for the police to check the house. Afterward, photograph the damage, list what is missing with serial numbers from your inventory, and call your insurer. When the frame is repaired, have it done with a heavy-duty strike plate and long screws.

## Quick Reference

```
HOME HARDENING

GET-OUT RULE
Every exit opens from inside,
no key needed. Two ways out of
every room. Test smoke alarms.

DOORS
- Solid or metal, 1 3/4 in thick
- Deadbolt: thumb turn inside,
  1 in (2.5 cm) throw or more
- Strike plate: 3 in (7.5 cm)
  screws into the framing
- One 2 1/2 in screw per hinge
- Sliding door: dowel in track
- No hidden keys

OUTSIDE
- Shrubs to 3 ft, limbs up 6 ft
- Entries lit all night
- Gates locked, ladders put away
- House number visible at night

WEATHER
- Wind: garage door, shutters,
  loose items, dead limbs
- Flood: insure 30 days ahead,
  valuables and papers up high
- Quake: anchor tall furniture
  and the water heater
- Wildfire: 0-5 ft clear, clean
  gutters, 1/8 in mesh on vents

UTILITIES
- Gas: off only for a leak;
  a professional turns it back on
- Water: tag and test main valve
- Power: breakers first, then main

BREAK-IN FOUND: don't enter,
go somewhere safe, call 911
```
