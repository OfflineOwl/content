# Water Rationing Guidelines

> **When water is limited, cut every use except drinking.** Official guidance is clear: don't ration drinking water unless authorities tell you to. Drink what you need today and work on finding more for tomorrow. Holding back drinking water causes dehydration, which leads to poor judgment, illness, and heat injury.

## Do This Now

1. Drink what you need. Don't cut drinking water to stretch supply.
2. Stop all non-essential water use: no flushing with drinking water, no laundry, no outdoor watering.
3. Reduce how much water your body needs: stay cool, stay in the shade, rest, and avoid hard work in the heat.
4. Avoid salty foods and alcohol. Both increase your need for water.
5. Count your supply and start finding more now (see FINDING MORE WATER below). Listen for official water distribution points.
6. Watch everyone, especially children, older adults, and sick people, for signs of dehydration (see below).

---

## How Much Water People Need

- A normally active person needs about 3/4 gallon (about 3 liters) of fluid a day, from water and other drinks.
- Children, nursing mothers, and people who are sick may need more.
- Needs can double in very hot weather.
- Fever, vomiting, and diarrhea all increase water needs.
- For planning, count 1 gallon per person per day for drinking plus basic sanitation.

There is no safe "survival minimum" to aim for. Less water than your body needs means dehydration, and in heat that can become an emergency within a day.

## Rationing Principles

1. KNOW YOUR SUPPLY
  - Add up all stored water, including hidden water at home (water heater, pipes, ice, toilet tank).
  - Estimate how long until service returns or you can resupply.

2. PROTECT DRINKING WATER FIRST Priority order for safe water:
  1. Drinking
  2. Medicines and medical needs (including mixing infant formula)
  3. Food preparation
  4. Handwashing before eating and after using the toilet
  5. Brushing teeth
  6. Other hygiene and cleaning

3. USE THE RIGHT WATER FOR THE JOB
  - Safe (treated or stored) water: drinking, food, medicine, brushing teeth.
  - Pool, rain, or untreated water: flushing toilets, cleaning, and washing (not drinking unless treated).

4. WASTE NOTHING
  - Reuse washing water for flushing.
  - Catch rainwater for cleaning and flushing.

## Conservation Levels

These levels cut non-drinking uses. Drinking water stays at what each person needs at every level.

LEVEL 1: CONSERVE (Supply adequate but uncertain)

- No lawn or garden watering
- Short showers, or sponge baths
- Full loads only for laundry and dishes
- Fix leaks
- Flush only when needed

LEVEL 2: CUT BACK (Supply limited, resupply expected)

- Drinking: what each person needs (about 3/4 gallon for an active adult, more in heat)
- Cooking: choose foods that need little or no water
- Hygiene: sponge baths and handwashing only
- No laundry
- Disposable plates and utensils, or wipe dishes before a minimal wash
- Flush with non-drinking water only

LEVEL 3: ESSENTIALS ONLY (Supply critical, resupply uncertain)

- Drinking water first
- Water for medicine and infant formula
- No-cook foods that don't need water
- Hand sanitizer instead of handwashing when hands aren't visibly dirty
- Bucket toilet instead of flushing
- Seek official water distribution or evacuate if you can

LEVEL 4: OUT OF WATER SOON (Supply nearly exhausted)

- Drink what you need now; don't save it for later
- Get help: call your local emergency management office or 911 if someone is in danger, go to a distribution point or shelter, or ask neighbors
- Stay still, cool, and in shade to reduce your body's needs
- Treat any available source (see the Water Purification guide)

## Water-Saving Techniques

REDUCE YOUR BODY'S NEED:

- Stay in the shade or the coolest part of the building
- Do essential work in the cooler early morning or evening
- Rest; avoid heavy exertion
- Wear light, loose clothing
- Avoid alcohol

EAT TO SAVE WATER:

- Avoid salty foods; they make you thirsty
- Choose salt-free crackers, whole-grain cereals, and canned foods with high liquid content (canned fruit, vegetables, soups)
- Avoid dehydrated and freeze-dried foods that need water
- Unlike water, food can be rationed safely for a while by healthy adults (not by children or pregnant women)

LOW-WATER HYGIENE:

- Hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol when soap and water are scarce (it doesn't work well on visibly dirty or greasy hands)
- Still wash hands with soap and water before preparing food and after using the toilet when you can; diarrhea costs far more water than handwashing
- Baby wipes or a damp cloth for a body wipe-down
- Brush teeth with a small cup of safe water

LOW-WATER SANITATION:

- Bucket toilet lined with a heavy-duty bag, with cat litter or sawdust to absorb liquid and odor
- Double-bag waste and keep it sealed and away from living areas and water sources
- Flush with non-drinking water only

## Special Situations

INFANTS:

- Breastfeeding needs no water and protects the baby. Mothers need extra fluids.
- Ready-to-feed formula needs no water. If you must mix formula, use bottled water, or water that was boiled and cooled.
- Never dilute formula more than the label says to stretch supply.

CHILDREN:

- Dehydrate faster than adults
- May not ask for water; offer it regularly
- Watch for dehydration signs closely

OLDER ADULTS:

- May not feel thirst
- Often take medicines that increase water loss
- Offer water regularly

PEOPLE WHO ARE SICK:

- Fever, vomiting, and diarrhea raise water needs sharply
- Use oral rehydration solution if available (see the Foodborne Illness guide)

PREGNANT AND NURSING WOMEN:

- Need more water than other adults

PETS:

- Need water too; count it in your supply
- Don't let pets drink floodwater or scummy water

HOT WEATHER:

- Needs can double
- Rest during the hottest hours
- Watch for heat exhaustion and heat stroke (see the heat guides)

## Calculating How Long Water Lasts

FORMULA: Total water ÷ (gallons per person per day × number of people) = days

EXAMPLE: 50 gallons ÷ (1 gallon × 4 people) = 12.5 days

ADJUST FOR:

- Hot weather (needs can double)
- Babies, sick people, nursing mothers, and pets
- When you expect service or resupply
- A safety margin: start looking for more water long before you run out

## Signs of Dehydration

MILD TO MODERATE:

- Thirst
- Dry or sticky mouth
- Peeing less than usual; dark yellow urine
- Headache, tiredness, muscle cramps
- Lightheadedness

INFANTS AND YOUNG CHILDREN:

- No wet diaper for 3 hours or more
- Crying without tears
- Dry mouth and tongue
- Sunken eyes
- Unusual sleepiness or irritability

SEVERE (MEDICAL EMERGENCY):

- No urine, or very dark urine
- Confusion, irritability, or extreme sleepiness
- Rapid heartbeat and rapid breathing
- Sunken eyes, dry shriveled skin
- Fainting or unconsciousness

WHAT TO DO:

- Mild to moderate: give water or oral rehydration solution in small, frequent sips, rest in the shade, and keep giving fluids.
- Call 911 or your local emergency number if the person is confused, has a seizure, loses consciousness, has a fever over 102°F (38.9°C), shows signs of heat stroke, or gets worse despite fluids.

## If There Isn't Enough for Everyone

This means you are in an emergency. Get help now: official distribution points, shelters, neighbors, or 911 if anyone is in danger. While you do, give water first to:

- Anyone showing signs of dehydration
- Infants and children
- People who are sick, elderly, pregnant, or nursing

---

## Finding More Water

WHILE CONSERVING, LOOK FOR MORE:

- Official water distribution points (listen to local radio and emergency alerts)
- Water heater tank and household pipes
- Toilet tank (not the bowl) if no cleaners have been added
- Ice cubes and liquid from canned fruit and vegetables
- Rainwater (treat before drinking)
- Streams, lakes, and springs (always treat) See the Finding Safe Water guide.

NEVER DRINK:

- Floodwater
- Pool or hot tub water (washing and cleaning only)
- Water from radiators, heating boilers, or waterbeds
- Toilet bowl water
- Water that smells of fuel or chemicals

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Count your water and do the sum. (1) Add up every gallon of drinking water in the house: bottles, jugs, and filled containers. Read the size of the water heater tank off its label and note it separately. Don't drain anything. (2) Use the formula in CALCULATING HOW LONG WATER LASTS: total gallons, divided by 1 gallon times the number of people. Count pets too. (3) Work it out again for a heat wave, when needs can double. (4) Write the answers on the Daily Water Log, and list the first three non-drinking uses your household would cut.

### What Would You Do?

1. The water has been off for two days in August. Four of you have 6 gallons left and there is no word on when service returns. Someone proposes two cups each per day so it lasts the week.

   **Answer:** Don't. Drinking water is the one use you do not cut. Everyone drinks what they need today, and the household's effort goes into finding more for tomorrow. At 1 gallon each per day, 6 gallons is about a day and a half for four people, so act now: stop flushing and washing with drinking water, eat no-cook foods, stay cool and still, drain the water heater and pipes, and find the official distribution point or a shelter.

2. Supplies are low and your 4-month-old is fed on powdered formula. A relative suggests mixing each bottle with extra water so the formula tin lasts longer.

   **Answer:** Never dilute formula more than the label says. Mix it as directed, with bottled water or water that was boiled and cooled. Water for formula comes straight after drinking water in the priority order, ahead of cooking and washing. If you are running out, that is the emergency: get to a distribution point or a shelter, or ask neighbors.

3. Your 78-year-old father says he isn't thirsty and wants his share saved for the grandchildren. By mid-afternoon he has a headache, his urine is dark, and he is lightheaded when he stands up.

   **Answer:** He is dehydrated, and he drinks now. Older adults often don't feel thirst, so "not thirsty" tells you nothing. Give water or oral rehydration solution in small, frequent sips, have him rest somewhere cool, and keep the fluids going. Anyone showing signs of dehydration gets water first. Call 911 if he becomes confused, faints, has a fever over 102°F (38.9°C), shows signs of heat stroke, or gets worse despite fluids.
