# PPE Basics for Civilians

> **Protective gear is the last line of defense, not the first.** A respirator, gloves, and goggles cut down what reaches you. They do not make a dangerous place safe, and the best-known mask of all, the N95, does nothing against gas or a lack of oxygen.

## Start here

1. **Buy real N95s and try one on today.** Look for "NIOSH", "N95", and an approval number starting TC-84A printed on the mask, and two straps that go around your head.
2. **Do a seal check** every time you put one on. If you have a beard where the mask seals, it will leak.
3. **Add the basics:** sealed goggles or safety glasses, a box of nitrile gloves, heavy work gloves, sturdy boots, and earplugs.
4. **Learn the removal order:** gloves, eye protection, gown, mask, then wash your hands. Taking gear off is when people contaminate themselves.
5. **Memorize the walk-away list** below. No mask you can buy at a hardware store makes those situations safe.

## Leave and get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) from a safe place:

- **You smell gas, see a chemical cloud, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds.** Get out. A dust mask or N95 does not filter any of these.
- **Someone has collapsed in an enclosed space** with exhaust, fumes, or bad air: a garage with a running generator, a tank, pit, well, silo, or basement. **Do not go in after them.** Call 911.
- **While working in a respirator you get dizzy or short of breath,** or a headache or nausea starts. Go to cleaner air and take it off. Call 911 for chest pain, or if the symptoms are severe or do not pass.
- **A chemical splashes in your eyes.** Flush with clean water for 10 to 15 minutes, then call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 in the US) or 911. See the Decontamination Basics guide.

## What PPE Can and Cannot Do

Safety professionals rank protection in order: get away from the hazard, then ventilate or wet it down, then wear PPE. Gear goes last because it fails quietly: a leak at the nose, a pinhole in a glove.

| Hazard | What helps | What does not |
|---|---|---|
| Germs from a sick person | A well-fitting N95, handwashing, fresh air | A loose mask with gaps |
| Wildfire smoke and ash | Staying indoors with cleaner air; an N95 or P100 outdoors | Bandanas (wet or dry), surgical masks, one-strap dust masks |
| Dust, debris, and mold during cleanup | N95 at minimum, goggles, gloves | A cloth over your face |
| Carbon monoxide, natural gas, propane, fuel and solvent fumes, chlorine or ammonia fumes | Leaving, and airing the space out from outside | Any N95 or P100: they filter particles only |
| Air without enough oxygen (tanks, pits, wells, silos, burning buildings) | Staying out | Every filtering mask, including cartridge "gas masks": they clean air but do not supply oxygen |
| A chemical release or unknown substance | Getting away, sheltering in place, decontaminating | Improvised suits and surplus masks |
| Old insulation, tile, or siding that may contain asbestos | Leaving it undisturbed for trained professionals | Sanding, sawing, or sweeping it in a dust mask |

## Respirators

A respirator seals to your face and makes the air you breathe pass through a filter. A mask that does not seal is not a respirator, whatever it is made of.

| Type | Filters | Fit | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **N95** (NIOSH approved) | At least 95% of airborne particles | Tight seal | The standard choice. Not resistant to oil mist |
| **P100** (NIOSH approved) | At least 99.97% of particles | Tight seal | Strongly oil resistant. Disposable, or as filters on a reusable half mask |
| **Reusable half mask** with filters or cartridges | Particles, and specific gases or vapors only with the matching cartridge | Tight seal | You must know the hazard to pick the cartridge. Still does not supply oxygen |
| **KN95** and other international types | Particles | Meant to seal, but may not fit as well | Built to other countries' standards, which often do not require proof of quality |
| **Surgical or other disposable mask** | May block droplets | Loose | Helps protect others from you. Not a respirator |
| **Cloth mask** | May block droplets | Loose | May not stop small particles |

**Reading the label.** The letter is oil resistance: **N** is not resistant, **R** somewhat, **P** strongly. The number is filtering: **95** means at least 95%, **99** at least 99%, **100** at least 99.97%.

**A myth worth dropping:** that N95s only stop particles bigger than some cutoff size. NIOSH tests them against the particle size that gets through filters most easily. Larger and smaller particles are caught at least as well.

**What no N95 or P100 does:**

- Filter gases or vapors: carbon monoxide, natural gas, fuel fumes, chlorine, ammonia, the irritating gases in smoke.
- Supply oxygen. Air with less than 19.5% oxygen is classed as oxygen-deficient, and no filter fixes that.
- Work without a seal.

**Is it real?** A NIOSH-approved respirator is printed with the maker's name, "NIOSH", the filter class (such as N95), a model number, and an approval number in the form TC-84A-XXXX. If any of those is missing, it is not NIOSH approved. Masks with ear loops are not designed to seal tightly.

**Exhalation valves** make breathing easier but let your unfiltered breath out. If you are the sick one, wear a mask without a valve.

### Fit and the seal check

1. Wash your hands. Hold the respirator in your palm with the straps hanging down.
2. Place it over your nose and under your chin.
3. Pull the top strap high on the back of your head, above the ears. The bottom strap goes around your neck, below the ears.
4. Use two hands to mold the metal strip to your nose. Pinching with one hand leaves a gap.
5. **Seal check, every time:**
   - **Breathe out gently** with your hands over the mask. You should feel slight pressure build, with no air escaping at the edges.
   - **Breathe in sharply.** The mask should pull in slightly toward your face.
6. Air leaking at the nose or cheeks? Reshape the nose strip, adjust the straps, and try again. If it still leaks, you need a different size or model.

Things that defeat the seal:

- **Facial hair** where the mask touches skin. A good seal is not possible with a beard or stubble there.
- **The wrong size.** Faces differ; one model does not fit everyone.
- **Children's faces.** NIOSH-approved respirators do not come in sizes for young children. A child aged 2 or older can wear one that fits, but check the fit and watch them. Never put any mask on a child under 2.

In workplaces, tight-fitting respirators are fit-tested each year, and a seal check is not a substitute for that. Even fit-tested, a disposable respirator is rated to cut what you breathe in to about one-tenth, not to zero. Treat yours as reducing the dose, and shorten your time in bad air.

**Health cautions.** A respirator makes breathing harder. If you have heart or lung disease or are pregnant, ask a doctor before relying on one. In heat or hard work it raises the risk of heat illness, so take breaks and drink water.

**When to replace it:** when it gets harder to breathe through, dirty, wet, or damaged. Between uses, only the same person reuses it, and it is stored clean and dry, out of sunlight.

### The limits of improvised protection

- **Cloth, bandanas, T-shirts, wet towels:** they may catch droplets and coarse dust. They should not be relied on against fine smoke particles, and nothing made of cloth filters gas. Wetting the cloth does not change that.
- **Covering your nose and mouth with fabric while you leave** a dusty or contaminated area is a reasonable stopgap. It buys seconds for moving away, not permission to stay.
- **Rain gear, rubber boots, dish gloves, swim goggles, a trash bag with arm holes:** useful barriers against mud, floodwater, and splashes of body fluid. They are not chemical suits.
- **Surplus "gas masks":** unknown fit, old or mismatched filters, no oxygen supply. Do not plan around them.

Improvised gear never changes the walk-away list.

## Gloves

| Glove | Use for | Not for |
|---|---|---|
| Disposable nitrile | Body fluids, caring for the sick, wound care, diluted bleach | Sharp debris; reuse |
| Reusable rubber or other waterproof gloves | Floodwater, sewage, mold, scrubbing with disinfectant | Fine tasks |
| Leather or heavy work gloves | Debris, broken glass, nails, rope, tools | Liquids: they soak through |

- **Gloves carry germs as well as hands do.** Whatever a dirty glove touches is now dirty: your phone, your face, the door.
- **Change them** when torn or heavily soiled, and between a dirty job and a clean one.
- **Wash your hands after taking them off.** Gloves are not a substitute.
- Latex can trigger allergies. Nitrile avoids the problem.

**Taking gloves off:**

1. With one gloved hand, pinch the palm of the other glove and peel it off. Hold it in the gloved hand.
2. Slide a bare finger under the wrist of the remaining glove, without touching the outside.
3. Peel it off over the first glove so both end up inside out in a bundle.
4. Throw them away and wash your hands.

## Eyes

Eyes are both fragile and an open door for germs and chemicals.

- **Safety glasses** stop flying chips and dust from the front. Choose ones with side protection, and look for the Z87 marking that shows they meet the US safety standard. Everyday glasses and sunglasses are not safety eyewear.
- **Goggles** seal around the eyes. Use them for dusty work, mold, splashes, and bleach. Wear them over contact lenses or ordinary glasses in dust.
- **A face shield** protects the whole face from splashes, but its curve can funnel particles in from the side. Always wear glasses or goggles underneath.

## Head, Feet, Ears, and Clothing

- **Boots:** sturdy, closed, with thick soles for debris and nails. Waterproof boots for floodwater and bleach.
- **Hard hat** wherever something can fall on you: damaged buildings, tree work.
- **Hearing protection** for chainsaws, generators, and power tools. A simple test: if you have to raise your voice to be heard 3 feet (1 m) away, the noise is about 85 decibels, the NIOSH limit for an 8-hour day. Every 3 decibels above that halves the safe time.
- **Clothing:** long sleeves and long pants. Disposable coveralls keep dust and mold off your clothes; they are not chemical protection, and they trap heat.

## Putting It On and Taking It Off

**On, clean hands first:** gown or coveralls, then mask or respirator (seal check), then goggles or face shield, then gloves pulled over the cuffs.

**Off, the CDC's usual order.** The outside of everything is contaminated.

1. **Gloves:** peel off as described above.
2. **Goggles or face shield:** lift away by the strap or earpieces, from behind.
3. **Gown or coveralls:** pull away from your body and roll it inside out into a bundle.
4. **Mask or respirator, last:** after you have left the room. Lift the bottom strap, then the top, and never touch the front.
5. **Wash your hands** with soap and water right away.

If your hands get dirty at any step, clean them before the next one.

A way to remember it: **dirtiest first, face last, hands always.**

Bag disposable items. Wash reusable goggles with detergent, then disinfect them. After dusty or moldy work, take work clothes off outside and wash them separately. For chemical or radioactive contamination, the Decontamination Basics guide takes over.

## What to Wear for Common Jobs

| Job | Breathing | Eyes | Hands | Also |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Caring for a sick person | N95 or best-fitting mask | Goggles if they are coughing near you | Nitrile for body fluids | See the Infection Prevention guide |
| Cleaning up vomit, diarrhea, or sewage | Mask | Goggles | Nitrile or rubber | Soap-and-water handwash after |
| Mold cleanup | N95 at minimum | Goggles | Waterproof gloves | People with asthma or weak immune systems should not do this work |
| Debris and tree work | N95 if dusty | Safety glasses | Leather | Hard hat, boots, hearing protection |
| Flood mud | N95 if dusty or moldy | Goggles | Waterproof gloves | Rubber boots; cover any wounds |

## PPE Kit

- [ ] NIOSH-approved N95 respirators that fit each adult (10 or more)
- [ ] Surgical masks for sick household members
- [ ] Goggles that seal, and Z87 safety glasses
- [ ] Nitrile gloves, one box
- [ ] Reusable waterproof gloves, and leather work gloves for each adult
- [ ] Sturdy boots; rubber boots in flood areas
- [ ] Earplugs or earmuffs
- [ ] Hard hat, and disposable coveralls or old long-sleeved clothes
- [ ] Soap, hand sanitizer, and trash bags

## Common Mistakes

- **Trusting a mask against gas.** Carbon monoxide passes straight through an N95.
- **A beard under a respirator.**
- **Mask under the nose,** or pulled down to talk.
- **Ear-loop "N95s".** Head straps are what make the seal.
- **Gloves instead of handwashing.**
- **Taking the mask off first.** It comes off last.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Two parts. First, put on an N95 and do both halves of the seal check in front of a mirror; adjust until nothing leaks. Second, put on disposable gloves and rub a little washable paint, cocoa, or flour over them as stand-in contamination. Take them off using the four steps above, then look at your bare hands and wrists. Any smudge shows where real contamination would have landed. Repeat until you come out clean.

**What would you do?**

1. *Through the window of your neighbor's attached garage you see him slumped beside a running generator. You have a P100 half-mask respirator in your truck.*

   **Answer:** Call 911 and say you suspect carbon monoxide. The P100 is useless here: carbon monoxide is a gas, and no particle filter stops it or adds oxygen. Do not walk into the exhaust. Open the big garage door from outside if you can, so the space airs out, and wait for responders or until you can reach him from fresh air. People who rush into bad air can become the second casualty. See the Generator Safety guide.

2. *Wildfire smoke has settled on your town. You need to walk ten minutes to pick up medication and would bring your 4-year-old. You have adult N95s and a child-sized cloth mask.*

   **Answer:** The best protection for the child is not going. Leave them indoors with another adult, windows closed, in the room with the cleanest air. An adult N95 will not seal on a small face, and a cloth mask does not filter smoke. If there is truly no one to stay with them, keep the trip as short as possible and treat the masks as a small help at best. Wear your own N95 properly.

3. *You have just cleaned up after a family member who vomited. Still in gloves and mask, you feel your phone buzz in your pocket and your nose itch.*

   **Answer:** Touch nothing yet. Gloves come off first, peeled inside out, then wash your hands. Only then remove the mask by its straps, and wash your hands again with soap and water, because hand sanitizer works poorly against norovirus. Then answer the phone. A dirty glove on your face or phone carries the germs exactly where you do not want them.
