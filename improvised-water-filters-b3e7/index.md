# Improvised Water Filters

> **Clearer is not safer.** A filter made of cloth, sand, and charcoal takes out mud and grit, and nothing else you can count on. The germs that cause diarrhea are far too small to see, and they pass straight through. Filter to clear the water, then boil or disinfect it. Every time.

## Do this now

To turn murky water into drinking water with what you have:

1. **Choose the best source you can reach.** Never use floodwater, or water with a fuel or chemical smell or an oily sheen. No filter and no amount of boiling fixes chemicals.
2. **Let it settle.** Stand the container still until the sediment drops, then gently pour the clearer water off the top.
3. **Strain it** through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter.
4. **Still cloudy?** Run it through a layered sand filter (instructions below), and repeat until it comes out clear.
5. **Now make it safe. Boil it:** a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 5,000 ft (1,500 m). **Or use unscented household bleach:** per gallon, 8 drops of 6% or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach (2 drops per quart or liter). Double the dose if the water is still cloudy, colored, or very cold. Stir and wait 30 minutes.
6. **Store it** in a clean, covered container that never held the dirty water.

> **A homemade filter does not make water safe to drink.** No health agency rates cloth, sand, or campfire charcoal for removing germs. Treat everything that comes out of an improvised filter as untreated water until you have boiled or disinfected it.
>
> If someone becomes ill after drinking doubtful water, get medical help, or call 911 or your local emergency number, for bloody diarrhea, a fever over 102°F (38.9°C), vomiting that prevents keeping fluids down, diarrhea lasting more than 3 days, confusion, or signs of severe dehydration (little or no urine, dizziness when standing). Babies, older adults, pregnant women, and people with weakened immune systems need help sooner.

## Why filter at all?

If a homemade filter can't make water safe, why bother? Because the step that does make it safe works badly in dirty water.

- **Particles shield germs.** EPA warns that disinfection does not work as well when water is cloudy or colored. Bleach gets used up on the mud before it reaches the germs, and ultraviolet light, from a UV pen or from the sun, can't shine through it.
- **Clear water needs less bleach.** Cloudy water takes a double dose. If your bleach is limited, clearing the water first makes it go twice as far.
- **People drink more of it.** Water that looks and tastes better gets drunk, and dehydration is its own emergency.

Think of filtering as washing your hands before surgery. Necessary, and not the operation.

## What a homemade filter can and cannot do

| In the water | Homemade cloth, sand, and charcoal filter | What actually works |
|--------------|-------------------------------------------|---------------------|
| Mud, silt, leaves, insects | Removes most of it. This is the filter's real job. | Settling, straining, sand filter |
| Parasites (Giardia, Cryptosporidium) | **Not reliable.** CDC says a filter needs an absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller. The gaps between sand grains are many times wider. | Boiling. A rated commercial filter. |
| Bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella, cholera) | **Not reliable.** A filter needs an absolute pore size of 0.3 micron or smaller. | Boiling, bleach, chlorine dioxide, iodine |
| Viruses (norovirus, hepatitis A) | **No.** Viruses pass through even most commercial portable filters. | Boiling, bleach, chlorine dioxide, iodine |
| Fuel, pesticides, industrial chemicals, heavy metals | **No.** Campfire charcoal is not the activated carbon in commercial filters. | Nothing you can do at home. Find another source. |
| Salt | **No.** Salt is dissolved and passes through any filter bed. | Distillation. See the Emergency Desalination Methods guide. |
| Bad taste, smell, color | May improve somewhat, mostly from the charcoal layer. | Pouring boiled water between two clean containers also helps. |

## The fastest filters: settle and strain

These take no building, and they are the methods CDC, EPA, and FEMA actually name. Do them first.

### Settling

Fill a bucket or bottle and leave it completely still. Heavy particles sink. Pour or ladle the clear water off the top without stirring up the bottom, and leave the last inch behind.

### Cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter

Stretch a clean, tightly woven cloth over the mouth of a clean container, or line a funnel or colander with a coffee filter or paper towel, and pour slowly. Fold cloth into several layers. A T-shirt, bandana, or pillowcase all work. Wash the cloth and dry it in the sun between uses.

### Both together

Settle first, then strain the water you pour off. For most muddy water this gets you clear enough to disinfect, and you can skip building anything.

## Build a layered sand filter

When settling and straining still leave the water cloudy, a layered filter does a better job. Each layer is finer than the one below it, so grit is trapped in stages and the fine sand does not wash out through the bottom.

```
        CLOUDY WATER IN
              |
              v
+-----------------------------+
| CLOTH                       |
|  spreads the pour,          |
|  keeps the sand still       |
+-----------------------------+
| FINE SAND   2-3 in (5-8 cm) |
|  traps the fine particles   |
+-----------------------------+
| CHARCOAL    2-3 in (5-8 cm) |
|  may improve taste, smell   |
+-----------------------------+
| GRAVEL      1-2 in (3-5 cm) |
|  holds the charcoal         |
+-----------------------------+
| SMALL ROCKS 2-3 in (5-8 cm) |
|  lets water drain freely    |
+-----------------------------+
| CLOTH PLUG at the outlet    |
+-------------+---------------+
              |
              v
     CLEARER WATER OUT
       NOT YET SAFE
```

### Gather

- [ ] A container: a 2-liter plastic bottle with the bottom cut off, or a food-safe bucket with a small hole near the bottom. Nothing that ever held chemicals, fuel, or paint.
- [ ] Two pieces of clean cloth, or coffee filters
- [ ] Small rocks or pebbles
- [ ] Pea-sized gravel
- [ ] Charcoal from a wood fire, fully cold: black chunks, not gray ash. Never barbecue briquettes, which contain additives.
- [ ] The finest, cleanest sand you can find. Avoid sand from roadsides, industrial sites, or anywhere fuel or chemicals may have spilled.
- [ ] A clean container to catch the output, and a separate one for dirty water

### Build

1. **Rinse the rocks, gravel, and sand** until the rinse water runs mostly clear. Dirty sand makes dirty water.
2. **Prepare the container.** Turn the cut bottle upside down so the neck is the outlet. Push a wad of cloth or a coffee filter into the neck so nothing but water can get out.
3. **Add small rocks,** 2 to 3 inches.
4. **Add gravel,** 1 to 2 inches.
5. **Add crushed charcoal,** 2 to 3 inches. Break it into pieces about the size of gravel, and rinse off the black dust. If you have no charcoal, leave this layer out. The filter clears water just as well without it.
6. **Add fine sand,** 2 to 3 inches, and level it.
7. **Lay cloth on top** so pouring does not dig a hole in the sand.
8. **Flush it.** Pour water through several times and throw away what comes out. The first runs are gray with charcoal dust and fine silt. Start collecting when the output runs clear.

### Use

- **Pour slowly and let it drip.** Fast water cuts channels through the sand and carries dirt with it. Don't squeeze the bottle.
- **Still cloudy? Run it through again.**
- **Keep dirty and clean apart.** Use one container for raw water and another for filtered water, and don't let the dirty one touch the outlet or the clean one.
- **Disinfect what comes out.** Boil it or add bleach, as in the steps at the top.
- **Rebuild it when it slows to a trickle,** stops improving the water, or starts to smell. A wet filter left standing can grow bacteria of its own. Tip everything out, wash or replace the sand and cloth, and use fresh charcoal.

## Myths that make people sick

**"It came out clear, so it's clean."** A glass of water can hold enough germs to make you ill and still look perfect. Clarity tells you the mud is gone. It tells you nothing about germs.

**"Charcoal takes out the poisons."** Charcoal from a campfire is not the activated carbon used in commercial filters, and it won't make chemically contaminated water safe. Even real activated carbon filters are sold to improve taste and odor. They are not rated to remove germs.

**"Sand filters are used all over the world, so mine works too."** The household slow sand filters used in aid programs are engineered devices about 3 ft (0.9 m) tall, and they depend on a living layer that has to grow and mature on the constantly wet sand. Even then, CDC reports that lab tests of filters with a mature layer removed 90 to 99 percent of bacteria, with variable results for viruses, and it lists "not as effective against viruses" as a drawback. Removing 99 percent still leaves 1 germ in every 100. A bottle filter you built this afternoon has no such layer at all.

**"I'll just run it through twice."** A second pass gives clearer water. It does not give safe water. Only heat or a disinfectant does that.

**"Running water is clean, so I only need to filter it."** Streams carry germs from animals and from people upstream. Treat every outdoor source.

## Better tools to keep on hand

An improvised filter is what you build when you have nothing else. A little preparation beats it easily:

- **A commercial portable filter** with an absolute pore size of 0.3 micron or smaller removes parasites and bacteria. It still does not remove viruses, so add a disinfectant afterward.
- **Unscented household bleach** with a dropper, replaced every year.
- **Chlorine dioxide tablets** for a go-bag.
- **A pot and a way to boil.** Boiling is the most reliable method of all.
- **Coffee filters.** A pack costs little, weighs nothing, and pre-filters a lot of water.

See the Complete Water Purification Guide for every method and dose, the Finding Safe Water Sources guide for where to look, and the Signs of Contaminated Water guide for water to refuse.

## People who need extra care

- **Babies:** use ready-to-feed formula if you have it. If you must mix formula, use bottled water, or water that has been boiled and cooled. Don't rely on filtered and bleached water for a baby if you can boil.
- **People with weakened immune systems:** Cryptosporidium can be severe for them, and it survives bleach. Boil the water or use bottled water.
- **Pregnancy or thyroid problems:** avoid iodine as a disinfectant.
- **Pets:** give them the same treated water you drink.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Build a bottle filter at the kitchen table. Cut the bottom off a plastic drink bottle, plug the neck with a coffee filter or scrap of cloth, and layer in whatever you have: aquarium gravel, play sand, a paper towel on top. Stir a spoonful of garden soil into a jug of tap water and pour half of it through. Set a glass of the filtered water next to a glass of the muddy water and compare. Then say the next step out loud before anyone forgets it: *boil it or bleach it*. Don't drink the test water. Pour it on a plant. This is a good one to do with children.

**What would you do?**

1. *Four days after a hurricane, you are filtering creek water through a bucket of sand and charcoal. It comes out clear and tastes fine. You are out of stove fuel. You have bleach, but the family hates the taste.*

   **Answer:** Use the bleach. Clear water from a homemade filter is still untreated water. Add 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon, stir, and wait 30 minutes. A slight chlorine smell means it worked. To soften the taste, pour the water back and forth between two clean containers and let it stand a few hours. A bad taste is a small price next to a week of diarrhea with no clean water to recover on.

2. *The street is flooded with brown water that has a rainbow sheen on it. A neighbor has built an impressive filter from a trash can, with a thick charcoal layer, and offers to share. "Charcoal pulls out the chemicals," he says.*

   **Answer:** Don't drink it, and tell him why. A sheen means fuel or oil, and floodwater also carries sewage. Campfire charcoal does not remove fuel or chemicals, and boiling or bleach won't either. This water is for nothing but flushing a toilet. Look for other sources: bottled water, the water heater tank, rain caught in clean containers, or an official distribution point.

3. *You have one gallon of muddy pond water and a small bottle of bleach that has to last. You could filter the water first, or skip the work and just double the bleach dose, as the instructions allow for cloudy water.*

   **Answer:** Filter first. Let it settle, strain it through cloth, and run it through sand if you need to. Mud shields germs and uses up chlorine, so even a double dose is less certain in cloudy water than a normal dose in clear water. Clearing it also means each gallon takes half as much of your bleach. The double dose is the fallback for when you can't get the water clear, not a shortcut.

## Quick Reference

```
IMPROVISED FILTER = CLEARER,
NOT SAFE. ALWAYS DISINFECT AFTER.

1. SETTLE, pour off the top
2. STRAIN: cloth, paper towel,
   or coffee filter
3. SAND FILTER if still cloudy

LAYERS (top to bottom)
  Cloth
  Fine sand      2-3 in
  Charcoal       2-3 in (optional,
                 wood fire only)
  Gravel         1-2 in
  Small rocks    2-3 in
  Cloth plug at outlet
Rinse materials. Pour slowly.
Throw away the first runs.

4. THEN MAKE IT SAFE
  BOIL 1 min rolling boil
   (3 min above 5,000 ft)
  or BLEACH per gallon:
   8 drops 6% / 6 drops 8.25%
   Double if cloudy or cold.
   Wait 30 min.

FILTERS DO NOT REMOVE
  Viruses, chemicals, fuel, salt.
  Sheen or fuel smell = don't use.
```
