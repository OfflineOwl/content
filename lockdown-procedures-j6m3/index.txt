LOCKDOWN PROCEDURES
===================

Locks, lights, out of sight. A lockdown puts a locked door between
you and a violent person and keeps you silent and unseen until
police reach you. Getting away is better whenever a safe way out
exists. Lockdown is what you do when it doesn't.


DO THIS NOW
-----------

If you hear gunfire, or an attacker is in or right outside the
building:

1. RUN if there is a safe way out. Leave your belongings. Go even
   if others won't. Keep your hands empty and visible.
2. CAN'T GET OUT? LOCK DOWN.
   - Get into the nearest room with a door that locks.
   - LOCKS: lock the door, then block it with heavy furniture.
   - LIGHTS: off. Close blinds and cover door windows.
   - OUT OF SIGHT: away from the door and windows, low, behind
     something solid.
3. GO SILENT. Phone on silent with vibration off (not powered
   down). No talking. Turn off radios, TVs, and screens.
4. CALL OR TEXT 911 when you can do it without being heard.
   Say WHERE you are first.
5. DO NOT OPEN THE DOOR for a voice. Wait for police to unlock it
   or for 911 to confirm who is outside.
6. FIGHT only as a last resort, when your life is in immediate
   danger and you can neither run nor hide.


GET EMERGENCY HELP
------------------

Call 911 (112 in the EU, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia, or your
local emergency number) as soon as it is safe. Don't assume
someone else has called.

TELL THE DISPATCHER:
- Where the attacker is (building, floor, room, direction of
  travel)
- How many attackers, if more than one
- What they look like
- What weapons you saw or heard, and how many
- How many people are with you, and whether anyone is hurt

IF YOU CAN'T SPEAK SAFELY:
- Call anyway and leave the line open so the dispatcher can
  listen.
- Or text 911. The rule is "call if you can, text if you can't."
  Put your location and the emergency in the first message. Use
  plain words, no abbreviations, emojis, or photos.
- Text-to-911 works in many US areas, not all. If it isn't
  available where you are, you should get an automatic reply
  telling you to call. A text also gives a less exact location
  than a voice call, so type your address or room.


WHICH WORD MEANS WHAT
---------------------

Schools and workplaces use different words for different threats.
Mixing them up costs time. Many US schools use the Standard
Response Protocol, which keeps five actions apart:

LOCKDOWN - "Locks, lights, out of sight."
  The threat is INSIDE or very close to the building. Lock the
  room, lights off, stay silent and hidden, don't open the door.

SECURE - "Get inside. Lock outside doors." (older name: lockout)
  The threat is OUTSIDE the building, such as police activity
  nearby. Everyone comes in, outside doors are locked, and work
  or class carries on as normal inside.

HOLD - "In your room or area."
  Hallways need to stay clear, for example for a medical
  emergency. Stay where you are and carry on.

EVACUATE - leave for a named location.

SHELTER - for a hazard such as a tornado or a chemical release.
  This is NOT a lockdown. See the Shelter in Place guide.

Your building may use other words. Find out what it calls each of
these, and what the signal for each sounds like, before you need
to know.

WHEN POLICE TELL A NEIGHBORHOOD TO "STAY INSIDE" OR "SHELTER IN
PLACE" during a search, that is a SECURE at home: get in, lock the
outside doors and windows, stay away from the windows, follow
official alerts, and don't go out to look.


WHY IT WORKS
------------

- These attacks are short. The US Department of Homeland Security
  notes they are often over within 10 to 15 minutes, before
  police arrive. What you do in the first minute matters
  more than anything that follows.
- A room that is locked, dark, and silent looks unoccupied. That
  is the whole aim of "locks, lights, out of sight."
- A locked and blocked door costs an attacker time, and time is
  what police need.
- Distance is the best protection of all. That is why RUN comes
  first.


RUN
---

- Know two ways out of any building you spend time in.
- Move as soon as you recognize danger. Don't wait to be sure the
  noise was gunfire, and don't stop to collect belongings.
- Take others with you if they'll come. Don't stay because they
  won't.
- Warn people away from walking into the area.
- Keep your hands empty and visible. Follow police instructions.
- Don't stop to move the wounded while the attacker is close.
- Call 911 once you are safe.

IF YOU'RE IN A HALLWAY OR OPEN AREA when a lockdown is called:
get into the nearest room that can be secured. If you can't reach
one, get out of sight, or leave the building and move well away
from it.


HIDE: HOW TO LOCK DOWN A ROOM
-----------------------------

A GOOD ROOM:
- Is out of the attacker's view
- Has a door that locks and solid walls
- Doesn't trap you if you need to move again

SECURE IT:
- Lock the door. Know how in advance: some doors lock only from
  the outside or with a key.
- Block it with the heaviest furniture in the room: desks,
  cabinets, tables.
- If the door has no lock, blocking it is all you have, so do
  that first.
- Cover or stay out of view of any window in or beside the door.
- Lights off. Blinds closed.

THEN:
- Stay low and out of sight of the door, behind solid objects
  such as desks and cabinets.
- Silence every phone, including vibration and alarms. Dim the
  screens.
- Don't answer knocks or voices. If a fire alarm sounds, look for
  real signs of fire, such as smoke, before you decide to move.
  Your building's plan should say what to do.
- Stay ready to move. Hiding is not giving up. If the situation
  changes and a safe way out opens, take it.


FIGHT: THE LAST RESORT
----------------------

Official US guidance (Ready.gov and DHS) includes fighting back
only when you cannot run or hide and your life is in immediate
danger. If it comes to that, the guidance is to commit fully and
act together with the others in the room. This guide does not go
further than that. Everything above is designed so you never
reach this point.


WHEN POLICE ARRIVE
------------------

The first officers are there to stop the attacker. They will go
past injured people, and they do not yet know who the attacker
is.

- Stay calm and do exactly what they say.
- Put down anything in your hands: phones, bags, jackets.
- Raise your hands, spread your fingers, and keep your hands
  visible.
- Don't run toward officers, grab them, point, or shout.
- Don't stop to ask them questions on your way out. Leave in the
  direction they came from.
- Medical teams follow behind the first officers.
- Expect to be kept at an assembly point until police have
  identified and spoken to witnesses. Don't leave until they say
  you may.


HOW A LOCKDOWN ENDS
-------------------

- It ends when police or a known official UNLOCKS your door from
  the outside. In schools using the Standard Response Protocol,
  staff and students are trained not to open the door at all.
- A voice saying "police, it's safe, open up" is not proof. Call
  or text 911 and ask the dispatcher whether officers are at your
  door.
- Quiet is not proof either. Clearing a building room by room can
  take a long time after the danger is over.
- Don't leave because you are tired, hungry, or need a bathroom.
  Use a bin or a corner if you must.


HELP THE WOUNDED
----------------

Look after your own safety first. Then:

- Move the injured to a safer spot if you can do it without
  exposing yourself.
- Heavy bleeding can kill within about five minutes. Press hard
  and directly on the wound with cloth or your hands, and don't
  let go. Use a tourniquet on an arm or leg if you have one and
  know how. See the Bleeding Control guide.
- Tell 911 how many are hurt and how badly.


AT WORK AND IN PUBLIC PLACES
----------------------------

- Learn the plan: the alarm or announcement, the exits, the rooms
  that lock, the assembly point.
- In a shop, cinema, or place of worship, note two exits when you
  arrive.
- If you are staff, customers and visitors will copy you. Lead
  them out or into a room.
- If you supervise others, account for them once you are safe and
  report who is missing.


SCHOOLS AND PARENTS
-------------------

WHAT STUDENTS ARE TAUGHT in a lockdown:
- Move away from sight
- Maintain silence
- Do not open the door

WHAT TEACHERS DO: bring in students from the hallway if it is
safe, lock the door, turn out the lights, move everyone out of
sight, keep silence, and leave the door closed.

IF YOU ARE A PARENT AND THE SCHOOL LOCKS DOWN:
- Don't go to the school. You are unlikely to be let onto the
  campus, and cars and crowds can get in the way of police and
  ambulances.
- Don't call your child. A ringing or buzzing phone can give away
  a hidden room. Early in a lockdown it may not be safe for
  students to text at all.
- Watch for messages from the school or district. Keep your
  contact details with the school up to date.
- If students are moved off site, you will be told where to
  collect them. Bring photo ID and expect a wait.

DRILLS should always be announced as drills and should suit the
children's ages. A drill practices the actions. It does not need
to simulate an attack. If a drill frightened your child, tell the
school.

TALKING TO CHILDREN: keep it short and calm. "If the teacher says
lockdown, we get quiet, we hide, and we wait for the police to
open the door. The grown-ups have a plan." Then answer the
questions they ask, at their level.


AT HOME
-------

THREAT OUTSIDE (violence nearby, police searching the area):
- Bring everyone in. Lock doors, windows, and the garage.
- Stay away from windows. Close blinds.
- Follow official alerts and local radio.
- Don't go out to look, and don't open the door to anyone you
  can't identify.

SOMEONE BREAKING IN OR INSIDE:
- Get out if you can do it safely and call from a neighbor's
  home.
- If you can't, get everyone into one room, lock and block the
  door, lights off, and call 911 with your address first.
- Don't go to investigate. No property is worth a life.

See the Safe Room Setup guide for choosing and preparing that
room, and the Home Security During Civil Unrest guide for
disturbances in your street. The Family Emergency Action Plan
guide covers meeting places and how you'll contact each other.

Laws on self-defense differ between states and countries. Learn
the ones that apply to you. This guide does not give legal
advice.


PEOPLE WHO NEED EXTRA THOUGHT
-----------------------------

YOUNG CHILDREN:
- Stay close, speak in a whisper, and give them one job: "stay
  quiet like a mouse."
- A quiet toy, a snack, or a screen with the sound off helps.

PEOPLE WITH LIMITED MOBILITY:
- Agree in advance who helps whom, and which lockable room is
  nearest to where they usually are.
- If you can't get out, hiding early is better than moving late.

DEAF OR HARD OF HEARING:
- Make sure the building's alert reaches you by light, vibration,
  or a buddy.
- Text-to-911 can be your first choice. Tell the dispatcher you
  are deaf or hard of hearing.

MEDICAL NEEDS:
- Keep urgent medication (inhaler, glucose, epinephrine) on you,
  not in a locker or another room.

PETS AT HOME:
- Bring them into the room if it costs no time. Don't go looking.


AFTERWARD
---------

- Check in with family by text.
- Get checked for injuries. Adrenaline hides them.
- Tell police what you saw when they ask.
- Strong reactions are normal for days or weeks: poor sleep,
  jumpiness, anger, sadness, trouble concentrating, fear of
  crowds or of being alone. Children may become clingy or slip
  back into younger behavior.
- Keep routines, limit news and video of the event, and talk
  about it when you or they are ready.
- Get help if the reactions don't ease or get in the way of daily
  life. In the US, call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline)
  at any hour, or the Disaster Distress Helpline at
  1-800-985-5990 (call or text). Other countries have their own
  crisis lines.

PRACTICE
--------

10-MINUTE DRILL:
At work or school, sit where you usually sit and answer three
questions without getting up: Where are my two nearest exits?
Which is the nearest room that locks? What would I push against
its door? Then get up and check. Try the lock. See which way the
door opens. Finally, time yourself putting your phone on silent
with vibration off. Do the same at home with your household.

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

1. You are at your desk when you hear several sharp bangs from
   the lobby. Colleagues gather at the window, arguing about
   whether it was fireworks. The stairwell exit is twenty steps
   away, in the opposite direction from the noise.

   Answer: Go, now, down the stairs. Waiting to be sure is the
   common mistake, and these events are measured in minutes. Tell
   the others "I'm leaving, come with me" and keep moving if they
   don't. Leave your bag. Call 911 once you are well clear, and
   keep your hands visible when you meet police.

2. You have been locked in a storeroom with four other people for
   25 minutes. It has gone quiet. A voice outside says, "Police.
   It's all clear. Open the door."

   Answer: Don't open it on a voice. Stay silent and out of sight,
   and call or text 911 to ask whether officers are at your door.
   Real police can wait while the dispatcher confirms it. When the
   door does open, show empty hands with fingers spread and do
   what you are told.

3. A text arrives from your child's school: "We are in LOCKDOWN.
   Police are on scene." You are five minutes away by car.

   Answer: Stay where you are, and don't call your child's phone.
   You are unlikely to be let near the building, your car could be
   in the way of responders, and a ringing phone could give away a
   hidden classroom. Watch for the school's next message. If
   students are moved, go to the reunification site they name and
   bring photo ID.


QUICK REFERENCE
---------------

ATTACKER IN OR NEAR THE BUILDING:
RUN   - Safe way out? Go now. Leave things. Hands visible.
HIDE  - LOCKS: lock and block the door
        LIGHTS: off, blinds closed
        OUT OF SIGHT: low, away from door and windows
        SILENT: phone muted, vibration off
FIGHT - Last resort only, life in immediate danger.

911: Location first. Attackers, description, weapons, injured.
Can't talk: leave the line open, or text 911 where available.

DON'T OPEN THE DOOR for a voice. Police unlock it, or 911
confirms.

POLICE ARRIVE: Drop everything. Hands up, fingers spread.
Don't run at them. Do what they say.

WORDS:
LOCKDOWN = threat inside. Locks, lights, out of sight.
SECURE   = threat outside. Get inside, lock outside doors.
SHELTER  = weather or chemical. Different rules.

PARENTS: Don't go to the school. Don't call your child.
Wait for the school's message. Bring ID to reunification.

BLEEDING: Press hard, don't let go.

US CRISIS LINES: 988, or 1-800-985-5990 (call or text)
