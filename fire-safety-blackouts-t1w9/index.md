# Fire Safety During Blackouts

> **Use flashlights, not candles.** Power outages bring out candles, space heaters, generators, and camp stoves, and fire risk rises with them. A home fire can become deadly in just a few minutes.

## Do this now

**If there's a fire:**

1. **Get out, and shout "Fire!"** to alert everyone. Don't stop for belongings.
2. **Stay low under smoke** and close doors behind you as you go.
3. **Call 911** (or your local emergency number) **from outside.**
4. **Never go back inside** a burning building.

**At the start of a power outage:**

- **Use flashlights and battery lanterns instead of candles.**
- **Check that smoke alarms and CO alarms work** (press the test button).
- **Run generators outdoors only**, at least 20 feet from the house. Never use grills or camp stoves indoors, and never use an oven or stove to heat your home.
- **Turn off stoves, ovens, and space heaters** that were on when the power went out, so they don't come back on unattended.

## Why Fire Risk Goes Up

During outages, people use:

- Candles and oil lamps for light
- Space heaters and fireplaces, sometimes ones that haven't been used or checked in years
- Generators and stored fuel
- Camp stoves and grills for cooking

At the same time, smoke alarms may have dead batteries or no backup power, water pressure may be low, and roads and phone lines may be jammed. In US home candle fires that killed someone, the power was out in about a quarter of the cases where the reason for using candles was known.

## Candles: Use Battery Lights Instead

Fire safety agencies say not to use candles for emergency lighting. Flashlights, headlamps, and battery or rechargeable lanterns are far safer. Keep enough for every room and every person.

**If you use candles anyway:**

- **Keep them at least 12 inches from anything that can burn**: curtains, bedding, paper, furniture, decorations. About 3 in 5 home candle fires start because something that can burn was too close.
- **Don't use candles in bedrooms, bathrooms, or other sleeping areas.**
- Use sturdy holders that won't tip over, on a stable, uncluttered surface.
- **Never leave a burning candle unattended.** Blow candles out when you leave the room or go to sleep.
- Keep candles away from children and pets, and keep matches and lighters out of reach.
- Don't carry a lit candle from room to room. Use a flashlight to move around.
- Don't let candles burn all the way down. Put them out before they reach the holder.
- Put candles out by blowing them out or with a snuffer, not with water.
- **Never use a candle or any open flame where medical oxygen is in use.**

Battery-powered "flameless" candles are a safe substitute.

## Smoke Alarms

The death rate in US home fires is about 60 percent lower in homes with working smoke alarms. Nearly 3 in 5 home fire deaths happen where there was no smoke alarm or it didn't work.

### Where to Install

- Inside every room where someone sleeps
- Outside each sleeping area (in the hallway)
- On every level of the home, including the basement

Interconnected alarms, where one sounds them all, are best. Ionization alarms respond faster to flaming fires and photoelectric alarms to smoldering fires, so use both types or dual-sensor alarms. For people who are deaf or hard of hearing, use alarms with strobe lights, bed or pillow shakers, or low-frequency sounders.

### Keep Them Working

- **Test alarms monthly** with the test button.
- Replace standard batteries at least once a year. Alarms with sealed 10-year batteries don't need battery changes.
- **Replace any smoke alarm that's 10 years old** (check the date on the back).
- If an alarm chirps, replace the battery right away.
- **Never take batteries out of alarms** to power something else during an outage.
- Hardwired alarms should have battery backup. Test them at the start of an outage.

### Carbon Monoxide Alarms

Outages increase CO risk from generators and fuel-burning heaters. Have battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms outside each sleeping area and on every level, and test them monthly. If one sounds, get everyone outside and call 911. See the Generator Safety and Emergency Heating guides.

## Heating Safety

- **Never heat your home with an oven or stove, a grill, a camp stove, or an outdoor heater.** These cause fires, and fuel-burning ones make deadly carbon monoxide.
- **Space heaters:** keep at least **3 feet** of clear space around them. Plug electric heaters directly into a wall outlet, never into an extension cord or power strip. Turn them off when you leave the room or go to sleep. Space heaters are involved in most home heating fire deaths.
- **Fireplaces and wood stoves:** have the chimney inspected yearly, use a screen, burn only dry wood, and don't leave a fire unattended. Put cooled ashes in a metal container with a tight lid, outside, at least 10 feet from the house.
- See the Emergency Heating guide for more.

## Cooking Safety

- **Cook outdoors** on grills and camp stoves, away from the house, overhangs, and anything that burns. **Never bring them indoors**, including into a garage or tent.
- Stay with food while it's cooking.
- Keep a lid nearby. **For a grease fire, slide a lid over the pan and turn off the heat. Leave the pan covered and don't move it. Never throw water on a grease fire.**
- Keep children and pets at least 3 feet away from grills and cooking areas.

## Generators and Fuel

- **Generators outdoors only**, at least 20 feet from the house, with exhaust pointed away from windows, doors, and vents.
- **Turn the generator off and let it cool before refueling.** Spilled fuel on a hot engine can ignite.
- Never store gasoline in your home. Keep it outside living areas, such as in a detached shed, in approved containers, away from anything that makes heat or sparks.
- Use heavy-duty outdoor cords rated above the load, and never plug a generator into a wall outlet.
- See the Generator Safety guide.

## When the Power Comes Back

- Turn off or unplug appliances and electronics during the outage. Power can come back with surges or spikes. When it returns, turn things back on gradually.
- Check that stoves, ovens, irons, and heaters are off.
- If you smell burning or see sparks or smoke from outlets or appliances, turn off power at the main breaker if it's safe to reach, get out, and call 911.

## Escape Planning

**You may have as little as two minutes to get out** once a smoke alarm sounds. A plan you've practiced is what gets everyone out in time, especially in the dark.

### Make Your Plan

- **Know two ways out of every room**, usually a door and a window. Make sure windows open and screens come off easily.
- **Pick an outside meeting place** in front of your home, a safe distance away, such as a mailbox, tree, or light pole, where firefighters will see you.
- **Plan for everyone:** decide who will help babies, young children, older adults, and anyone with a disability or who uses a mobility device.
- Keep a flashlight and shoes by each bed during outages.
- **Practice the plan at least twice a year**, once during the day and once at night.

### When There's a Fire

1. Shout "Fire!" and get out fast.
2. **Get low and go under the smoke.** Smoke rises, and the cleaner air is near the floor.
3. **Before opening a door, feel the door and handle with the back of your hand.** If it's hot, use your second way out. If it's cool, open it slowly and be ready to shut it if smoke or fire is on the other side.
4. **Close doors behind you.** A closed door slows the spread of fire and smoke.
5. Go to your meeting place and count heads.
6. **Call 911 from outside.**
7. **Never go back inside.** Tell firefighters if anyone is missing.

### If You're Trapped

1. Close the door between you and the fire.
2. Stuff towels or clothing into the cracks around the door and cover vents.
3. Call 911 and tell them exactly where you are, even if firefighters are already outside.
4. Open a window and signal with a flashlight or a light-colored cloth.
5. Stay low, near the window, until help arrives.

### If Your Clothes Catch Fire

**Stop, drop, and roll:** stop where you are, drop to the ground, cover your face with your hands, and roll over and over until the flames are out. Then cool the burn with cool water and get medical help.

## Fire Extinguishers

### When to Use One

Use an extinguisher **only if all of these are true:**

- The fire is small and not spreading, such as in a wastebasket
- Everyone else is getting out, and someone is calling 911 from outside
- The room isn't filling with smoke
- You have a clear way out behind you
- You're an adult who knows how to use the extinguisher

**If you have any doubt, get out.** If one extinguisher doesn't put the fire out, leave immediately.

### How to Use It: PASS

- **P**ull the pin.
- **A**im low, at the base of the fire.
- **S**queeze the handle slowly and evenly.
- **S**weep from side to side.

Keep your back to an exit. If the fire doesn't go out quickly, leave.

### Which Type

A multipurpose "ABC" extinguisher (rated at least 2-A:10-B:C) works on ordinary materials (wood, paper, cloth), flammable liquids, and electrical fires. It's a good choice for most homes. Keep one in the kitchen and one on each level. For a grease fire in a pan, a lid is the first choice; only Class K extinguishers are rated for cooking-oil fires.

## Checklists

### Before an Outage

- [ ] Flashlights or lanterns for every person, with spare batteries
- [ ] Smoke and CO alarms tested, with fresh batteries
- [ ] Fire extinguisher charged (gauge in the green) and easy to reach
- [ ] Escape plan practiced, including in the dark
- [ ] Chimney and heating equipment inspected
- [ ] Generator fuel stored safely, away from the house

### Every Day During an Outage

- [ ] Use battery lights, not candles
- [ ] Keep 3 feet of clear space around heaters and fireplaces
- [ ] Put out every flame before leaving a room or going to sleep
- [ ] Generators outdoors only, far from the house
- [ ] Keep exits and hallways clear
