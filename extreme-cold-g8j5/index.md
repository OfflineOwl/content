# Extreme Cold Survival

> **Cold can kill.** Hypothermia (dangerously low body temperature) and frostbite can develop quickly in wind, wet clothes, or cold water, and even indoors in an unheated home. Stay dry, get out of the wind, and know the warning signs.

## Do This Now

If someone may have HYPOTHERMIA (shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness; in babies, bright red cold skin and very low energy):

1. Take their temperature if you can. Below 95°F (35°C) is a medical emergency. Call 911 (or your local emergency number).
2. Get them into a warm room or shelter. Handle them gently.
3. Remove any wet clothing.
4. Warm the CENTER of the body first: chest, neck, head, and groin. Use an electric blanket if you have one, or skin-to-skin contact under loose, dry layers of blankets, clothing, towels, or sheets.
5. Give warm, sweet, non-alcoholic drinks ONLY if they are fully awake and able to swallow. Never give drinks to an unconscious person.
6. Don't put a confused or drowsy person in a warm shower or bath.
7. No breathing or pulse? Start CPR and keep going while they are warmed, until they respond or help takes over.

If someone may have FROSTBITE (white or grayish-yellow skin, skin that feels unusually firm or waxy, numbness):

1. Check for hypothermia first. It is more serious and needs emergency care.
2. Get to a warm room. Seek medical care.
3. Do not walk on frostbitten feet or toes unless you must.
4. Remove rings and other tight jewelry.
5. Rewarm in warm (NOT hot) water, or with body heat (for example, fingers in an armpit). Don't thaw it if it could freeze again.
6. Do NOT rub or massage the area, and do NOT use snow.
7. Do NOT use a heating pad, heat lamp, hot water bottle, hair dryer, stove, fireplace, or radiator. Numb skin burns easily.

## Understanding Cold Danger

HOW YOU LOSE HEAT:

- Wind strips heat from exposed skin (wind chill).
- Wet clothing and wet skin lose heat much faster than dry.
- Cold water pulls heat away far faster than cold air.
- Sitting or lying on cold ground or snow drains heat.

WIND CHILL: Wind chill is how cold it feels on exposed skin when wind is added to the air temperature. The colder the wind chill, the faster frostbite can happen.

Example (US National Weather Service wind chill chart):

- Air temperature 0°F (-18°C) with a 15 mph wind = wind chill of -19°F. At that wind chill, exposed skin can get frostbite in about 30 minutes.
- At colder wind chills, frostbite can happen in 10 minutes or less.

Frostbite only happens when the air temperature is below freezing. Be extra careful with children, who lose heat faster.

Follow cold alerts from your national weather service. In the US, the National Weather Service issues Cold Weather Advisories and Extreme Cold Warnings.

WHO IS MOST AT RISK:

- Older adults, especially those living alone or without enough heat
- Babies sleeping in cold rooms
- People who are outdoors for long periods: outdoor workers, hikers, hunters, people experiencing homelessness
- People who drink alcohol or use drugs
- People with diabetes, heart disease, poor circulation, or conditions that affect thinking or movement
- Anyone who is wet, tired, hungry, or exposed to wind

ALCOHOL DOES NOT WARM YOU. Alcohol and caffeine make your body lose heat faster. Avoid them in the cold, and drink warm, sweet drinks or broth instead.

## Recognizing Hypothermia

Hypothermia happens when your body loses heat faster than it can make it, and body temperature falls below 95°F (35°C). It affects the brain, so the person may not realize what is happening.

WARNING SIGNS IN ADULTS:

- Shivering
- Exhaustion or feeling very tired
- Confusion
- Fumbling hands
- Memory loss
- Slurred speech
- Drowsiness

WARNING SIGNS IN BABIES:

- Bright red, cold skin
- Very low energy

AS IT GETS WORSE:

- Shivering may STOP. A cold person who stops shivering is getting worse, not better.
- Breathing and pulse slow down and may be hard to detect.
- The person may become unconscious and appear dead. Keep helping: people with severe hypothermia who seem dead have been revived.

Hypothermia can happen even at temperatures above 40°F (4°C) if a person is wet from rain, sweat, or cold water.

## Hypothermia First Aid

1. CALL FOR HELP. If the temperature is below 95°F (35°C), or you can't measure it but the person has warning signs, get medical help immediately.

2. GET THEM OUT OF THE COLD. Move them into a warm room, shelter, or vehicle. Handle them gently; rough handling can be dangerous in severe hypothermia. If you can't move them indoors:
  - Get them out of the wind and insulate them from the ground.
  - Cover the head and neck.
  - Wrap them in a dry insulating layer (blankets, sleeping bag), with a wind- and waterproof layer (plastic sheet, tarp, or foil blanket) on the outside.

3. REMOVE WET CLOTHING and replace it with dry layers.

4. WARM THE CENTER OF THE BODY FIRST: chest, neck, head, and groin.
  - Use an electric blanket if available.
  - Or use skin-to-skin contact under loose, dry layers of blankets, clothing, towels, or sheets.
  - Chemical heat packs wrapped in cloth can be placed on the chest, armpits, and groin. Never put heat packs directly on bare skin.
  - Don't put heat on the arms and legs, and don't rub or massage them.
  - Don't put a confused or drowsy person in a warm shower or bath. It can make their blood pressure drop and lead to falls or drowning.

5. WARM, SWEET DRINKS or high-calorie food can help, but only if the person is fully awake and can swallow safely. No alcohol. Never give anything to someone who is unconscious or very drowsy.

6. ONCE THEIR TEMPERATURE HAS RISEN, keep them dry and wrapped in a warm blanket, including the head and neck.

7. GET MEDICAL CARE as soon as possible, even if they seem to recover.

IF THEY ARE UNCONSCIOUS OR HAVE NO PULSE OR BREATHING:

- Handle them very gently and get emergency help immediately.
- Start CPR even if the person appears dead.
- Keep doing CPR while they are being warmed, until they respond or medical help takes over.

## Recognizing Frostbite

Frostbite is freezing of the skin and the tissue underneath. It most often affects the nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingers, and toes. It can cause permanent damage and, in severe cases, amputation.

FIRST SIGNS:

- Redness or pain in any skin area. Get out of the cold or cover the skin right away: frostbite may be starting.

SIGNS OF FROSTBITE:

- A white or grayish-yellow skin area
- Skin that feels unusually firm or waxy
- Numbness

Because the skin goes numb, people often don't notice frostbite until someone else points it out. Check each other's faces in the cold.

---

## Frostbite First Aid

1. CHECK FOR HYPOTHERMIA FIRST. If present, treat it first and get emergency care.

2. GET MEDICAL CARE if you can. If it's not available right away:

3. GET INTO A WARM ROOM as soon as possible.

4. DON'T WALK on frostbitten feet or toes unless absolutely necessary. It increases the damage.

5. REMOVE RINGS, watches, and other tight items before swelling starts.

6. REWARM GENTLY, as soon as you can do it without risk of refreezing:
  - Put the area in warm, NOT hot, water: about 99 to 104°F (37 to 40°C), comfortable to the touch on unaffected skin (test it with your wrist). Rewarming usually takes 20 to 30 minutes.
  - Or warm the area with body heat, such as frostbitten fingers in an armpit.

7. DO NOT:
  - Rub or massage the area, or rub it with snow
  - Use a heating pad, heat lamp, hot water bottle, hair dryer, or the heat of a stove, fireplace, or radiator. Numb skin burns easily.
  - Break any blisters that form

8. DON'T THAW IT IF IT MIGHT FREEZE AGAIN. If you're still outdoors and can't keep the area warm, it's better to wait until you reach shelter. Thawing and refreezing causes more damage.

9. AFTER REWARMING, protect the area with loose, dry, bulky dressings, and keep it from refreezing.

Rewarming is often painful (thawing skin can feel like it's burning), and the skin may swell and blister. Get it checked by a medical professional.

## Other Cold Injuries

TRENCH FOOT happens when feet stay wet and cold for a long time, even at temperatures as high as 60°F (16°C). Feet may be red, swollen, numb, or painful. -> Remove wet shoes and socks, dry the feet, keep them warm, and avoid walking on them. Get medical care.

CHILBLAINS are itchy, red, swollen patches on the skin after repeated exposure to cold that isn't freezing. -> Warm the skin slowly, don't scratch, and keep any blisters covered.

## Staying Warm Outdoors

DRESS IN LAYERS:

1. Base layer: wicks moisture away from skin (wool or synthetic). Avoid cotton, which stays wet and cold.
2. Middle layer: insulates (fleece, wool, down).
3. Outer layer: blocks wind and water.

Loose layers trap warm air. Take a layer off before you start to sweat, and put it back on when you stop.

COVER EXPOSED SKIN:

- Hat, scarf or face covering, mittens (warmer than gloves), and warm, dry socks with waterproof boots.
- Your head doesn't lose more heat than any other uncovered part of your body, but it's often the part left uncovered. Cover it.

STAY DRY:

- Change out of wet clothes and socks as soon as you can.
- Carry spare socks and gloves.

FUEL AND WATER:

- Eat regularly. Your body needs food to make heat.
- Drink water or warm, non-alcoholic drinks. Dehydration makes cold injuries more likely.

LIMIT TIME OUTSIDE in extreme cold, and take breaks indoors. Watch others for signs of frostbite and hypothermia.

## Indoors Without Heat

See the Emergency Heating guide for full details.

CREATE A WARM ROOM:

- Choose a small room with few windows.
- Close off the rest of the house.
- Cover windows with blankets or plastic sheeting.
- Block drafts under doors with rolled towels.
- Gather everyone together. Body heat adds up.

CONSERVE BODY HEAT:

- Wear layers, a hat, and socks, even indoors and in bed.
- Use sleeping bags and several blankets.
- Eat regular meals and drink fluids.
- Move around to stay warm, but don't get sweaty.
- Check on older neighbors and relatives often.

NEVER USE THESE INDOORS FOR HEAT (deadly carbon monoxide):

- Gas oven or stovetop
- Charcoal or gas grills
- Camp stoves or outdoor propane heaters
- Generators (outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents)

Use only heaters made for indoor use, and have a working, battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide (CO) alarm. If the CO alarm sounds, or anyone gets a headache, dizziness, nausea, or confusion, get everyone outside and call 911.

If you can't stay warm safely, go to a warming center, shelter, or a home with heat.

## If You're Stranded in a Vehicle

1. STAY WITH THE VEHICLE. It shelters you, and rescuers can find a car more easily than a person. Don't walk away in a storm unless you can see a building close by.
2. CALL OR TEXT for help if you have a signal, and tell them where you are.
3. MAKE YOURSELF VISIBLE: tie a bright cloth to the antenna. Raise the hood if it isn't snowing. At night, turn on the dome light while the engine is running.
4. RUN THE ENGINE AND HEATER ABOUT 10 MINUTES EACH HOUR to stay warm.
5. KEEP THE EXHAUST PIPE CLEAR of snow. A blocked tailpipe can fill the car with deadly carbon monoxide. Check it before each time you run the engine.
6. OPEN ONE WINDOW SLIGHTLY while the engine is running, for fresh air.
7. KEEP MOVING: move your arms and legs and clap your hands to keep blood flowing. Don't overexert yourself.
8. STAY WARM: huddle together, use blankets, newspapers, or floor mats for insulation.
9. STAY AWAKE if you can. If others are with you, take turns sleeping so someone stays alert.
10. DON'T EAT SNOW. It lowers your body temperature. Melt it first if you can.

---

## If You're Caught Outside

1. Get out of the wind: find or make a windbreak (a vehicle, rocks, trees, a snow wall).
2. Get off the cold ground: sit or lie on a pack, branches, or anything that insulates.
3. Cover exposed skin and stay as dry as you can.
4. If you're with others, huddle together.
5. Signal for help: whistle, bright cloth, light, or phone.
6. Don't exhaust yourself. Sweat and fatigue make hypothermia more likely.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Do a cold-readiness check, indoors and at the car. (1) Pick your warm room: the small room with few windows where everyone would gather if the heat failed. Put the blankets, sleeping bags, and towels for the door gaps where you can find them in the dark. (2) Find your thermometer and keep it with the first aid kit. (3) Press the test button on your carbon monoxide alarm. (4) Go out to the car and check it against the Winter Vehicle Kit list, and look at the fuel gauge. (5) Say the hypothermia steps out loud: call for help, warm room, wet clothes off, warm the chest, neck, head, and groin first, warm drinks only if fully awake.

### What Would You Do?

1. Your neighbor's furnace has been out since yesterday. This morning she was shivering. Now she has stopped shivering, is drowsy and slurring her words, and says she only wants to sleep. A hot bath looks like the fastest way to warm her up.

   **Answer:** Call 911. A cold person who stops shivering is getting worse, not better, and drowsiness and slurred speech are warning signs of hypothermia. No bath or shower: in a confused or drowsy person it can make blood pressure drop and lead to a fall or drowning. Handle her gently, get her into a warm room, replace anything wet with dry layers, and warm the center of her body first (chest, neck, head, and groin) with an electric blanket or skin-to-skin contact under loose, dry blankets. Give nothing to drink unless she is fully awake and can swallow.

2. Your teenager comes in after two hours of sledding. Two toes are white, firm, and numb. He wants to prop his feet against the radiator, and his sister offers to rub them warm.

   **Answer:** No radiator and no rubbing. Numb skin burns easily without the person feeling it, and rubbing adds damage. Check him for signs of hypothermia first. Keep him off his feet as much as you can, then rewarm the toes in warm, not hot, water: about 99 to 104°F (37 to 40°C), comfortable on your own wrist, for 20 to 30 minutes. It will probably hurt as they thaw. Afterward, wrap them in loose, dry dressings and get them checked by a medical professional.

3. Your car slides into a ditch on a rural road in a snowstorm. There is no phone signal. You have half a tank of fuel, and you think you passed a gas station a mile or two back.

   **Answer:** Stay with the car. It is shelter, and rescuers can find a car far more easily than a person on foot in a storm. Leave only if you can see a building close by. Tie a bright cloth to the antenna. Run the engine and heater about 10 minutes each hour, check that the tailpipe is clear of snow before each run, and open one window slightly while it is running. Between runs, move your arms and legs, huddle under whatever insulates, and keep trying the phone. When the snow stops, raise the hood.
