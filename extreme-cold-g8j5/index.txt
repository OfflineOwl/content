EXTREME COLD SURVIVAL
=====================

Cold can kill. Hypothermia (dangerously low body temperature) and
frostbite can develop quickly in wind, wet clothes, or cold water, and
even indoors in an unheated home. Stay dry, get out of the wind, and
know the warning signs.


DO THIS NOW
-----------

If someone may have HYPOTHERMIA (shivering, exhaustion, confusion,
fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness; in babies,
bright red cold skin and very low energy):

1. Take their temperature if you can. Below 95°F (35°C) is a medical
   emergency. Call 911 (or your local emergency number).
2. Get them into a warm room or shelter. Handle them gently.
3. Remove any wet clothing.
4. Warm the CENTER of the body first: chest, neck, head, and groin.
   Use an electric blanket if you have one, or skin-to-skin contact
   under loose, dry layers of blankets, clothing, towels, or sheets.
5. Give warm, sweet, non-alcoholic drinks ONLY if they are fully
   awake and able to swallow. Never give drinks to an unconscious
   person.
6. Don't put a confused or drowsy person in a warm shower or bath.
7. No breathing or pulse? Start CPR and keep going while they are
   warmed, until they respond or help takes over.

If someone may have FROSTBITE (white or grayish-yellow skin, skin that
feels unusually firm or waxy, numbness):

1. Check for hypothermia first. It is more serious and needs emergency
   care.
2. Get to a warm room. Seek medical care.
3. Do not walk on frostbitten feet or toes unless you must.
4. Remove rings and other tight jewelry.
5. Rewarm in warm (NOT hot) water, or with body heat (for example,
   fingers in an armpit). Don't thaw it if it could freeze again.
6. Do NOT rub or massage the area, and do NOT use snow.
7. Do NOT use a heating pad, heat lamp, hot water bottle, hair dryer,
   stove, fireplace, or radiator. Numb skin burns easily.


UNDERSTANDING COLD DANGER
-------------------------

HOW YOU LOSE HEAT:
- Wind strips heat from exposed skin (wind chill).
- Wet clothing and wet skin lose heat much faster than dry.
- Cold water pulls heat away far faster than cold air.
- Sitting or lying on cold ground or snow drains heat.

WIND CHILL:
Wind chill is how cold it feels on exposed skin when wind is added
to the air temperature. The colder the wind chill, the faster
frostbite can happen.

Example (US National Weather Service wind chill chart):
- Air temperature 0°F (-18°C) with a 15 mph wind = wind chill of -19°F.
  At that wind chill, exposed skin can get frostbite in about 30
  minutes.
- At colder wind chills, frostbite can happen in 10 minutes or less.

Frostbite only happens when the air temperature is below freezing.
Be extra careful with children, who lose heat faster.

Follow cold alerts from your national weather service. In the US,
the National Weather Service issues Cold Weather Advisories and
Extreme Cold Warnings.

WHO IS MOST AT RISK:
- Older adults, especially those living alone or without enough heat
- Babies sleeping in cold rooms
- People who are outdoors for long periods: outdoor workers, hikers,
  hunters, people experiencing homelessness
- People who drink alcohol or use drugs
- People with diabetes, heart disease, poor circulation, or conditions
  that affect thinking or movement
- Anyone who is wet, tired, hungry, or exposed to wind

ALCOHOL DOES NOT WARM YOU. Alcohol and caffeine make your body lose
heat faster. Avoid them in the cold, and drink warm, sweet drinks or
broth instead.


RECOGNIZING HYPOTHERMIA
-----------------------

Hypothermia happens when your body loses heat faster than it can make
it, and body temperature falls below 95°F (35°C). It affects the
brain, so the person may not realize what is happening.

WARNING SIGNS IN ADULTS:
- Shivering
- Exhaustion or feeling very tired
- Confusion
- Fumbling hands
- Memory loss
- Slurred speech
- Drowsiness

WARNING SIGNS IN BABIES:
- Bright red, cold skin
- Very low energy

AS IT GETS WORSE:
- Shivering may STOP. A cold person who stops shivering is getting
  worse, not better.
- Breathing and pulse slow down and may be hard to detect.
- The person may become unconscious and appear dead. Keep helping:
  people with severe hypothermia who seem dead have been revived.

Hypothermia can happen even at temperatures above 40°F (4°C) if a
person is wet from rain, sweat, or cold water.


HYPOTHERMIA FIRST AID
---------------------

1. CALL FOR HELP. If the temperature is below 95°F (35°C), or you
   can't measure it but the person has warning signs, get medical
   help immediately.

2. GET THEM OUT OF THE COLD. Move them into a warm room, shelter, or
   vehicle. Handle them gently; rough handling can be dangerous in
   severe hypothermia.
   If you can't move them indoors:
   - Get them out of the wind and insulate them from the ground.
   - Cover the head and neck.
   - Wrap them in a dry insulating layer (blankets, sleeping bag),
     with a wind- and waterproof layer (plastic sheet, tarp, or foil
     blanket) on the outside.

3. REMOVE WET CLOTHING and replace it with dry layers.

4. WARM THE CENTER OF THE BODY FIRST: chest, neck, head, and groin.
   - Use an electric blanket if available.
   - Or use skin-to-skin contact under loose, dry layers of blankets,
     clothing, towels, or sheets.
   - Chemical heat packs wrapped in cloth can be placed on the chest,
     armpits, and groin. Never put heat packs directly on bare skin.
   - Don't put heat on the arms and legs, and don't rub or massage
     them.
   - Don't put a confused or drowsy person in a warm shower or bath.
     It can make their blood pressure drop and lead to falls or
     drowning.

5. WARM, SWEET DRINKS or high-calorie food can help, but only if
   the person is fully awake and can swallow safely. No alcohol.
   Never give anything to someone who is unconscious or very drowsy.

6. ONCE THEIR TEMPERATURE HAS RISEN, keep them dry and wrapped in a
   warm blanket, including the head and neck.

7. GET MEDICAL CARE as soon as possible, even if they seem to recover.

IF THEY ARE UNCONSCIOUS OR HAVE NO PULSE OR BREATHING:
- Handle them very gently and get emergency help immediately.
- Start CPR even if the person appears dead.
- Keep doing CPR while they are being warmed, until they respond or
  medical help takes over.


RECOGNIZING FROSTBITE
---------------------

Frostbite is freezing of the skin and the tissue underneath. It most
often affects the nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingers, and toes. It can
cause permanent damage and, in severe cases, amputation.

FIRST SIGNS:
- Redness or pain in any skin area. Get out of the cold or cover the
  skin right away: frostbite may be starting.

SIGNS OF FROSTBITE:
- A white or grayish-yellow skin area
- Skin that feels unusually firm or waxy
- Numbness

Because the skin goes numb, people often don't notice frostbite until
someone else points it out. Check each other's faces in the cold.


FROSTBITE FIRST AID
-------------------

1. CHECK FOR HYPOTHERMIA FIRST. If present, treat it first and get
   emergency care.

2. GET MEDICAL CARE if you can. If it's not available right away:

3. GET INTO A WARM ROOM as soon as possible.

4. DON'T WALK on frostbitten feet or toes unless absolutely
   necessary. It increases the damage.

5. REMOVE RINGS, watches, and other tight items before swelling
   starts.

6. REWARM GENTLY, as soon as you can do it without risk of
   refreezing:
   - Put the area in warm, NOT hot, water: about 99 to 104°F (37 to
     40°C), comfortable to the touch on unaffected skin (test it with
     your wrist). Rewarming usually takes 20 to 30 minutes.
   - Or warm the area with body heat, such as frostbitten fingers in
     an armpit.

7. DO NOT:
   - Rub or massage the area, or rub it with snow
   - Use a heating pad, heat lamp, hot water bottle, hair dryer, or
     the heat of a stove, fireplace, or radiator. Numb skin burns
     easily.
   - Break any blisters that form

8. DON'T THAW IT IF IT MIGHT FREEZE AGAIN. If you're still outdoors
   and can't keep the area warm, it's better to wait until you reach
   shelter. Thawing and refreezing causes more damage.

9. AFTER REWARMING, protect the area with loose, dry, bulky
   dressings, and keep it from refreezing.

Rewarming is often painful (thawing skin can feel like it's burning),
and the skin may swell and blister. Get it checked by a medical
professional.


OTHER COLD INJURIES
-------------------

TRENCH FOOT happens when feet stay wet and cold for a long time, even
at temperatures as high as 60°F (16°C). Feet may be red, swollen,
numb, or painful.
-> Remove wet shoes and socks, dry the feet, keep them warm, and avoid
   walking on them. Get medical care.

CHILBLAINS are itchy, red, swollen patches on the skin after repeated
exposure to cold that isn't freezing.
-> Warm the skin slowly, don't scratch, and keep any blisters
   covered.


STAYING WARM OUTDOORS
---------------------

DRESS IN LAYERS:
1. Base layer: wicks moisture away from skin (wool or synthetic).
   Avoid cotton, which stays wet and cold.
2. Middle layer: insulates (fleece, wool, down).
3. Outer layer: blocks wind and water.

Loose layers trap warm air. Take a layer off before you start to
sweat, and put it back on when you stop.

COVER EXPOSED SKIN:
- Hat, scarf or face covering, mittens (warmer than gloves), and
  warm, dry socks with waterproof boots.
- Your head doesn't lose more heat than any other uncovered part of
  your body, but it's often the part left uncovered. Cover it.

STAY DRY:
- Change out of wet clothes and socks as soon as you can.
- Carry spare socks and gloves.

FUEL AND WATER:
- Eat regularly. Your body needs food to make heat.
- Drink water or warm, non-alcoholic drinks. Dehydration makes cold
  injuries more likely.

LIMIT TIME OUTSIDE in extreme cold, and take breaks indoors. Watch
others for signs of frostbite and hypothermia.


INDOORS WITHOUT HEAT
--------------------

See the Emergency Heating guide for full details.

CREATE A WARM ROOM:
- Choose a small room with few windows.
- Close off the rest of the house.
- Cover windows with blankets or plastic sheeting.
- Block drafts under doors with rolled towels.
- Gather everyone together. Body heat adds up.

CONSERVE BODY HEAT:
- Wear layers, a hat, and socks, even indoors and in bed.
- Use sleeping bags and several blankets.
- Eat regular meals and drink fluids.
- Move around to stay warm, but don't get sweaty.
- Check on older neighbors and relatives often.

NEVER USE THESE INDOORS FOR HEAT (deadly carbon monoxide):
- Gas oven or stovetop
- Charcoal or gas grills
- Camp stoves or outdoor propane heaters
- Generators (outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors,
  and vents)

Use only heaters made for indoor use, and have a working,
battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide (CO) alarm. If the
CO alarm sounds, or anyone gets a headache, dizziness, nausea, or
confusion, get everyone outside and call 911.

If you can't stay warm safely, go to a warming center, shelter, or a
home with heat.


IF YOU'RE STRANDED IN A VEHICLE
-------------------------------

1. STAY WITH THE VEHICLE. It shelters you, and rescuers can find a car
   more easily than a person. Don't walk away in a storm unless you
   can see a building close by.
2. CALL OR TEXT for help if you have a signal, and tell them where
   you are.
3. MAKE YOURSELF VISIBLE: tie a bright cloth to the antenna. Raise the
   hood if it isn't snowing. At night, turn on the dome light while
   the engine is running.
4. RUN THE ENGINE AND HEATER ABOUT 10 MINUTES EACH HOUR to stay warm.
5. KEEP THE EXHAUST PIPE CLEAR of snow. A blocked tailpipe can fill
   the car with deadly carbon monoxide. Check it before each time you
   run the engine.
6. OPEN ONE WINDOW SLIGHTLY while the engine is running, for fresh
   air.
7. KEEP MOVING: move your arms and legs and clap your hands to keep
   blood flowing. Don't overexert yourself.
8. STAY WARM: huddle together, use blankets, newspapers, or floor
   mats for insulation.
9. STAY AWAKE if you can. If others are with you, take turns sleeping
   so someone stays alert.
10. DON'T EAT SNOW. It lowers your body temperature. Melt it first if
    you can.


IF YOU'RE CAUGHT OUTSIDE
------------------------

1. Get out of the wind: find or make a windbreak (a vehicle, rocks,
   trees, a snow wall).
2. Get off the cold ground: sit or lie on a pack, branches, or
   anything that insulates.
3. Cover exposed skin and stay as dry as you can.
4. If you're with others, huddle together.
5. Signal for help: whistle, bright cloth, light, or phone.
6. Don't exhaust yourself. Sweat and fatigue make hypothermia more
   likely.
