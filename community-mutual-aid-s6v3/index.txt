COMMUNITY MUTUAL AID BASICS
===========================

In a large disaster the first help to arrive is often the
person next door. Fire, ambulance and utility crews go to the
worst problems first, and that can leave a street on its own for
hours or days. Neighbors who already know each other's names,
needs and skills fill that gap. Neighbors who meet for the first
time in the dark mostly do not.


START HERE
----------

1. KNOCK ON TWO DOORS THIS WEEK: the homes on either side of
   you. Swap names and phone numbers. Write them on paper.
2. ASK ONE QUESTION: "If the power were out for three days, is
   there anything you would need a hand with?" Then answer it
   yourself.
3. NOTICE WHO MIGHT BE ALONE on your street or floor: an older
   person, someone who uses a wheelchair, oxygen or home care, a
   parent on their own with small children, someone new who does
   not speak the local language well.
4. AGREE ON A SIGNAL. A card in the front window that says OK on
   one side and HELP on the other is enough.
5. PICK A GATHERING SPOT everyone can reach on foot: a driveway,
   a lobby, the corner by the mailboxes.

That is a working mutual aid group. Everything below makes it
better, but those five steps are most of the value.


GET EMERGENCY HELP IF
---------------------

Mutual aid does not replace emergency services. Call 911 (or
your local emergency number) when:

- Someone is trapped, badly hurt, not breathing normally, or
  cannot be woken
- You smell gas, hear it hissing, or see fire or downed power
  lines. Keep everyone away and call from a distance.
- A neighbor who should be home does not answer and you have
  reason to think they are hurt or ill
- Someone is threatening people or property. Do not confront
  them. Move away and call.

If phones are down, send two people together to the nearest fire
station, police post or staffed shelter with a written note:
the address, what is wrong, how many people.

DO NOT:
- Go into a building that is damaged, leaning, flooded or
  smells of gas to look for someone. Shout, listen, and report
  it.
- Wade into moving water or go near downed lines.
- Give anyone another person's prescription medicine.


WHAT MUTUAL AID IS
------------------

Mutual aid is neighbors agreeing to look out for each other and
share what they have, with everyone both giving and receiving.
It is not charity, and it is not a militia. Most of it is
ordinary: a charged phone lent for a call, a meal for the
household with a newborn, a ride to the pharmacy, someone to sit
with a frightened person.

It works because local knowledge is exact. An emergency
dispatcher does not know that the man in number 14 is deaf and
will not hear a knock, or that the family in 9 has a chainsaw
and a nurse. You do, if you have asked.


BEFORE: GET ORGANIZED IN AN HOUR
--------------------------------

Keep the group small: one block, one cul-de-sac, one floor of a
building. A group small enough to meet in one living room is a
group where everyone gets checked.

Invite everyone to a one-hour meeting, and bring food. Cover
four things:

1. A CONTACT SHEET
   Name, address or unit, phone, an emergency contact outside
   the household, pets. Paper copies for each home, because
   phones die.

2. A MAP
   Sketch the street or floor. Mark each home, the gathering
   spot, and where the gas meters, water shutoffs and fire
   extinguishers are. A stranger helping in the dark needs to
   find them.

3. SKILLS AND EQUIPMENT
   Ask what people are willing to share. Nobody owes the group
   anything.

   Skills: first aid or medical training, CPR, child care,
   electrical or plumbing work, languages spoken, sign language,
   ham radio, cooking for a crowd.

   Equipment: generator, chainsaw and safety gear, ladders, hand
   tools, wheelbarrow, pry bar, camp stove, water containers,
   battery radio, tarps, a vehicle with high clearance, a spare
   room.

4. WHO MAY NEED A KNOCK FIRST
   Ask people to say for themselves what help they would want.
   Typical answers: help getting down stairs, a check when the
   power is out because of medical equipment, someone to read
   or translate alerts, a hand with a pet, a ride.

A NOTE ON PRIVACY: this list says who lives alone and who owns
what. Keep it on paper inside the group, share only what each
person agreed to, and do not post it online.

Then choose the signal and the gathering spot, and decide who
checks on whom so that no home is left to chance. Pair every
household with two others.


READY-MADE PROGRAMS
-------------------

You do not have to invent this.

MAP YOUR NEIGHBORHOOD (MYN)
A program long run by Washington State's emergency management
agency that walks a block through exactly the steps above: a
contact list, a map, a skills and equipment inventory, an
OK/HELP card for each home, and a gathering site. The agency no
longer prints the original workbook, but its current "Make a
Community Plan" pages offer the same tools as free downloads: a
contacts sheet, a skills and resource list, grid paper for the
map, and the OK/HELP card. Many cities and counties run their
own version; ask your local emergency management office.

CERT (COMMUNITY EMERGENCY RESPONSE TEAM)
A FEMA-backed volunteer program, started in Los Angeles in 1985
and now running in all 50 US states, with more than 3,200 local
programs and more than 600,000 people trained. The course covers
fire safety, light search and rescue, team organization and
disaster medical operations. Some areas run Teen CERT. Ask your
fire department or emergency management office about a local
program. One or two trained people on a block change what that
block can safely do.

FIRST AID AND CPR CLASSES
The most useful single skill to add. See the Basic First Aid
Without Power guide.

Outside the US, look for the equivalent: community first
responder schemes, Red Cross or Red Crescent volunteer branches,
neighborhood or civil defense groups. The method is the same
everywhere.


DURING: THE FIRST HOURS
-----------------------

1. YOUR OWN HOUSEHOLD FIRST. Check for injuries. Put on sturdy
   shoes, long sleeves and gloves. Deal with fire, gas and water
   at home (see the Household Water Shutoff & Safety guide).
2. PUT OUT YOUR SIGNAL. OK or HELP in the front window or on the
   door.
3. GO TO THE GATHERING SPOT. Bring the contact sheet, the map
   and a flashlight.
4. PICK ONE PERSON TO COORDINATE at the spot and one to listen
   to the radio for official instructions.
5. SEND PAIRS, NEVER ONE PERSON ALONE, to:
   - Homes showing HELP
   - Homes showing no card at all
   - The people on the "knock first" list, card or no card
   - Gas meters and visible hazards, to mark and report
6. AT EACH DOOR: knock, call the person's name, look through
   windows, try their phone. If there is no answer and the
   building looks damaged, do not go in. Report it.
7. COME BACK AND WRITE IT DOWN: address, who is there, what they
   need, what was done. A written list is what you hand to
   responders when they arrive.

Stay within what you are trained and equipped to do. Lifting
heavy debris, cutting trees tangled in power lines and entering
damaged buildings are how would-be rescuers get hurt. Putting out
a small fire, giving first aid, moving someone away from danger
and sending for help are the jobs that fit a neighbor.


CHECKING ON PEOPLE WHO ARE ISOLATED
-----------------------------------

Older adults, people with disabilities, and people who live
alone are the ones most often missed. Agree before anything
happens how they want to be checked on, and by whom.

- VISIT AT LEAST DAILY while the emergency lasts, and more often
  in extreme heat or cold.
- LOOK AS WELL AS ASK. Is the home dangerously hot or cold? Is
  there water and food they can actually open and prepare? Do
  they have their medicines? Are they confused, unusually
  sleepy or unsteady?
- POWER-DEPENDENT EQUIPMENT: oxygen concentrators, ventilators,
  powered wheelchairs, refrigerated medicines such as insulin.
  Know who has them and what their backup plan is. See the
  Managing Chronic Conditions in Emergencies guide.
- A SPARE KEY with a trusted neighbor, by the person's own
  choice, turns a welfare check from a guess into a visit.
- LEARN THE EQUIPMENT. Ask to be shown how the wheelchair
  folds, how the lift works, where the medicines are kept.
- DEAF OR HARD OF HEARING: knocking does not work. Agree on a
  text, a flashlight at the window, or a key.
- LANGUAGE: pair people with a neighbor who speaks their
  language and can pass on official alerts accurately.
- EVACUATION: decide now who offers a ride to people who do not
  drive. Pets come too.

Let people give as well as receive. The neighbor who cannot
leave her chair can run the phone list, watch children or keep
the written log. Being needed is good for people.


SHARING FAIRLY
--------------

Shortage turns small unfairness into big resentment. Settle the
rules while everyone is calm.

- SHARE BY NEED FIRST. Babies, pregnant and nursing women,
  people who are ill and people doing heavy work need more water
  and food. Say so openly and most people agree.
- WHAT YOU OFFER IS YOURS TO OFFER. Nobody is required to hand
  over their supplies, and no one should be shamed for keeping a
  reserve for their own family. Pooling works when it is
  voluntary.
- KEEP A WRITTEN LOG of what came in, what went out and to whom,
  kept by two people and open for anyone to read.
- LENT TOOLS COME BACK. Write the owner's name on tape. Whoever
  borrows the chainsaw also needs the safety gear and the skill.
- NO BUYING FAVOR. Help is not traded for payment or for
  promises. See the Emergency Cash & Barter Basics guide for
  fair exchange between households.
- INCLUDE THE PEOPLE YOU DO NOT KNOW WELL: renters, new
  arrivals, the household nobody talks to. A group that only
  looks after its friends is not safer; it is smaller.

FOOD AND WATER SAFETY WHEN POOLING:
- Use refrigerated and thawing food first, and only while it
  is still cold. When in doubt, throw it out. See the Food
  Safety Without Refrigeration guide.
- Share water from sealed or known-safe containers, or treat it.
  See the Water Purification guide.
- Wash hands before handling shared food.

GENERATORS AND POWER:
- Run a shared generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet (6 m)
  from every home's doors, windows and vents.
- Run a heavy-duty outdoor extension cord straight to the
  appliance: a refrigerator, a medical device, a phone-charging
  strip. Never plug a generator into a wall outlet.
- A charging table at the gathering spot serves the most people
  for the least fuel. See the Generator Safety guide.

MEDICINES:
- Do not swap prescription medicines. Help people reach a
  pharmacy, a clinic or emergency services instead.


INFORMATION AND DISAGREEMENTS
-----------------------------

- ONE NOTICE BOARD at the gathering spot: official instructions
  with the time and source, needs, offers, road conditions.
  Rumor fills any gap you leave. See the Verifying Information
  During Emergencies guide.
- SET CHECK-IN TIMES, for example 9 a.m. and 5 p.m., so people
  are not wandering around looking for news.
- SAFETY MEANS WATCHING AND REPORTING. Note hazards and anything
  suspicious, and call the police. Do not set up patrols that
  stop, question or confront people. That is how neighbors get
  hurt, and laws on it vary by place.
- WHEN PEOPLE DISAGREE: hear each side out, talk about the need
  instead of the person, and ask someone both sides trust to
  decide. Tired, frightened people are short-tempered. Assume
  that before you assume bad intent.


WHEN OFFICIAL HELP ARRIVES
--------------------------

Hand the first responder or relief worker your written list:

- How many homes and people you have checked, and how many you
  could not reach
- Injuries and urgent medical needs, with addresses
- People who cannot leave without help
- Hazards: gas smells, downed lines, unstable buildings,
  blocked roads
- What the group already has covered

Then follow their direction. If you want to help beyond your own
street, do it through an established relief organization. FEMA's
advice is blunt: do not self-deploy to disaster areas, give only
goods that have been asked for, and remember that money to a
trusted relief organization is usually the fastest and most
effective help.


AFTER
-----

Recovery outlasts the emergency by months. Keep checking on the
same people, because the hard part for many comes when the
attention goes away. Help with forms, rides and cleanup (see the
Post-Event Cleanup Safety and Long-Term Recovery Planning
guides).

Meet once more and ask: Who did we miss? What did we lack? What
would we do differently? Update the contact sheet and the map,
and set a date to check them each year.

PRACTICE
--------

10-MINUTE DRILL:
Draw your block from memory. Take a sheet of paper and sketch
the eight homes or units nearest yours. For each one write the
name of an adult who lives there, a phone number, and one thing
you know about them that would matter in an emergency (works
nights, has a baby, uses a walker, owns a generator, is a
nurse). Count the blanks. Then pick the emptiest box, walk over
and introduce yourself. That conversation is the drill.

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

1. It is the second day of a summer outage. You own the only
   generator on the street. The man next door runs an oxygen
   concentrator and his battery backup is nearly flat. Another
   neighbor, an electrician's son, offers to wire your generator
   into your house panel "so we can run a cord to him from your
   kitchen."

   ANSWER: Say no to the wiring and yes to the neighbor. Feeding
   a generator into house wiring without a proper transfer
   switch can send power back down the line and kill a utility
   worker, and it can start a fire. Set the generator outdoors,
   at least 20 feet from both houses, and run one heavy-duty
   outdoor cord straight to the concentrator. Then make the
   calls: his oxygen supplier for backup tanks, the power
   company to report a medical need, and 911 if he is short of
   breath or the supply is about to run out.

2. Your group has pooled bottled water at the gathering spot.
   On day three, a family from the next street, whom nobody
   knows, asks for some for their baby. One of your neighbors
   says the water is for the people who contributed.

   ANSWER: Give them water. Need comes first, a baby's need is
   the clearest there is, and a group that turns away an infant
   has lost the thing that makes it worth having. Then deal
   with the real worry, which is running out: count what is
   left, write it in the log, set a daily amount per person,
   send someone to find out where official water is being
   distributed, and invite the next street to set up the same
   system.

3. After an earthquake, every home on your side of the street
   shows an OK card except the house of an 84-year-old widower.
   No card, no answer to knocking, his car is there. The
   chimney has fallen through part of the roof and the front
   door is jammed.

   ANSWER: Do not force your way into a damaged building. Call
   his name at every window, listen, look in with a flashlight,
   and ring his phone. Call 911 and report a possible trapped
   person with the address and what you see. If phones are
   down, send two people to the nearest fire station and leave
   one person outside, in the open, to keep calling to him and
   to meet the crew. If you can see and reach him without
   entering the damaged part, talk to him and pass in water and
   a blanket.


QUICK REFERENCE
---------------

BEFORE:
Names + numbers on paper. Map. Skills and gear list.
Knock-first list. OK/HELP cards. Gathering spot.

FIRST HOURS:
1. Own household safe
2. OK or HELP in the window
3. Go to the gathering spot
4. Pairs check HELP homes, no-card homes, knock-first list
5. Write down needs. Report to responders.

CALL 911: trapped, badly hurt, gas, fire, downed lines,
no answer from someone who should be home.

NEVER:
- Enter damaged buildings
- Go out alone
- Plug a generator into a wall outlet
- Share prescription medicines
- Confront suspicious people
