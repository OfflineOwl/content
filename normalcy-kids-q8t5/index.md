# Re-Establishing Normalcy for Kids

> **To a child, routine is the proof that things are safe.** After a disaster, most children recover well when the adults around them stay steady, tell the truth simply, and bring back the ordinary rhythm of the day. This guide shows how, and how to spot the child who needs more than that.

## Start here

1. **Steady yourself first.** Children take their emotional cues from adults. Take a breath before you pick them up or answer a question. You do not have to hide that you are sad; you do have to look like someone who is coping.
2. **Bring back three anchors today: wake-up, meals, and bedtime.** Same order, about the same time, wherever you are sleeping. Everything else can stay messy for now.
3. **Ask what they know, then tell the truth in a few plain sentences.** "The flood broke our house. We are staying with Aunt Rosa. You are safe and I am staying with you."
4. **Turn off the news.** Keep coverage of the event, and adult conversations about it, away from young children.
5. **Give them something to do.** Play for the little ones, a real job for the older ones, and time with friends for everyone.

This guide is about children's emotional recovery. For keeping children physically safe and not getting separated, see the Protecting Children During Chaos guide.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if a child or teen:

- **Has hurt themselves or is about to,** or is threatening to hurt someone else
- **Is confused, very hard to wake, or not making sense.** This can be a medical problem, not an emotional one. See the Pediatric Emergency Care guide.

Get help the same day if a child or teen:

- **Talks about wanting to die, not being here, or killing themselves,** in words, drawings or messages. In the US, call or text **988** (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), any time. Stay with them. See the Mental Health First Aid in Crisis guide.
- **Has stopped eating or drinking,** or has not slept for several nights.

Other countries have their own crisis lines and emergency numbers. Write yours in the Quick Reference below before you need it.

## What is normal, by age

Most of what worries parents in the first weeks is an ordinary reaction to an extraordinary event. Children often cannot say "I am scared," so they show it with their bodies and behavior.

| Age | What you may see | What helps most |
|-----|------------------|-----------------|
| **Babies and toddlers (under 3)** | More crying and fussing, wanting to be held, changes in sleep and feeding. They cannot understand the event, but they feel their caregiver's distress. | Holding, a calm voice, regular feeding and sleep, the same few familiar people |
| **Preschool (about 3 to 6)** | Clinging and fear of being apart, tantrums, going back to bed-wetting, thumb-sucking or baby talk, fear of the dark, nightmares, acting out the event in play again and again | Closeness, short honest answers repeated as often as asked, "you are safe now," a firm routine |
| **School age (about 7 to 10)** | Fear it will happen again, sadness or anger, stomachaches and headaches, trouble concentrating, not wanting to go to school, telling the story over and over or refusing to talk about it, guilt about what they did or did not do | Facts, correcting rumors, "it was not your fault," play and friends, school, a useful job |
| **Preteens and teens** | Withdrawing, arguing, sleeping too much or too little, saying "I'm fine" and nothing else, risk-taking, alcohol or drug use, worry about seeming different from friends | Your time without a lecture, real information, a say in family decisions, friends, clear limits |

Going backward (a toilet-trained child wetting the bed, a ten-year-old wanting to be dressed) is one of the most common reactions and one of the most misread. It is not naughtiness and it is not permanent. Expect it, do not punish or shame it, and let it fade.

Reactions can also arrive late. A child who seemed untouched for two weeks may fall apart in the third, or when a reminder comes: heavy rain, sirens, the smell of smoke.

## Why routine works

A disaster teaches a child that the world can change without warning. Routine teaches the opposite, many times a day: breakfast came, then school, then the story, then lights out, just as you said. Each kept promise is a small piece of evidence that life is predictable again. The National Child Traumatic Stress Network singles out the plainest version of this: be where you said you would be, pick children up when you said you would, and tell them where you are going and when you will be back.

**Start with the three anchors.** Wake-up, meals and bedtime hold the rest of the day in place. Eat together when you can.

**Make the routine portable.** In a shelter, a motel or a relative's spare room you cannot recreate home, but you can keep the order of things: wash, pajamas, the same story or song, the same goodnight words. Consistency matters more than getting it perfect. If there is no bath, a wash with a cloth at the usual time does the same job.

**Say what has changed and for how long.** "We will sleep here for about two weeks. Your school is closed until Monday. Dad goes back to work tomorrow and will be home for dinner." If you do not know, say so, and say what you do know.

**Bend the rules, keep the frame.** A night light, a parent sitting by the bed, or a mattress on your floor for a while is reasonable comfort. Bedtime itself, meals and kindness to siblings stay. Children feel safer when the adults are still in charge.

**Give small choices.** Which shirt, which book, which of two snacks. A child who has lost control of the big things gets some back through small ones.

A simple day that works almost anywhere:

| Time | Anchor |
|------|--------|
| Morning | Up at the usual time, wash, dressed, breakfast together |
| Midday | School or a set "work time" (reading, drawing, a chore), then lunch |
| Afternoon | Active play outdoors if it is safe, time with other children |
| Evening | Dinner together, a quiet hour with no news, the usual bedtime ritual |

## Talking about what happened

- **Ask first.** "What have you heard?" and "What are you wondering about?" Their answers tell you what to correct and how much to say. Many fears come from something half-heard.
- **Give basic facts, not graphic details.** Then stop and let them ask for more.
- **Do not give false reassurance.** You cannot promise it will never happen again. You can promise what is true: "I will do everything I can to keep you safe, and here is our plan."
- **It is fine to say "I don't know."**
- **Name the helpers.** Firefighters, neighbors, the people rebuilding the school.
- **Say the important things more than once.** "You are safe. It was not your fault. I am staying with you." Young children need to hear them many times.
- **End warmly, and leave the door open.** "You can ask me again any time, even at bedtime."

| Instead of | Try |
|------------|-----|
| "Don't worry about it." | "It makes sense that you're worried. What's the scariest part?" |
| "Be brave. Big kids don't cry." | "It's okay to cry. I get sad about it too." |
| "Everything is fine." | "Some things are hard right now, and we are safe." |
| "That will never happen again." | "If it did, here is exactly what we would do." |
| "Stop talking about it." | "I can listen for a bit now, and again after dinner." |

**By age:** For a preschooler, two or three concrete sentences about what happened and what it means for them. For a school-age child, more facts, plus correcting rumors from friends. For a teenager, an honest adult conversation, including what you are worried about and what the family will decide together. For a child with a developmental disability, pitch the explanation to their level of understanding rather than their age, start with less, and use whatever usually comforts them.

Never push a child to talk, draw or "let it out" before they are ready. Most children talk easily in their own time. Some are upset all over again by retelling it or by hearing others tell it. Let them leave the conversation.

## News, screens and overheard talk

The American Academy of Pediatrics is direct about this: limit media coverage of the disaster, and keep young children away from repeated graphic images and sounds on TV, radio, social media and the internet.

- **Young children:** no coverage of the event at all if you can manage it. That includes a TV left on in the background.
- **Older children:** discourage watching it live. Look at it first yourself, then watch together and talk.
- **Teens:** they will see it on their phones regardless. Ask what they are seeing and whether it is true. The Verifying Information During Emergencies guide can help.
- **Adults:** children overhear phone calls and worried kitchen conversations. Save the insurance and money talk for after bedtime.

The hours after a crisis are a good time to switch everything off and sit down together.

## Play, drawing and useful jobs

Play is how young children think. A preschooler who floods the toy house every afternoon or drives the ambulance to every game is working through what happened, and that is expected for some weeks. Join in if invited. You can gently add a safe ending: the family gets to the roof, the helpers come.

- **Offer the tools, not the assignment:** paper and crayons, blocks, toy vehicles, dolls, a notebook for an older child.
- **Get them moving.** Running, ball games and playing with other children burn off stress that talking does not reach.
- **Bring back the ordinary pleasures:** the favorite game, the familiar movie, friends, hobbies, a birthday kept.
- **Give a job.** Let a child off chores for a day or two, then give tasks that fit their age and matter: handing out water, feeding the pet, reading to a younger child. Feeling useful is the opposite of feeling helpless.
- **Help them help.** Thank-you cards for the people who helped, or a letter to a friend who lost their home.

Watch play that stays grim and stuck, the same frightening scene with no change and a child who seems more upset afterward, not less. If it goes on for weeks, mention it to the pediatrician.

## Sleep and school

**Bedtime** is when fears come out. Keep the ritual, add comfort (a night light, a door left open, you sitting nearby), and plan to step it back gradually as the child settles. Nightmares are common in the first weeks: comfort, a few words, back to bed.

**School** is routine, friends and a second set of caring adults all at once, so get children back as soon as it reopens. Tell the teacher what your child has been through and ask how they seem in class. If school is closed for a long time, set a daily "school hour" and arrange time with other children.

## Look after yourself

Parents and caregivers are almost always a child's best source of support, and you can only give it if you are coping yourself. Eat, sleep, move, and go easy on alcohol. Take your own fears to another adult, not to your child. If you lose your temper, repair it: "I shouted because I was scared. That was not your fault." The Mental Health First Aid in Crisis guide has more for adults.

## Children who need extra support

- **Children with disabilities or special health needs** may need more reassurance, more explanation and more physical comfort, and feel a broken routine more sharply. Rebuild their routine first. See the Special Needs Preparedness guide.
- **Children who lost someone,** including a pet. Use the real words ("died"), expect questions in bursts, and consider a counselor who works with grieving children.
- **Children who have moved,** changed schools or lost their belongings are dealing with losses on top of the event. Say them out loud and help the child stay in touch with old friends.
- **Children with earlier trauma, anxiety or depression** are more likely to struggle. Tell their doctor or counselor early.

## When to get professional help

Most reactions ease over the first few weeks. Talk to your child's doctor, a school counselor or a child mental health professional if:

- Your child is still very anxious, fearful, sad or angry **more than two to four weeks** after the event
- Things are **getting worse** instead of better, or new problems appear later
- Reactions are getting in the way of **school, friendships or family life**
- You see flashbacks, constant jumpiness, emotional numbness, deep sadness, or grim play that stays stuck
- A teen starts or increases **alcohol or drug use,** or takes dangerous risks
- You are not sure. Children often hide distress, and asking early costs nothing.

In the US, the **Disaster Distress Helpline (call or text 1-800-985-5990)** offers free crisis counseling for anyone affected by a disaster, including parents who are worried about a child. For thoughts of suicide, call or text **988**. Outside the US, ask your doctor, school or local health service.

## First two weeks checklist

- [ ] Wake-up, meals and bedtime at regular times
- [ ] Bedtime ritual kept, wherever we are sleeping
- [ ] Each child told, simply and truthfully, what happened and what comes next
- [ ] News off around the children; adult talk saved for later
- [ ] Time to play and move every day
- [ ] Each child has a small job
- [ ] Contact with friends, and back to school when it reopens
- [ ] Teacher or caregiver told what the child went through
- [ ] I have another adult to talk to
- [ ] Date marked four weeks out to ask honestly: better, the same, or worse?

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Make a "portable bedtime" with your child. Together, write or draw the steps of your bedtime routine on a card, in order: wash, pajamas, teeth, story, song, the words you say at lights out. Let the child pick one book and one small comfort item that could live in the go-bag (or pack a duplicate). Put the card and the items in the bag. Then tell them why: "If we ever have to sleep somewhere else, bedtime comes with us." Children enjoy this, and you now have the single most useful routine written down and packed.

**What would you do?**

1. *A week after the flood, your six-year-old, dry at night for three years, has wet the bed four times. A relative you are staying with says he is too old for this and needs firm consequences.*

   **Answer:** No consequences and no shame. Going back to earlier behavior is one of the most common reactions at this age, and anger or punishment only adds to the distress behind it. Change the sheets without comment, put a waterproof cover on the mattress, keep the bedtime routine steady, and give him extra closeness in the day. Tell the relative it is expected and temporary. If it is still going on after about a month, or comes with other worrying signs, talk to his doctor.

2. *You are staying with family and the television has been on the news channel all day, replaying footage of the wildfire. Your four-year-old is playing on the floor nearby and does not seem to be watching.*

   **Answer:** Turn it off, or move her to another room. Young children take in far more than they appear to, and repeated graphic images and sounds do them more harm than good. Ask the household for news-free hours while the children are up, and check the updates on your phone out of sight. Then ask her what she has seen and correct anything she has misunderstood.

3. *Three weeks after a tornado damaged your home, your fifteen-year-old says "I'm fine" to every question, stays in his room, and his teacher emails that he has stopped handing in work. On Saturday you find an empty bottle in his bag.*

   **Answer:** "I'm fine" from a teenager is often a closed door, not an answer, and the two tempting moves, leaving him alone or coming down hard, both miss. He is past two weeks, school is suffering and alcohol has appeared: that is the pattern that calls for help. Pick a calm moment, say what you have noticed without a lecture, ask what the worst part has been, and listen. Then call his doctor or the school counselor this week. If he says anything about not wanting to be alive, call or text 988 with him that day.
