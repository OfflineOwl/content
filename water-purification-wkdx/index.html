Complete Water Purification Guide

💧 CRITICAL - WATER IS LIFE

Most people can survive only a few days without water, and less in heat. Untreated water can carry germs that cause severe diarrhea, which drains the body of water even faster. This guide covers every practical way to make water safe to drink.

⚡ Do This Now

  1. Use safe water first. Drink stored or commercially bottled water before treating anything. If local officials have issued a boil-water or do-not-drink notice, follow it.
  2. Rule out chemicals. If the water may contain fuel, toxic chemicals, or radioactive material (chemical or fuel smell, oily sheen, floodwater, runoff from industrial sites), don't drink it. Boiling and disinfecting will not make it safe. Find another source.
  3. Clear it. If the water is cloudy, let it settle, then pour the clear water through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter.
  4. Boil it. Bring it to a rolling boil for 1 minute. If you are above 5,000 ft (about 1,500 m), boil for 3 minutes. Let it cool.
  5. Can't boil? Use unscented household bleach. Per gallon: 8 drops of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach. Per quart or liter: 2 drops of either. Double the dose if the water is cloudy, colored, or very cold. Stir, then wait 30 minutes.
  6. Store it in clean, covered containers. Use treated water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, making ice, washing food, and mixing baby formula.

💧 Method 2: Chemical Disinfection

Chemicals are light and cheap. They kill most bacteria and viruses, but they are weak against parasites, especially Cryptosporidium, and work poorly in cloudy or very cold water.

Household Bleach (EPA Method)

Use regular, unscented liquid chlorine bleach labeled as suitable for disinfection or sanitization. Not scented, color-safe, splashless, or "with cleaners" products. Use bleach less than a year old.

Double the dose if the water is cloudy, colored, or very cold. If your bleach is 1% (sold in some countries), CDC's dose is 10 drops per quart or liter, 40 drops per gallon.

  1. Clear cloudy water first (settle, then filter through cloth or a coffee filter).
  2. Add the bleach with a clean dropper.
  3. Stir well and let it stand 30 minutes.
  4. Smell it. A slight chlorine smell means it's ready.
  5. No smell? Repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes. If it still doesn't smell of chlorine, don't drink it; find another source.
  6. Too strong? Pour it between clean containers and let it stand a few hours.

Chlorine Dioxide Tablets or Drops

Kill bacteria, viruses, and Giardia, and are the only common chemical option that works against Cryptosporidium. That takes a long contact time, often several hours. Follow the product label for dose and wait time.

Iodine

2% tincture of iodine: 5 drops per quart or liter of clear water, 10 drops if cloudy or colored. Wait at least 30 minutes. For iodine tablets, follow the label. Iodine does not kill Cryptosporidium.

⚠️ Iodine Warning

Don't use iodine if you are pregnant, have thyroid problems, or are sensitive to iodine. No one should drink iodine-treated water for more than a few weeks at a time.

🔦 Method 3: UV Devices

Battery or USB-powered UV pens and bottle caps damage germs' DNA so they can't cause infection. They work on bacteria, viruses, and parasites, but only in clear water: cloudy water shields germs, so filter it first.

  1. Treat small batches (about 1 liter at a time) in a wide-mouth container.
  2. Turn on the device and stir or shake for the time the manufacturer specifies.
  3. Wait for the completion signal. If the device fails mid-cycle, start again.

UV devices need charged batteries and don't remove chemicals.

☀️ Method 4: Solar Disinfection (SODIS)

Sunlight and heat can disinfect clear water in clear plastic bottles. It's free, but slower and less certain than boiling. Use it when you have no fuel, no chemicals, and no filter.

  1. Use clear PET plastic bottles (soda or water bottles), 2 liters or smaller, without deep scratches.
  2. Check clarity: set the filled bottle on a newspaper headline and look down through it. If you can't read it, filter or settle the water first.
  3. Fill the bottle completely and close it.
  4. Lay it on its side in full sun, ideally on a roof or a sheet of metal, away from shade.
  5. Leave it for the time below, then drink straight from the bottle or pour into a clean cup.

🧪 Method 5: Filtration

Filters physically remove germs and particles. What they catch depends on pore size.

Filter typeRemovesDoesn't remove
Absolute pore size 1 micron or smaller, or NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 "cyst reduction"Parasites (Giardia, Cryptosporidium)Viruses, some bacteria
Absolute pore size 0.3 micron or smallerParasites and bacteriaViruses
Ultrafilter (about 0.01 micron)Parasites, bacteria, most virusesChemicals
Reverse osmosis (RO)Parasites, bacteria, viruses, salt, many chemicalsCheck the label for which chemicals
Activated carbon aloneImproves taste and odor; may reduce some chemicalsGerms (not rated for them)

Most portable hiking filters do not remove viruses. After filtering, add bleach, iodine, or chlorine dioxide, or boil the water. Filters labeled "purifier" are meant to remove viruses too; manufacturers make that claim and EPA does not independently test it. Look for an absolute pore size, and follow the manufacturer's instructions for cleaning, storing, and replacing the filter.

Build an Improvised Filter

An improvised filter clears sediment so that disinfection works better. It does not make water safe on its own.

🔬 Improvised Sand/Charcoal Filter

Cloth: Spreads water, keeps sand in place
Sand: Fine layer (2-3 inches)
Charcoal: May improve taste and odor (2-3 inches); hardwood, not briquettes
Gravel: Keeps charcoal in (1-2 inches)
Rocks: Drainage layer (2-3 inches)
⚠️ Warning: An improvised filter only clears sediment. It does not remove germs or chemicals. Always boil or disinfect the water afterward.

Layer from bottom to top: rocks, gravel, crushed charcoal from a hardwood fire (not briquettes), fine sand, then cloth. Pour water slowly, re-filter if it's still cloudy, then boil or disinfect it. Campfire charcoal is not the activated carbon used in commercial filters and won't make chemically contaminated water safe.

♨️ Method 6: Distillation

Distillation boils water and collects the steam that condenses. It removes germs, salt, heavy metals, and most chemicals, but not some chemicals that evaporate easily, such as certain solvents and fuels. It's the main home method for making seawater drinkable.

Pot distillation (FEMA method):

  1. Fill a pot about halfway with water.
  2. Tie a cup to the handle on the pot's lid so the cup hangs right-side up when the lid is upside down. Make sure the cup doesn't touch the water.
  3. Put the lid on upside down and boil the water for 20 minutes.
  4. The water that drips from the lid into the cup is distilled. Let it cool before handling.

This yields only a small amount for the fuel used. A ground solar still (plastic sheet over a hole, container in the center) also works, but yields are small and unreliable, and digging can cost more sweat than it produces.

🔄 Combining Methods (Maximum Safety)

For doubtful water sources, combine methods:

  1. Settle and pre-filter (cloth, coffee filter)
  2. Filter (commercial filter if you have one)
  3. Disinfect (boil, chemical, or UV)
  4. Store safely in a clean, covered container. Pour water out instead of dipping hands or cups in.

👶 Special Cases

  • Babies: use ready-to-feed formula if you have it. If you must mix formula, use bottled water, or water that has been boiled and cooled.
  • People with weakened immune systems: Cryptosporidium can be severe for them. Boil water, or use bottled water, rather than relying on bleach or iodine.
  • Pregnancy or thyroid problems: avoid iodine. Use boiling, bleach, chlorine dioxide, or a filter.
  • Pets: give pets the same treated or bottled water you drink.
  • Chemical spill, fuel, or radiation: no home method reliably fixes this. Use stored or bottled water, and contact the local health department.

🫙 Storing Treated Water

Use clean, food-grade containers with tight lids. Keep them cool and out of sunlight, away from gasoline, pesticides, and other chemicals. Label them with the date, and replace water you treated and stored yourself every 6 months. See the Water Storage and Rotation guide for details.

🚑 If Someone Gets Sick

Diarrhea and vomiting after drinking untreated water can quickly cause dehydration. Get medical help, or call 911 or your local emergency number, for bloody diarrhea, a fever over 102°F (38.9°C), vomiting that prevents keeping fluids down, diarrhea lasting more than 3 days, confusion, or signs of severe dehydration (little or no urine, dizziness when standing). See the Foodborne Illness guide for rehydration.
