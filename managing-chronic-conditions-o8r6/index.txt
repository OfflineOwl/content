MANAGING CHRONIC CONDITIONS IN EMERGENCIES
==========================================

A disaster does not pause diabetes, heart disease, asthma, kidney
failure, or epilepsy. It takes away the things that keep them
quiet: the pharmacy, the fridge, the wall socket, the clinic. The
people who do well are the ones who wrote a list, built a small
buffer, and decided in advance what they would do when the power
or the pills ran short.


START HERE
----------

Five steps that give most of the protection. All can be started
today.

1. WRITE A ONE-PAGE MEDICATION LIST. Every medicine, the dose,
   when you take it, and what it is for. Add allergies, your
   conditions, your doctor, and your pharmacy. Keep a copy in
   your wallet, one in your kit, and a photo on your phone.

2. BUILD A BUFFER. Ask your doctor, pharmacist, or insurer how to
   keep an emergency supply. Aim for at least a week, two if you
   can: CDC advises 1 to 2 weeks of diabetes supplies, and the
   National Kidney Foundation advises 2 weeks of medicines for
   people on dialysis. Use the oldest first so nothing expires.

3. ASK ONE QUESTION NOW: "What should I do if I miss doses or run
   out?" The answer is different for every medicine. Write it on
   your list. An emergency is the wrong time to guess.

4. PLAN FOR POWER AND COLD. If you rely on a device that plugs in
   (oxygen concentrator, CPAP, nebulizer, home dialysis, powered
   wheelchair) or a medicine that lives in the fridge (insulin),
   decide today what replaces the socket and the fridge. Details
   are below.

5. WEAR MEDICAL ID. A bracelet or necklace speaks for you when you
   cannot. Emergency crews look for one.


GET EMERGENCY HELP IF
---------------------

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) for any of these. Do
not drive yourself.

HEART ATTACK
- Chest pressure, squeezing, or pain that lasts more than a few
  minutes or comes and goes
- Pain or discomfort in an arm, the back, neck, jaw, or stomach
- Shortness of breath, cold sweat, nausea, lightheadedness
- Women more often have the less typical signs: shortness of
  breath, nausea or vomiting, back or jaw pain, unusual tiredness

STROKE (BE FAST)
- Balance lost, Eyesight changed, Face drooping, Arm drifting
  down, Speech slurred or strange: Time to call 911
- Note the time the symptoms started. Treatment depends on it.

BREATHING
- An asthma or COPD flare where your medicines are not relieving
  the symptoms, or breathing is still very hard
- A child who is drowsy or confused, has blue lips or skin, or a
  racing heartbeat
- Oxygen or a ventilator has failed and the backup is running out

DIABETES
- Someone with low blood sugar who has passed out or cannot
  swallow (give glucagon if you have it and know how, and call)
- Blood sugar that stays at 300 mg/dL or higher, fruity-smelling
  breath, vomiting with nothing staying down, or trouble
  breathing. These are signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

SEIZURE
- Lasts longer than 5 minutes, or another follows soon after
- The person does not wake up, has trouble breathing, or is hurt
- First seizure ever, in water, during pregnancy, or in someone
  with diabetes who loses consciousness

MENTAL HEALTH
- Thoughts of suicide or self-harm: in the US call or text 988,
  any time. In immediate danger, call 911.

DIALYSIS
- Missed treatments plus trouble breathing, chest pain, or
  severe weakness

Outside the US, use your local emergency number. Crisis lines
differ by country.


THE MEDICATION LIST
-------------------

In a shelter or an unfamiliar emergency room, nobody has your
records. The list is how a stranger keeps you alive, and how a
pharmacist can refill you without a phone call.

Include:
[ ] Name and date of birth
[ ] Every medicine: name (brand and generic), dose, time of day
[ ] What each one is for
[ ] Allergies and bad reactions
[ ] Conditions and major surgeries
[ ] Devices: make, model, serial number, settings (insulin pump
    rates, oxygen flow, CPAP pressure)
[ ] Doctor, specialist, pharmacy, and supplier phone numbers
[ ] Insurance card copy and photo ID copy
[ ] Emergency contacts

Store it with copies of your prescriptions in a sealed plastic
bag. Update it every time a prescription changes.

A blank version is at the end of this guide.


SUPPLY, REFILLS, AND RUNNING LOW
--------------------------------

BEFORE:
- Refill as early as your plan allows, and keep the buffer in
  one grab bag with the list.
- Check dates every few months and replace what is close to
  expiring.
- When a storm or evacuation is forecast, refill before it
  arrives, not after.

IF YOU ARE RUNNING LOW:
- DO NOT skip doses, split pills, or stop a medicine on your own
  to make it last. Some medicines are dangerous to stop suddenly,
  including anti-seizure medicines and many psychiatric
  medicines. Missed insulin is one of the two most common causes
  of DKA.
- Act while you still have several days left. Go to any open
  pharmacy with your bottles or your list. In a declared
  emergency, some US states let pharmacists give an emergency
  supply without a new prescription. Rules vary by state and
  country, so ask.
- Ask shelter medical staff, a clinic, or a hospital. Getting a
  prescription replaced is a valid reason to be seen.
- No insurance and in a US disaster area? The federal Emergency
  Prescription Assistance Program (EPAP), when it is activated,
  gives a free 30-day supply of most prescription medicines,
  plus medical supplies and some equipment. Call 855-793-7470
  to check whether it is active and to find a pharmacy.
- Only a doctor or pharmacist should change a dose or switch you
  to a different medicine.


MEDICINES AFTER FLOOD, FIRE, OR HEAT
------------------------------------

From the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA):

- FLOOD OR UNSAFE WATER: throw away any medicine that touched
  it, even in its original container with the cap on. Throw
  away anything wet or changed in appearance.
- FIRE OR HIGH HEAT: heat can weaken medicines. Plan to replace
  them.
- THE EXCEPTION: if a medicine is life-sustaining and you cannot
  replace it yet, it may be used if it looks unchanged. Replace
  it as soon as you can.
- MIXING A MEDICINE THAT COMES AS A POWDER: use only purified or
  bottled water.


INSULIN AND OTHER COLD-STORED MEDICINES
---------------------------------------

Insulin does not instantly spoil when the fridge dies. Knowing
the real limits stops people from throwing away good insulin, or
freezing it by accident.

FDA guidance for insulin in an emergency:

- NORMAL STORAGE: in a refrigerator, about 36 to 46°F
  (2 to 8°C).
- NO FRIDGE: insulin in the manufacturer's vial or cartridge,
  opened or unopened, can be left unrefrigerated at 59 to 86°F
  (15 to 30°C) for up to 28 days and still work.
- KEEP IT AS COOL AS YOU CAN, away from direct heat and direct
  sunlight.
- NEVER FREEZE IT. Do not use insulin that has been frozen. If
  you use ice, keep the insulin from touching it: wrap it in a
  towel or put it in a second container.
- ABOVE 86°F (30°C): it loses strength over time. In an
  emergency you may still have to use it. Check your blood sugar
  more often, and replace it as soon as you can.
- INSULIN PUMPS: discard pump insulin that has been above
  98.6°F (37°C).
- AFTERWARD: once you have a fresh supply, throw away insulin
  that sat at room temperature or in extreme heat or cold.

SWITCHING INSULIN: if your usual insulin is not available, FDA
publishes emergency guidance on switching between types. It
should be done under a doctor's care whenever possible, with
close blood sugar checks. Print that page for your kit (see
Sources) and ask your doctor now what your own fallback is.

OTHER REFRIGERATED MEDICINES: check the label or ask a
pharmacist. If the medicine is life-sustaining and you have no
replacement, FDA says it may be used until you can get a new
supply.

A COOLER PLAN: keep a small cooler and reusable ice packs with
your kit. Freeze water bottles ahead of a storm.


DIABETES
--------

KIT (CDC list, 1 to 2 weeks' worth, in a waterproof bag):
[ ] Insulin and syringes or pen needles
[ ] Other diabetes and prescription medicines
[ ] Blood sugar meter, strips, lancets, spare batteries
[ ] Pump and glucose monitor supplies, written pump settings
[ ] Glucagon
[ ] Ketone strips
[ ] Glucose tablets or other fast sugar
[ ] Alcohol wipes
[ ] Empty hard plastic container for used sharps
[ ] Small cooler and ice packs

LOW BLOOD SUGAR (below 70 mg/dL, or shaky, sweaty, hungry,
dizzy, confused, irritable, fast heartbeat):
1. Take 15 to 20 grams of fast sugar:
   - 4 glucose tablets or 1 tube of glucose gel, or
   - 1/2 cup (4 oz / 120 mL) of regular fruit juice, or
   - 1/2 can (4 to 6 oz) of regular, not diet, soda, or
   - 1 tablespoon of sugar or honey
2. Wait 15 minutes and recheck.
3. Still low? Repeat.
4. If your next meal is more than an hour away, eat a snack.
- Passed out or cannot swallow: do not put food or drink in the
  mouth. Give glucagon if you have it and call 911.
- Kidney disease too? Use apple, grape, or cranberry juice, not
  orange juice.

HIGH BLOOD SUGAR AND DKA:
- Stress, illness, and missed insulin all push blood sugar up.
- If you are sick, or your blood sugar is 250 mg/dL or above,
  check blood sugar every 4 to 6 hours and check for ketones.
- Early signs of DKA: great thirst, urinating far more than
  usual. Later: fast deep breathing, fruity breath, flushed
  face, nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, tiredness.
- High ketones are a medical emergency. See the 911 list above.

IN A SHELTER: tell the person in charge that you have diabetes.
They can help with medical care and with keeping insulin cool.

US HELP LINE: American Diabetes Association, 1-800-DIABETES
(800-342-2382).


HEART DISEASE, BLOOD PRESSURE, AND STROKE
-----------------------------------------

- Keep taking your medicines on schedule. Disasters bring heat,
  cold, heavy lifting, poor sleep, and salty food all at once.
- Stock low-sodium canned and packaged food. Emergency food is
  often very salty.
- Keep a home blood pressure monitor and spare batteries if you
  use one.
- Pace cleanup work. Take breaks and share heavy jobs.

SUSPECTED HEART ATTACK:
1. Call 911 first. Do not take anything first and wait to see
   if it helps.
2. The dispatcher may tell you to chew an aspirin and will check
   that it is safe for you. Follow their instructions.
3. If you have been prescribed nitroglycerin, use it as
   prescribed.
4. Sit down, stay calm, unlock the door.

SUSPECTED STROKE:
- Call 911 and note the time symptoms began.
- Do NOT give aspirin. Some strokes are bleeds, and aspirin can
  make them worse.


ASTHMA AND COPD
---------------

- Keep a written action plan from your doctor. It tells you
  what to take as symptoms worsen and when to get help.
- Keep a spare reliever (rescue) inhaler in the kit. Check the
  expiry date and the dose counter.
- If you use a nebulizer, ask your doctor what to use when
  there is no power.
- Smoke, dust, mold, and cold air after a disaster are all
  triggers. See the Air Filtration for Emergencies guide.

IN A FLARE:
1. Use your reliever inhaler as soon as symptoms start.
2. Follow your action plan.
3. Call 911 if your medicines are not relieving the symptoms or
   breathing is still very hard.


OXYGEN AND POWERED MEDICAL DEVICES
----------------------------------

ANSWER THESE BEFORE AN OUTAGE (from the FDA's home-device
booklet). Ask the manufacturer or supplier, and write the
answers down:
[ ] Does the device have a backup? How long does it run, and
    where is it?
[ ] Can it run on another power source? Which?
[ ] How long can I safely be without it?
[ ] What if power fails in the middle of a treatment?
[ ] What batteries does it use, how many, how long do they last?
[ ] Do my supplies or medicines need to stay cold?
[ ] Do I have at least 3 days of supplies?

THEN:
- Tell your power company, and your fire department, that
  someone in the home depends on electrical medical equipment.
  Ask to be on the priority restoration list. A place on the
  list is not a promise: plan as if the power will stay off.
- Have more than one backup plan, and a place to go that has
  power: a relative, a shelter that can meet medical needs, or a
  hospital. Leave while your backup still has plenty of time
  left on it.
- Keep the device's make, model, and serial number with your
  list.

OXYGEN:
- Keep a backup that needs no electricity, normally a large
  oxygen tank, and know how to switch to it. Show a neighbor.
- Portable concentrator: keep spare batteries charged and a car
  charger.
- DO NOT turn your flow rate down to make a battery or tank last
  longer. Stay on the prescribed rate and move to backup or to
  power.
- Oxygen makes everything burn faster. Keep flames and heat
  sources at least 5 feet (1.5 m) away. No candles, no smoking,
  no gas stove while using it. Use flashlights.
- Watch tank gauges and call your supplier early. Deliveries are
  slow after a disaster.

CPAP, BIPAP, NEBULIZER:
- Find out whether it has a battery option or a car adapter.
- Keep distilled water if it has a humidifier.

VENTILATOR:
- Keep a hand-operated resuscitation bag at the bedside.
- Ask your care team about external batteries.
- When backup power is running low, go to a hospital or a home
  with electricity.

GENERATORS: outdoors only, at least 20 feet (6 m) from windows,
doors, and vents, never in a garage. See the Generator Safety
guide.

AFTER THE POWER RETURNS: check the device and its cords for
damage and check that the settings are still correct before you
rely on it.


DIALYSIS
--------

BEFORE (National Kidney Foundation and CDC):
[ ] Ask your unit for its disaster plan and read it.
[ ] Keep a list of other dialysis units and hospitals near you
    and near where you would evacuate to.
[ ] Keep your phone number and address current with the unit.
[ ] Keep 2 weeks of medicines and the foods for the emergency
    diet.
[ ] Register with your water and power companies for priority
    restoration.
[ ] Wear medical ID.
[ ] Review the emergency diet with your renal dietitian now.
[ ] Home hemodialysis: 2 weeks of supplies, and practice coming
    off the machine in an emergency.
[ ] Peritoneal dialysis: 2 weeks of supplies, and know how to do
    manual exchanges if the cycler cannot run.

IF YOU CANNOT GET TO DIALYSIS:
1. Call your unit. If it is closed or you cannot reach it, call
   the Kidney Community Emergency Response (KCER) hotline,
   866-901-3773, or the National Kidney Foundation,
   855-653-2273, to find an open unit. You can dialyze at a
   different unit.
2. No transport? Contact police or emergency medical services
   for help getting there.
3. Start the emergency diet right away. It is stricter than
   your normal diet, it is meant for a few days, and it is not
   a substitute for dialysis:
   - Fluid: 2 cups (16 oz, about 475 mL) a day
   - Protein: half of what you usually eat
   - No salt and NO SALT SUBSTITUTES (they are made of
     potassium)
   - No high-potassium foods, and no orange juice
4. In a shelter, tell the person in charge that you need
   dialysis.

Why it matters: without dialysis, fluid and potassium build up.
Too much fluid makes it hard to breathe. Too much potassium can
cause dangerous heart rhythms.


EPILEPSY AND SEIZURES
---------------------

- Keep taking anti-seizure medicine on schedule. Missed doses
  and lost sleep are common seizure triggers.
- Keep rescue medicine with the kit if it has been prescribed,
  and make sure someone else knows how to give it.

SEIZURE FIRST AID:
1. Stay with the person. Time the seizure.
2. Ease them to the ground. Move hard or sharp things away.
3. Put something soft under the head. Remove eyeglasses and
   loosen anything tight around the neck.
4. Roll them gently onto one side to keep the airway clear.
5. Check for a medical ID bracelet.
6. Afterward, stay until they are fully alert. Tell them
   calmly what happened.

DO NOT:
- Hold them down
- Put anything in the mouth
- Give mouth-to-mouth during the seizure
- Give food or water until they are fully alert


MENTAL HEALTH CONDITIONS
------------------------

- Treat psychiatric medicines like any other critical medicine:
  keep the buffer, keep the list, do not stop suddenly. A
  prescriber can lower a dose slowly and safely if it has to
  change.
- Write a short crisis plan: your warning signs, what helps,
  who to call.
- Keep sleep, meals, and contact with people as regular as you
  can. Avoid alcohol and drugs.
- US crisis lines: call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis
  Lifeline). Disaster Distress Helpline: call or text
  1-800-985-5990.
- See the Mental Health First Aid in Crisis guide.


AT A SHELTER
------------

- Tell the staff your conditions when you arrive. Say it even
  if you feel fine.
- Ask where medicines can be kept cold, and where you can plug
  in or charge medical equipment. US shelters are expected to
  provide refrigeration for medicines where possible and to
  give power priority to life-sustaining equipment.
- If you need a nurse, oxygen, wound care, or help with daily
  care, ask about a medical needs shelter.
- Keep your medicines and devices with you, in their labeled
  containers.

CHILDREN: send a copy of the list and a spare supply to school
or daycare, and make sure the adults there know the plan.

OLDER ADULTS: many medicines means many chances to miss one. A
filled weekly pill organizer plus the list makes a handover to
a helper possible.


PRACTICE
--------

10-MINUTE DRILL: Set a timer for 10 minutes. Collect every
medicine bottle and device in the house and write the list from
the labels, using the template below. Photograph it. Then ask
out loud: "If the power went out for three days tonight, which
item on this list is in trouble first?" Whatever you answer is
your next task.

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

1. The power has been out for two summer days. Your insulin has
   been in a dead fridge and the kitchen is about 80°F (27°C).
   A neighbor says it is ruined and offers to bury it in a bag
   of ice.

   ANSWER: It is not ruined, and the ice is the real danger.
   Insulin in its original vial or cartridge can sit at 59 to
   86°F for up to 28 days. Freezing destroys it. Keep it in the
   coolest shaded spot or in a cooler where it cannot touch the
   ice, keep using it, check blood sugar more often, and replace
   it when you can.

2. Your father's oxygen concentrator is on its battery, which
   lasts about three hours. The utility estimates eight hours.
   He wants to turn the flow down to make the battery last.

   ANSWER: Do not turn it down. The prescribed flow is the
   dose. Switch to the backup tank now and call the oxygen
   supplier. If the backup will not cover the gap, move him
   somewhere with power, or to a hospital, while there is still
   time on the clock. Call 911 if he becomes short of breath.
   No candles near the oxygen.

3. You evacuated in a hurry. You have four days of blood
   pressure and anti-seizure pills, and the roads home will be
   closed for at least a week. Taking half doses would stretch
   them to eight days.

   ANSWER: Do not halve them. A half dose of seizure medicine
   can mean a seizure. Four days is enough time to solve this
   properly: take the bottles to any open pharmacy today and ask
   for an emergency supply, ask the shelter's medical staff, or
   call your doctor's office. If you are uninsured in a US
   disaster area, ask about EPAP.


QUICK REFERENCE
---------------

RUNNING LOW: don't skip, split, or stop. Go to any pharmacy
with your bottles or list while you still have days left.

INSULIN: fridge 36-46°F. No fridge: OK at 59-86°F up to 28
days. Never freeze. Keep off the ice. Hotter than 86°F: still
use if you must, check sugar more often, replace soon.

LOW BLOOD SUGAR (under 70): 15-20 g fast sugar (4 glucose
tablets, 1/2 cup juice, 1/2 can regular soda). Wait 15 min.
Recheck. Repeat. Passed out: nothing by mouth, glucagon, 911.

DKA: sugar stays 300+, fruity breath, vomiting, hard to
breathe -> 911.

HEART ATTACK: call 911 FIRST. Aspirin if dispatcher says so.
STROKE: BE FAST. Note the time. No aspirin.

SEIZURE: time it, cushion head, roll on side, nothing in the
mouth. Over 5 minutes -> 911.

OXYGEN: never turn the flow down. Backup tank. Flames 5 ft
away. Leave for power before the backup runs out.

DIALYSIS MISSED: fluid 2 cups a day, half protein, no salt,
no salt substitute, no high-potassium food.
KCER 866-901-3773. NKF 855-653-2273.

US CRISIS: call or text 988.
