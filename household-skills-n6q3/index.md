# Household Skills Everyone Should Know

> **Most emergency skills are ordinary household skills done under pressure.** Closing a valve, covering a burning pan, changing a wheel, washing dishes in a bucket. Each takes minutes to learn on a quiet Saturday and is nearly impossible to work out for the first time in the dark with water on the floor.

## Start here

1. **Find your three shutoffs today:** the main water valve, the electrical panel, and the gas meter valve if you have gas. Tag each one. Show every adult and older child in the house.
2. **Look at your fire extinguisher.** Is there one? Is the gauge needle in the green? Can you reach it without passing the stove? Read the label and say PASS out loud.
3. **Put a lid that fits your frying pan where you can grab it.** It is the best tool there is for a pan fire.
4. **Open your car's trunk** and find the spare, the jack and the wheel wrench, and the page of the owner's manual that shows the jack points.
5. **Book a first aid and CPR class.** It is the one skill on this page you cannot learn from reading.

Each skill below is the short version. Where OfflineOwl has a full guide, it is named at the end of the section.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) for:

- **Any fire that is not out within seconds,** or that is spreading or filling the room with smoke. Get everyone out, close doors behind you, call from outside.
- **A gas smell or a hissing sound.** Leave first. Do not touch light switches. Call from outside or from a neighbor's home.
- **Someone who is unresponsive, not breathing normally, or bleeding heavily.**
- **A carbon monoxide alarm,** or headache, dizziness or nausea while something is burning fuel. Get into fresh air first.
- **Downed power lines,** or electrical equipment that is wet, sparking or hot.

No repair is worth an injury. If a job needs you to stand in water near electricity, work on gas piping, or crawl under a raised car, it is a job for a professional.

## Shutting off the water

**Why it matters:** a burst pipe can put a lot of water on the floor every minute. After an earthquake, closing the house valve also keeps the clean water already in your pipes, water heater and toilet tanks from draining away or being contaminated by broken street lines.

1. **Find the main valve** where the water line enters the house: often a basement, crawl space, garage or utility closet, or outside on the wall facing the street.
2. **Close it.** A lever handle (ball valve) takes a quarter turn, until the lever sits crosswise to the pipe. A round wheel handle (gate valve) turns clockwise until it stops.
3. **Open a faucet** on the lowest floor to confirm the flow stops.

Test the valve once a year. If it is rusted open or will not close fully, have it replaced now. Leave the valve at the street to the water company: it is hard to turn and needs a special tool.

**One fixture only?** Sinks and toilets have their own small valve underneath; turn it clockwise. For an overflowing toilet, lift the tank lid, push the rubber flapper down over the hole, then close the valve.

Full detail: the Household Water Shutoff & Safety guide.

## Electricity: the panel and the breakers

**Resetting a tripped breaker.** A tripped breaker sits between ON and OFF, or looks out of line with its neighbors.

1. Unplug or switch off what was running on that circuit.
2. Push the breaker firmly all the way to OFF, then back to ON.
3. **If it trips again, leave it off.** The circuit is overloaded or has a fault. Repeated resetting is how wires overheat. Move appliances to another circuit, and if it still trips, call an electrician.

**Shutting off the whole house** (flooding on the way, sparking, a burning smell from wiring): switch off the individual breakers first, then the main. To restore, main first, then the others one at a time.

**Never touch the panel while standing in water or with wet hands.** If you cannot reach it from a dry spot, stay out and call an electrician or the utility. After a flood, have the system inspected before the power goes back on.

Label the breakers now, in daylight, with one person at the panel and one calling out which lights went off.

## Gas: know where, and know when not to

Shut off natural gas at the meter **only if you smell gas, hear hissing or blowing, or are told to by officials.** Get everyone out first.

- The shutoff is on the pipe that comes up out of the ground to the meter. It needs a wrench: a quarter turn, until the flat tab on the valve sits crosswise to the pipe.
- **Never turn the gas back on yourself.** Only the gas company or a qualified professional should do it, and after a big disaster that can take days. That is the reason not to shut it off "just in case."
- Practice finding the valve and fitting the wrench. Do not actually turn it. Meters differ, so ask your gas company to show you yours.

Full detail: the gas section of the Household Water Shutoff & Safety guide.

## Using a fire extinguisher: PASS

An extinguisher is for a small fire, used by an adult, with an escape route behind them. The U.S. Fire Administration's test is that every one of these is true:

- Others have been alerted and are getting out, and someone is calling the fire department
- The fire is small and contained to one object, such as a wastebasket
- You are not breathing smoke
- You have a clear way out, and you keep your back to it
- You are physically able to use it

If any answer is no, leave and close the door.

- **P**ull the pin.
- **A**im low, at the base of the fire.
- **S**queeze the lever slowly and evenly.
- **S**weep the nozzle from side to side.

If the fire does not go out with one extinguisher, get out and close doors behind you. Home extinguishers are made for small fires and empty quickly.

**Choosing and keeping one:** buy a multipurpose "ABC" unit rated at least 2-A:10-B:C and listed by a testing laboratory. Keep one on each level, near an exit, not beside the stove. Check that the gauge is in the green, the pin and seal are intact and nothing blocks it. Replace it after any use, and replace disposable units within 12 years of the date on the label. Children should not be taught to use extinguishers; their job is to get out.

Reading PASS is not the same as doing it. Many fire departments run short hands-on sessions.

## Kitchen fires

Cooking is the leading cause of home fires and home fire injuries, and unattended cooking is the leading factor.

**Pan fire (grease or oil):**

1. Slide a lid or a baking sheet over the pan.
2. Turn off the burner.
3. Leave it covered until the pan is completely cool. Do not move the pan.

**Never pour water on a grease fire.** It turns to steam and throws burning oil out of the pan. Do not carry the pan to the sink or outdoors.

**Oven fire:** turn off the heat and keep the door closed until it is cool. Only open it once you are sure the fire is completely out, standing to one side as you do.

If the flames do not die down at once, get everyone out, close the door and call 911.

## Changing a tire

**Where matters more than how.** You need level, firm ground well away from traffic. On a narrow shoulder with the flat on the traffic side, creep forward on the flat to a safer spot, or stay belted in the car and call for help.

1. Hazard lights on, parking brake on, car in park (or in gear). Everyone out and away from the road. Block a wheel at the opposite corner with a rock or wedge.
2. **Loosen each wheel nut about half a turn while the tire is still on the ground.** Do not remove them yet.
3. Put the jack under the jack point shown in the owner's manual and raise the car until the flat clears the ground. **Never put any part of your body under a car held up by a jack.**
4. Remove the nuts, pull the wheel straight off, fit the spare, and thread the nuts on by hand.
5. Lower the car until the tire rests on the ground, then tighten the nuts firmly in a star pattern (opposite nut next, not the neighbor).
6. Check the spare's pressure as soon as you can. A compact spare is temporary: its speed and distance limits are printed on its side.

Check the spare's pressure when you check the others, about once a month. Some cars carry a sealant kit and no spare; find out which you have. Full detail: the Vehicle Emergency Kit guide.

## Basic first aid

Take a class. Until you do, three actions cover the emergencies where minutes decide the outcome:

- **Call for help early.** 911, phone on speaker, so the dispatcher can talk you through it.
- **Heavy bleeding:** press hard on the wound with a cloth or your hands and do not let up.
- **Unresponsive and not breathing normally:** push hard and fast in the center of the chest, 100 to 120 times a minute, and send someone for an AED.

For burns, cool the area under cool running water; the Treating Burns, Cuts, and Fractures guide gives the times and details. Keep a stocked first aid kit where everyone can find it.

Full detail: the Basic First Aid Without Power, CPR & Rescue Breathing, and Bleeding Control & Tourniquet Use guides.

## Cooking without power

- **Anything that burns fuel and was made for outdoors stays outdoors:** charcoal and gas grills, camp stoves, generators. Not in the house, the garage, a tent or a camper, and not near an open window. They produce carbon monoxide, which you cannot see or smell.
- **Never heat the house with a gas oven or range.**
- **Canned food is already cooked** and can be eaten cold. Plan a few no-cook meals so that cooking is a choice, not a need.
- **Keep the refrigerator and freezer closed.** See the Food Safety Without Refrigeration guide for what to keep and what to throw out.

Full detail: the Cooking Without Power or Gas guide.

## Washing dishes by hand, safely

When the water supply is uncertain, the aim is to remove food and then kill germs, using as little water as possible. Use three tubs or pots:

1. **Wash** in hot water with dish soap. Scrape plates first so the water lasts.
2. **Rinse** in clean water.
3. **Sanitize:** dip in a mix of 1 tablespoon (15 mL) of regular unscented household bleach (5% to 9% sodium hypochlorite) per gallon (3.8 L) of clean water. This is the CDC mix for dishes and food surfaces that may have touched floodwater.
4. **Air dry** on a clean rack. Do not towel dry.

Use water that is safe to drink for all three steps, or treat it first (see the Water Purification guide). Never mix bleach with ammonia or any other cleaner. Disposable plates are a reasonable choice when water is very short.

## Washing laundry by hand

1. **Sort.** Clothes that touched floodwater, sewage or body fluids are washed separately from everything else, in hot water and detergent.
2. **Soak** in a bucket or tub with a small amount of detergent to loosen the dirt. Too much soap takes more water to rinse out.
3. **Agitate** for a few minutes: knead and squeeze, or work a clean plunger up and down. Scrub dirty spots against each other.
4. **Rinse** in clean water until it runs clear, usually twice.
5. **Wring and dry completely,** in sun and moving air if possible. Clothes that stay damp grow mildew and chill the wearer.

Wash underwear, socks and anything worn next to the skin first, while the water is cleanest. Wash your hands afterward. Pour used wash water out well away from wells, gardens and any water source. See the Hygiene With Limited Water guide.

- **Sewing on a button and closing a seam:** see the Clothing Repair & Sewing Basics guide.

- **Clearing a clogged drain:** a plunger and patience first. Skip chemical drain cleaners when water is short, and never mix them.
- **Testing smoke and carbon monoxide alarms:** press the test button once a month. Replace batteries as the maker directs and the whole alarm after 10 years.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a shutoff race. Start a timer at the kitchen table. Each adult and teen, one at a time, walks (no running) to the main water valve and touches it, then the breaker panel, then the gas meter valve, then the nearest fire extinguisher, and comes back. Write down each time. Nobody turns anything. Then fix what slowed people down: a box in front of the valve, a panel with no labels, a wrench that could not be found, an extinguisher behind the stove.

**What would you do?**

1. *You are frying when the oil in the pan catches fire. Flames are a foot high. The sink is two steps away and there is a fire extinguisher on the wall by the back door.*

   **Answer:** Neither the sink nor, at first, the extinguisher. Water makes burning oil erupt, and carrying the pan spreads the fire and burns you. Slide a lid or baking sheet over the pan, turn off the burner, and leave it covered until it is cold. An extinguisher fired at close range can splash burning oil: NFPA says never to use one on a grease fire from closer than 6 to 8 feet (about 2 m), and only Class K units are rated for cooking oil. It is a fallback, not the first move. If the fire has left the pan, get out, close the door and call 911.

2. *The breaker for the kitchen trips while the space heater, kettle and microwave are all on. You reset it and it trips again a minute later. Your partner says to hold it on, or tape it.*

   **Answer:** Leave it off. A breaker that trips twice is doing its job: the circuit is carrying more than the wiring can safely take, or something on it is faulty. Forcing it on is how wiring fires start. Unplug everything on the circuit, move the heater to a different circuit, and reset once. If it still trips with nothing plugged in, leave it off and call an electrician.

3. *A moderate earthquake has just stopped. Nothing is broken that you can see, and nothing smells. Your teenager, who did the shutoff drill last month, calls out that she will go and turn off the gas and the water.*

   **Answer:** Water yes, gas no. Closing the main water valve protects the clean water in your tank and pipes if street mains are broken, and you can reopen it yourself. Gas is shut off only when you smell it, hear it or see a damaged line, because only the utility can safely restore it and that may take days. Have her sniff around the meter and the appliances instead, and check again after aftershocks.
