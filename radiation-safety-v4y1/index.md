# Radiation Safety Fundamentals

> You can't see, smell, or taste radiation. In almost every radiation emergency (a nuclear explosion, a "dirty bomb," or a nuclear power plant accident) the first protective step is the same: **Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.**

## Do This Now

1. **Get inside** the nearest sturdy building. Brick or concrete is best. A car is not a shelter.
2. **Go to the basement or the center of the building**, away from windows, outside walls, and the roof. In a tall building, go to the center of a middle floor.
3. **If you were outside, take off your outer layer of clothing** before joining others (it can remove up to 90% of radioactive material), bag it, and wash with soap and water.
4. **Close windows, doors, and fireplace dampers.** Turn off fans, air conditioners, and forced-air heating that pull in outside air.
5. **Stay inside for at least 24 hours** unless officials tell you otherwise or there is an immediate danger like fire or collapse.
6. **Stay tuned** to a battery or hand-crank radio, phone alerts, or TV, and follow official instructions.
7. **Bring pets inside.** Don't go out to collect family; schools and care facilities have their own shelter plans.

**Call 911** (or your local emergency number) for serious injuries, burns, or fire.

## Key Principles: Time, Distance, Shielding

**TIME:** less time near radioactive material means a lower dose.
- Get inside quickly and stay there.
- Fallout loses strength fast: roughly 90% of its radiation is gone 7 hours after an explosion, and 99% after about 2 days.

**DISTANCE:** more distance means a lower dose.
- For a single source, such as a dropped radioactive object, doubling your distance cuts the dose to about one-quarter.
- Fallout settles on roofs and the ground, so the middle of a building or a basement keeps you farther from it.

**SHIELDING:** more heavy material between you and the source means a lower dose.
- Concrete, brick, stone, and earth shield best.
- Wood, drywall, and thin metal shield little, but many layers together (rooms in the center of a large building) still help.

## Types of Radiation Emergencies

| Event | Main danger | What to do |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| Nuclear explosion | Blast, heat, then fallout over a wide area | Get inside a sturdy building within minutes, go to the basement or center, stay at least 24 hours |
| Dirty bomb (radiological dispersal device) | The explosion; radioactive dust nearby | Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned; remove outer clothing and wash if you were near it |
| Nuclear power plant accident | Radioactive release carried by wind | Follow official orders to shelter or evacuate; KI only if officials say so |
| Hidden radioactive source or transport accident | Exposure if you are close | Keep away; don't touch cargo or objects; report it to 911 or law enforcement |

## Shelter in Place

### Where to Shelter

**Best protection:**
- Basement
- Center of a large brick or concrete building
- Center of the middle floors of a tall building (not the top floor, where fallout lands on the roof, and not the ground floor)
- Underground parking garage, subway, or tunnel

**Poor protection:**
- Vehicles
- Single-story wood-frame houses without a basement
- Rooms by windows or outside walls

**Protection factor (PF)** means how much a spot cuts your dose. PF 10 means one-tenth of the dose outside. FEMA counts PF 10 or more as adequate. Examples from FEMA planning guidance:

| Location | About PF |
|----------|----------|
| Wood-frame house, main floor | 2-3 |
| Wood-frame house, basement | 10 |
| Brick-veneer house, basement | 20 |
| Center of middle floors, large office or apartment building | 50-100 |
| Underground levels of a large building | 200 |

**You don't need to seal the room with plastic for radiation.** Buildings don't have to be airtight to protect against fallout; mass and distance do the work. (Sealed, above-ground rooms are for chemical releases. See the Sealing a Room guide.)

### How Long

- Stay inside for at least 24 hours unless officials say otherwise.
- Radiation from fallout is highest at first and drops quickly.
- Officials may tell you to stay longer or to evacuate. If you evacuate, don't return until they say it's safe.

## Decontamination

### If You Were Outside

1. **Take off your outer layer of clothing** carefully, without shaking it (removes up to 90% of radioactive material).
2. **Seal it in a plastic bag**, away from people and pets.
3. **Shower with warm water and lots of soap.** Wash hair with shampoo or soap.
4. **Do not use conditioner.** It makes radioactive material stick to hair.
5. **Do not scald, scrub, or scratch your skin.** Keep cuts covered.
6. **No shower?** Wash hands, face, and uncovered skin at a sink, or use a moist wipe or wet cloth.
7. **Gently blow your nose and wipe your eyelids, eyelashes, and ears.**
8. **Put on clean clothes** that were stored inside.

Tap water is fine for washing even if officials say not to drink it.

### What NOT to Do

- Don't use conditioner (it binds radioactive material to hair).
- Don't scrub or scald your skin (broken skin lets contamination in).
- Don't use household cleaning wipes on skin.
- Don't rely on hand sanitizer; it doesn't remove radioactive material.
- Don't touch your eyes, nose, or mouth until you've washed.

## Potassium Iodide (KI)

### What It Does

- Helps block the thyroid from absorbing **radioactive iodine**.
- Protects **only the thyroid**. It does not protect other parts of the body.
- Does **not** protect against other radioactive materials or fallout on the ground.
- Works best taken within 24 hours before or up to 4 hours after exposure. One dose protects for about 24 hours; officials will say if more doses are needed.

### Who Should Take It

- Only people told to by public health or emergency officials, or a doctor.
- Infants, children, and pregnant or breastfeeding women benefit most.
- Adults over 40 should take KI only when officials recommend it.
- If you live within about 10 miles of a nuclear power plant, your state may offer KI ahead of time. Ask your local emergency management or health department.

### Limitations and Risks

- Never take more than directed. Too much can cause severe illness or death.
- Side effects can include stomach upset, rash, and swollen salivary glands.
- Table salt and iodine-rich foods are **not** substitutes.

## Signs of Radiation Sickness

Radiation sickness only happens after a large dose in a short time. Early signs can start within minutes to days:
- Nausea and vomiting
- Headache
- Diarrhea

Skin redness or burns may show up within hours or days later. People often feel better for a while, then get sick again (tiredness, fever, loss of appetite).

**Get medical care as soon as officials say it's safe to travel.** Call 911 for emergencies.

---

## Special Considerations

- **Children and pregnant women** are more sensitive to radiation. Give them the most protected spot and help children wash.
- **Pets:** bring them inside. If they were outside, brush their coat, then wash them with shampoo or soap and water. Wash your hands afterward.
- **People with disabilities or medical needs:** keep medications, glasses, hearing aids, and spare batteries for medical devices with you in the shelter spot.
- **Food and water:** anything that was inside is safe. Wipe sealed containers from outside with a damp cloth. Don't eat garden produce or uncovered food from outside until officials say it's safe.
