Mental Health First Aid in Crisis

The Invisible Wounds - Disasters and emergencies don't just injure bodies—they affect minds. Understanding how to support mental health during crisis is as important as physical first aid.

Normal Reactions to Abnormal Situations

It's important to understand: Strong emotional reactions to crisis are normal, not weakness.

Common Stress Reactions

Physical:

  • Fatigue
  • Sleep problems
  • Changes in appetite
  • Headaches
  • Stomach problems
  • Racing heart
  • Muscle tension

Emotional:

  • Fear and anxiety
  • Grief and sadness
  • Anger and irritability
  • Guilt
  • Numbness
  • Mood swings

Cognitive:

  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Memory problems
  • Confusion
  • Recurring thoughts of event
  • Difficulty making decisions

Behavioral:

  • Social withdrawal
  • Changes in routine
  • Increased use of substances
  • Hypervigilance
  • Avoiding reminders

These reactions are typically temporary and diminish over time with support.

Psychological First Aid (PFA)

The Five Core Actions

1. Safety

  • Ensure physical safety
  • Create sense of security
  • Remove from ongoing danger
  • Meet basic needs (food, water, shelter)

2. Calm

  • Stay calm yourself
  • Speak quietly and calmly
  • Provide accurate information
  • Reduce stimulation if helpful
  • Help with grounding techniques

3. Self-efficacy

  • Help people help themselves
  • Involve them in their own care
  • Give simple, achievable tasks
  • Respect their capabilities
  • Support their decisions

4. Connection

  • Connect with family/friends
  • Help access support systems
  • Facilitate group support
  • Share information about resources

5. Hope

  • Remind them that reactions are normal
  • Point out their strengths
  • Help them identify coping strategies
  • Focus on what they can control
  • Acknowledge their resilience

What to Say

Helpful phrases:

  • "You're safe now."
  • "I'm here with you."
  • "This is a normal reaction to what you've been through."
  • "You don't have to talk about it if you don't want to."
  • "What do you need right now?"
  • "Is there someone I can help you contact?"
  • "You're doing the best you can."
  • "It's okay to feel this way."

Avoid saying:

  • "I know how you feel."
  • "You should be grateful you survived."
  • "It could have been worse."
  • "You need to stay strong."
  • "Just get over it."
  • "Everything happens for a reason."
  • "At least..."

Grounding Techniques

When someone is overwhelmed, panicking, or dissociating:

5-4-3-2-1 Technique

Guide them through:

  • 5 things you can SEE
  • 4 things you can TOUCH
  • 3 things you can HEAR
  • 2 things you can SMELL
  • 1 thing you can TASTE

Deep Breathing

Box breathing:

  1. Breathe in for 4 counts
  2. Hold for 4 counts
  3. Breathe out for 4 counts
  4. Hold for 4 counts
  5. Repeat

Belly breathing:

  1. Place hand on belly
  2. Breathe in slowly through nose
  3. Feel belly expand
  4. Exhale slowly through mouth
  5. Feel belly fall
  6. Repeat 5-10 times

Physical Grounding

  • Feel feet firmly on ground
  • Press palms together
  • Hold something cold or warm
  • Squeeze a stress ball
  • Splash cold water on face
  • Take a short walk

Supporting Children

Age-Specific Reactions

Preschool (under 6):

  • Regression (thumb sucking, bedwetting)
  • Clinginess
  • Fear of dark/being alone
  • Changes in eating/sleeping
  • Repetitive play about event

School-age (6-12):

  • Worry about safety of family
  • Guilt or responsibility
  • Physical complaints
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Behavior changes
  • Loss of interest in activities

Teens (13-18):

  • Risk-taking behavior
  • Withdrawal from family
  • Physical complaints
  • Sleep/appetite changes
  • Substance use
  • Anger/irritability
  • Academic problems

Supporting Children

Do:

  • Maintain routines as much as possible
  • Provide physical comfort
  • Validate their feelings
  • Give honest, age-appropriate information
  • Let them express feelings through play/art
  • Limit media exposure
  • Reassure safety
  • Be available and patient
  • Model healthy coping

Recognizing Crisis States

Panic Attack

Signs:

  • Racing heart
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Chest tightness
  • Sweating
  • Trembling
  • Feeling of doom
  • Fear of dying

Response:

  1. Stay calm and present
  2. Speak in slow, calm voice
  3. Guide through breathing
  4. Remind them it will pass
  5. Don't minimize their experience
  6. Stay until it passes

What to say:

  • "This is a panic attack. It's scary but it will pass."
  • "You're safe. I'm here with you."
  • "Try to breathe with me—in... out..."

Acute Stress/Shock

Signs:

  • Dazed appearance
  • Disorientation
  • Not responding appropriately
  • Repetitive behaviors
  • Extreme agitation or complete stillness

Response:

  1. Ensure safety
  2. Speak calmly and simply
  3. Orient to present (name, location, time)
  4. Meet basic needs
  5. Stay with them
  6. Don't force them to talk about event

Suicide Risk Assessment

Warning Signs

Direct signs:

  • Talking about wanting to die
  • Looking for ways to kill oneself
  • Talking about feeling hopeless
  • Talking about being a burden

Behavioral signs:

  • Giving away possessions
  • Saying goodbye
  • Withdrawing from people
  • Increased substance use
  • Reckless behavior
  • Sudden calmness after depression

How to Ask

It's okay—and important—to ask directly.

Questions:

  • "Are you thinking about hurting yourself?"
  • "Are you thinking about suicide?"
  • "Do you have a plan?"

Asking about suicide does NOT increase risk.

If Someone is Suicidal

  1. Don't leave them alone
  2. Remove access to means (weapons, medications, etc.)
  3. Listen without judgment
  4. Don't promise secrecy
  5. Get help:
    • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text)
    • Crisis Text Line: Text HOME to 741741
    • Emergency services if immediate danger

Self-Care for Helpers

You cannot pour from an empty cup.

Signs of Caregiver Burnout

  • Exhaustion
  • Feeling numb or detached
  • Irritability
  • Sleep problems
  • Difficulty caring about others
  • Physical symptoms
  • Guilt about taking breaks

Preventing Burnout

During crisis:

  • Take breaks when possible
  • Share the load with others
  • Accept your limitations
  • Eat and hydrate
  • Sleep when you can
  • Talk to someone about your experiences

After crisis:

  • Debrief with others
  • Allow yourself to process
  • Reduce other stressors
  • Return to normal activities gradually
  • Seek support if needed

Crisis Resources

Keep these numbers accessible:

988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: Call or text 988
Crisis Text Line: Text HOME to 741741
Disaster Distress Helpline: 1-800-985-5990
Veterans Crisis Line: 1-800-273-8255, Press 1
SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357

Key Takeaways

  1. Strong reactions are normal responses to abnormal situations
  2. Presence matters - Being there is powerful
  3. Listen without judgment - Don't try to fix everything
  4. Meet basic needs - Safety, food, water, rest
  5. Connect people with support systems
  6. Take care of yourself - You can't help others if depleted
  7. Know when to get help - Some situations need professionals
  8. Hope is essential - People are resilient
