# Mental Health First Aid in Crisis

> **Most people do not need a therapist after a disaster. They need safety, a calm person nearby, practical help, and their own people.** Giving those four things has a name, psychological first aid, and anyone can do it. This guide shows how, and how to spot the few situations that need professional help right now.

## Do this now

For someone who is overwhelmed, panicking, or shut down:

1. **Check for danger and injuries first.** Move away from hazards. Physical first aid and 911 come before anything in this guide.
2. **Stay close and stay calm.** Say who you are and that you are there to help. Speak slowly and quietly. Ask before you touch.
3. **Meet one basic need.** Water, a blanket, a place to sit, a phone to reach family. Practical help is calming in itself.
4. **Listen, and do not push.** Let them talk if they want to. Do not make them tell the story. Silence is fine.
5. **If they are shaking, spaced out, or cannot catch their breath:** have them press their feet into the floor, breathe slowly with you, and name things they can see and hear around them.
6. **Link them up.** Help them reach family or friends, give accurate information, and do not leave a very distressed person alone.

## Get emergency help if

**Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if someone:**

- Has hurt themselves, or is about to
- Is threatening to hurt someone else
- Is so upset or confused that they cannot keep themselves or their children safe
- Has chest pain, trouble breathing, or has fainted. A panic attack and a heart attack can look alike. If there is any doubt, treat it as medical.

**In the US, call or text 988** (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) for thoughts of suicide, self-harm, or any mental health or substance use crisis. It is free, confidential, and open 24 hours a day. You can call for yourself or about someone you are worried about.

| Line (US) | How to reach it |
|-----------|-----------------|
| 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline | Call or text 988 |
| Veterans Crisis Line | Call 988 then press 1, or text 838255 |
| Disaster Distress Helpline | Call or text 1-800-985-5990 (Spanish: press 2) |
| Crisis Text Line | Text HOME to 741741 |

Outside the US, crisis lines have different numbers. Crisis text lines: 85258 in the UK, 686868 in Canada, 50808 in Ireland. Find your country's number now and write it in the Quick Reference card at the end.

**If phones are down:** stay with the person, move anything they could use to hurt themselves out of reach, and get them to medical staff at a shelter, clinic, or hospital.

## What is normal after a disaster

Strong reactions to a frightening event are the mind and body doing their job. They are not weakness and they are not illness.

| Where it shows | Common reactions |
|----------------|------------------|
| Body | Racing heart, headaches, stomach pain, exhaustion, being easily startled |
| Feelings | Fear, anxiety, sadness, anger, guilt, numbness |
| Thinking | Trouble concentrating, replaying what happened, trouble making decisions |
| Behavior | Poor sleep, changes in appetite, pulling away from people, avoiding reminders |

For most people these ease with time, support, and a return to routine. It becomes a problem to get help for when reactions last longer than a month, keep getting worse, or get in the way of daily life. See **When to get professional help** below.

## Psychological first aid: Look, Listen, Link

The World Health Organization teaches psychological first aid (PFA) as three actions.

**Look.** Check for safety. Check for people with urgent basic needs. Look for people in serious distress: someone who cannot move or respond, someone who is not caring for their children, someone alone.

**Listen.** Approach, introduce yourself, and ask what they need and what worries them most. Listen to the answer. Help them feel calm.

**Link.** Help people meet basic needs and reach services. Give accurate information. Reconnect them with family, friends, and community. Help them work out their own next step.

**What PFA is not.** It is not counseling, and it is not getting people to talk through what happened. Asking someone to recount a traumatic event in detail soon afterward, often called debriefing, has been found ineffective and is not recommended. Being available matters. Pushing does not help.

**Why this works.** Studies of disaster recovery keep finding the same five ingredients: people recover better when they feel **safe**, **calm**, **connected** to others, **hopeful**, and **able to help themselves**. Each thing you do should feed one of those. Giving someone a small job, such as looking after the water or reading to the children, is often worth more than sympathy, because it restores the feeling of being capable.

### What to say and what to avoid

| Say and do | Avoid |
|------------|-------|
| "I'm here. I'm going to stay with you." | "I know how you feel." |
| "You're safe here." (only if it is true) | "You're lucky you survived." "It could have been worse." |
| "What do you need most right now?" | "You need to talk about it." |
| "I'm so sorry. That sounds very hard." | "You shouldn't feel that way." "Be strong." |
| "I don't know, but I'll try to find out." | Guesses, rumors, and promises you cannot keep |
| Notice what they have done well: "You got your kids out." | Taking over things they can do themselves |
| Allow silence | Talking about your own troubles |

Keep what you are told private unless someone is in danger. Respect that people of different ages, cultures, and faiths show distress and accept comfort differently.

## Calming techniques

These work for the person in front of you and for yourself. They are worth learning before you need them, because they are hard to remember mid-panic.

**Grounding.** When someone feels unreal, numb, or far away:

1. Put both feet flat on the floor and feel them there.
2. Tap your fingers or hands on your lap.
3. Look around and name ordinary things, out loud: 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste. The counting does not matter. The point is to pull attention out of the head and into the room.

**Slow breathing.** Breathe in through the nose and out through the mouth, slowly, counting to four each way. Do it with the person, not at them. A few minutes is enough.

**Panic attacks.** A panic attack is a sudden wave of fear with a pounding heart, sweating, trembling, trouble breathing, dizziness, tingling hands, chest pain, and often a feeling of doom. It is not life-threatening. It can last from a few minutes to an hour, sometimes longer, and then it passes. Stay with the person, keep your voice low, breathe slowly together, and say plainly: "This is frightening, and it will pass. I'm staying." Because the symptoms overlap with a heart attack, call 911 if the person has chest pain and has never had a panic attack, has heart disease, or is not improving.

## If someone may be thinking about suicide

**Warning signs.** Take these seriously, especially if they are new or increasing:

- Talking about wanting to die, about great guilt or shame, or about being a burden
- Feeling empty, hopeless, trapped, or in unbearable pain
- Making a plan, saying goodbye, giving away important things
- Withdrawing from people, taking dangerous risks, extreme mood swings
- Using alcohol or drugs more often

**Five steps from the US National Institute of Mental Health:**

1. **Ask.** "Are you thinking about suicide?" Use the word. Studies show that asking does not put the idea in someone's head or make it more likely.
2. **Be there.** Listen without judging, arguing, or rushing to fix it.
3. **Help keep them safe.** Ask whether they have thought about how. Put distance between them and anything they could use: lock away or remove firearms and medicines, or have someone else hold them. Do not leave them alone if the danger feels close.
4. **Help them connect.** Call or text 988 together, or reach a doctor, counselor, faith leader, or trusted family member.
5. **Follow up.** Check in the next day and the day after. Staying in touch makes a difference.

Do not promise to keep it secret. You can say: "I care about you too much to keep this to myself."

Suicidal crises pass. Getting someone safely through the next few hours and connected to help is what matters, and it is something an ordinary person can do.

## Children and teens

Children take their cue from the adults around them. A calm, steady adult is the best protection a child has.

| Age | What you may see | What helps |
|-----|------------------|------------|
| Babies and toddlers | More crying and fussing, wanting to be held | Warmth, cuddles, quiet, regular feeding and sleep |
| Preschool (about 3 to 6) | Clinging, tantrums, sleep trouble, going back to bedwetting or thumb-sucking, acting out the event in play | Extra time and closeness, simple honest answers without frightening detail, "you are safe" said often, routines |
| School age (about 7 to 12) | Fear it will happen again, sadness or anger, stomachaches and headaches, trouble concentrating, believing it was their fault | Facts, correcting rumors, saying clearly it was not their fault, time to play, regular routines |
| Teens | Withdrawal, anger, risk-taking, alcohol or drug use, feeling "nothing," arguing | Your time, real information, clear rules, a useful job to do, listening without judgment |

**Do:**

- Let them talk, draw, or play about what happened, in their own time.
- Answer questions truthfully in words they understand.
- Keep meals, bedtime, and school as regular as you can.
- Limit how much news and social media coverage of the event they see.
- Keep young children with their caregivers and siblings.
- Offer small choices, so they feel some control.

**Do not:**

- Expect them to be brave or tough
- Make them talk before they are ready
- Get angry at strong emotions or at going back to younger behavior
- Make promises you cannot keep, such as "this will never happen again"

Most children's reactions fade with time. Talk to a doctor, school counselor, or child psychologist if your child is still very anxious, sad, or angry after two to four weeks, if things are getting worse, or if school or friendships are suffering. A teenager who talks about suicide needs the same five steps as an adult, today.

See also the Normalcy Kids guide.

## Older adults, disabilities, and existing conditions

- **Existing mental health conditions:** keep medicines going on schedule. Do not stop psychiatric medicine suddenly or without a prescriber's help. See the Managing Chronic Conditions guide.
- **Dementia:** unfamiliar places increase confusion and wandering. Stay with the person, find a quiet corner, name the feeling you see ("you seem worried"), and reassure.
- **People who are deaf, blind, or use communication aids** can be cut off from information, which feeds fear. Make sure they get the same facts as everyone else.
- **Alcohol and drugs** make sleep, mood, and judgment worse after a disaster. In the US, the SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) gives free, confidential treatment referrals around the clock.

## Looking after yourself

Helpers and caregivers absorb what they see and hear. You cannot keep helping if you run yourself down.

A daily check for anyone doing the helping:

- [ ] I ate a meal and drank water today
- [ ] I slept, even in short stretches
- [ ] I took a real break and handed over to someone else
- [ ] I checked in with a partner or friend about how I am doing
- [ ] I kept alcohol and drugs out of my coping plan
- [ ] I limited how much news I took in
- [ ] I reminded myself that I cannot fix everything, and that doing what I can is enough

Signs you need to step back: you cannot sleep, you feel numb or irritable with the people you are helping, you are drinking more, or you cannot stop thinking about what you saw. The crisis lines above are for helpers too.

## When to get professional help

See a doctor or mental health professional, for yourself or someone else, if any of these apply:

- Distress has lasted more than a month, or is getting worse instead of better
- It gets in the way of work, school, caring for family, or relationships
- Frightening flashbacks or nightmares, avoiding anything that is a reminder, feeling constantly on edge
- Withdrawing from family and friends
- Relying on alcohol or drugs to cope
- Any thoughts of suicide or self-harm: call or text 988 now, do not wait for an appointment

Getting help early is not a sign that something is wrong with you. Treatment for trauma works.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Two parts, five minutes each. First, fill in the crisis card below with your own country's numbers and your own two people, save 988 (or your local line) and the Disaster Distress Helpline in every phone in the house, and tape a copy inside your emergency kit. Second, sit down with your household and walk through the grounding exercise once, out loud: feet on the floor, slow breaths, 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear. Children enjoy it as a game, and it means that on a bad day you can say "let's do the five things" and everyone knows what you mean.

**What would you do?**

1. *The morning after a flood, a neighbor is sitting on a cot in the shelter, shaking, staring at the floor, not answering questions. A volunteer tells you, "Get her to talk about it. She needs to get it out."*

   **Answer:** Do not make her talk. Pushing someone to relive the event does not help, and it is the opposite of what psychological first aid asks of you. Sit near her, say who you are, offer water or a blanket, and tell her she is safe here. If she seems far away, ask her to feel her feet on the floor and name a few things she can see. Ask if there is anyone she wants to reach. Listen if she chooses to talk. If she still cannot respond or look after herself, bring the shelter's medical or mental health staff to her and do not leave her alone.

2. *Two weeks after a wildfire took the family home, your nineteen-year-old brother gives his guitar to a friend and says, "Everyone would be better off without me." When you look worried, he says, "Forget it. Don't tell Mom."*

   **Answer:** Do not forget it and do not promise secrecy. Talking about being a burden and giving away something that matters are both warning signs. Ask him directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Asking will not plant the idea. Stay with him, listen, and call or text 988 together. Ask whether he has thought about how, and get anything dangerous out of the house or locked up. Bring in a parent or another adult he trusts, and check on him again tomorrow.

3. *Your five-year-old has wet the bed four nights running since the tornado, insists on sleeping in your room, and keeps crashing toy cars into a block "house." Your partner wants to tell him to stop the game and be a big boy.*

   **Answer:** All three behaviors are normal for his age after a scare, and the play is how he is working through it. Do not shame him or stop the game. Give extra closeness, keep bedtime routines steady, tell him often that he is safe and that the storm was nobody's fault, and keep storm coverage off the screen. Expect it to ease over a few weeks. If he is still this unsettled after two to four weeks, or it gets worse, talk to his doctor.
