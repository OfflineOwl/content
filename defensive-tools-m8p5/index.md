# Defensive Tools

> **The tools that protect people are mostly boring ones.** A light, a loud noise, a charged phone, a locked door and a working smoke alarm prevent far more harm than anything sold as a weapon. None of them needs a permit, and you can set all of them up today.

## Start here

1. **Put a flashlight or headlamp by every bed** and one by the main door. Switch each one on to check it.
2. **Clip a whistle to each key ring, go bag and child's backpack.**
3. **Charge your phone and a power bank,** and learn your phone's emergency-call shortcut so you can use it without looking.
4. **Press the test button on every smoke alarm and carbon monoxide (CO) alarm.** Replace any that stay silent.
5. **Walk to every outside door and ground-floor window and lock it.** Fix or report any lock that doesn't work.
6. **Decide now that your plan is to leave and call for help,** not to stand and face a threat. Every tool in this guide exists to buy time for that.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **Someone is threatening you, following you, or trying to get into your home.** Get away or get behind a locked door first, then call.
- **There is a fire that isn't out in seconds.** Get everyone out and call from outside.
- **A CO alarm sounds, or people in the home get a headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion together.** Get into fresh air first, then call.
- **Someone exposed to pepper spray or another irritant has trouble breathing that isn't easing in fresh air,** or eye pain that continues after rinsing.

For questions about an irritant-spray exposure that is not an emergency, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 (US, free, 24 hours). Outside the US, use your local emergency number or poison center.

## What this guide covers

This guide covers safety tools that mainstream emergency agencies recommend and that are legal almost everywhere: light, noise, communication, locks, alarms and first aid supplies. They work by helping you see trouble early, call attention, get help, and get out.

Weapons are outside this guide's scope. See "Pepper spray" and "Weapons" below for why, and for what to check before you buy anything.

| Tool | What it does for you | Its limit |
|------|----------------------|-----------|
| Flashlight or headlamp | Lets you see hazards and be seen; signals for help | Dead batteries |
| Whistle | Calls for help farther and longer than your voice can | Someone has to hear it |
| Personal alarm | Draws attention and buys seconds to get away | Stops no one by itself |
| Phone and power bank | Reaches 911, family and emergency alerts | Needs charge and signal |
| Door and window locks | Slow an intruder and give you time to call | Only work when locked |
| Smoke and CO alarms | Wake you while you can still get out | Need testing and replacing |
| First aid kit | Treats the injuries that actually happen | Needs someone who knows how |
| Fire extinguisher | Puts out a small fire | Small fires only, with your exit behind you |

## Light

Ready.gov's power outage advice is one flashlight for every member of the household, and its basic emergency kit lists a flashlight and extra batteries. In an emergency a light does four jobs:

- **It prevents falls,** a common injury when the lights are out.
- **It lets you look before you go.** Check a noise, a stairwell or a parking lot from where you are before walking into it.
- **It makes you visible** to drivers and rescuers.
- **It signals.** A light waved or flashed from a window shows searchers where you are.

A headlamp keeps both hands free. Keep lights where you will need them: by the bed, by the door, in the car, in your bag. The Off-Grid Lighting Options guide covers choosing lights and planning batteries.

## Noise: whistles and personal alarms

**A whistle** is on Ready.gov's basic kit list "to signal for help," and it costs almost nothing. It is loud, it carries, and you can keep blowing it long after your voice gives out. If you are trapped under debris, Ready.gov says to use a whistle instead of shouting, with your mouth covered, so you breathe in less dust. Three blasts, repeated, is widely understood as a call for help.

**A personal alarm** is a small battery siren set off by a button or pull-pin. Its job is to turn heads and to give you a few seconds to move toward people and light. Treat it that way:

- Carry it where one hand can reach it, not at the bottom of a bag.
- Set it off once at home so you and your household know the sound.
- Check the battery when you check your smoke alarms.
- When it sounds, move. Don't stand and wait to see whether it worked.

Noise helps only if someone is near enough to hear it and willing to respond, so pair it with a call to 911.

## Phone and power

Your phone is the tool that brings trained help, so protect its battery and your access to it.

- **Keep it charged,** and keep a charged power bank and cable in your bag and car. Ready.gov lists a cell phone with chargers and a backup battery as a basic kit item.
- **Turn on emergency alerts** so warnings reach you. See the Understanding Sirens and Alert Systems guide.
- **When you call 911, say where you are first.** If the call drops, they still know where to send help.
- **Texting 911 works in some areas but not all.** Call if you can.
- **Fill in your phone's emergency contacts and medical information,** which responders can read from the lock screen on most phones.
- **Look up while you walk.** A phone in front of your face hides exactly the things the other tools are for. See the Situational Awareness guide.

## Doors, windows and locks

A lock works only when it is locked. Most of the value comes from habit, not hardware.

- **Lock every outside door and reachable window,** including when you are home and at night.
- **Fit a deadbolt on each outside door** if there isn't one, and make sure the door frame it locks into is solid. The Home Hardening Basics guide has the details.
- **Don't open the door to find out who it is.** Use a door viewer, a window, a camera or your voice through the closed door.
- **Light the outside of your doors.**
- **Never trade away your fire escape.** Every lock on your way out must open from the inside without a key, and security bars on windows need a quick-release that everyone in the home can work. The U.S. Fire Administration says to make sure doors and windows are not blocked and to know two ways out of every room.

If you rent, ask the landlord in writing to repair broken locks. What a landlord must provide varies by place.

## Alarms that wake you

Fire and carbon monoxide kill people in their sleep. An alarm is the only tool here that can wake you in time to get out.

**Smoke alarms.** The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) found that from 2018 to 2022 nearly three out of five home fire deaths were in homes with no smoke alarms or alarms that did not work, and that the death rate per fire is about 60 percent lower in homes with working alarms. Missing, dead or disconnected power, usually batteries, was the most common reason alarms failed to sound.

- Put smoke alarms inside and outside each bedroom and sleeping area, and on every level of the home.
- Test them monthly.
- Replace the whole alarm every 10 years, and replace 9-volt batteries at least once a year.
- Interconnected alarms are best: when one sounds, they all sound.
- People who are deaf or hard of hearing can get alarms with strobe lights or vibrating pads.

**CO alarms.** Carbon monoxide has no smell or color, and the Consumer Product Safety Commission counts more than 200 accidental non-fire CO deaths in the US each year. Install battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas. Symptoms are headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, sleepiness and confusion. If you suspect CO, get outside to fresh air, then call 911.

## First aid kit and fire extinguisher

**First aid kit.** Keep one at home and one in the car, and learn to use what is in it. The Basic First Aid and Bleeding Control guides cover the skills. A kit with no training is mostly a box.

**Fire extinguisher.** A multipurpose "A-B-C" extinguisher handles ordinary materials, flammable liquids and electrical fires. The U.S. Fire Administration says to use one only when everyone has been alerted, 911 has been called, the fire is small, and you have a clear way out behind you. Otherwise leave. To use it, remember **PASS**:

1. **Pull** the pin.
2. **Aim** low, at the base of the fire.
3. **Squeeze** the lever slowly and evenly.
4. **Sweep** the nozzle from side to side.

## Pepper spray

Pepper spray is an irritant aerosol sold for personal protection. Some people choose to carry it. If you are considering it, know these points first:

- **The law varies by place.** In the US, rules on who may buy it, canister size, strength and where you may carry it differ by state and city. In some countries, including the United Kingdom, it is illegal for the public to have at all. Check local law before you buy it, carry it or travel with it. This guide does not give legal advice.
- **It can't go in a carry-on bag.** US air travel rules allow one container of up to 4 fluid ounces (118 ml) with a safety mechanism in checked baggage only, and some airlines ban it entirely.
- **It affects whoever breathes it,** including you and bystanders if the wind shifts or you are indoors or in a car. People with asthma or other lung disease are at higher risk of serious breathing trouble.
- **It needs training and safe storage.** Learn from a qualified instructor, follow the label, and store it where children can't reach it.
- **It is not a substitute for leaving.** Nothing in a canister is a reason to stay near a threat you could walk away from.

**If someone is sprayed by accident:** get them into fresh air. Don't rub the eyes. Remove contact lenses and don't put them back in. Rinse the eyes with plain water for 10 to 15 minutes and wash skin with soap and water. Take off contaminated clothing, cutting it off if it would otherwise be pulled over the head. The CDC says the effects of irritant agents usually last only 15 to 30 minutes once the person is away from the source and cleaned off. Get medical care if breathing trouble or eye pain continues.

## Weapons

Firearms and other weapons are outside the scope of this guide. Owning, carrying and using them is governed by local law, which differs greatly from place to place, and they demand proper training and secure storage. Take those questions to your local law and to qualified, certified instructors. This guide also does not cover improvised weapons.

## What keeps people safe

In order, and almost always in this order:

1. **Avoid.** Don't go where trouble is.
2. **Notice early.** See the Situational Awareness guide.
3. **Leave.** Distance solves most problems.
4. **Talk it down only if you can't leave.** See the Personal Safety and De-Escalation guide.
5. **Call for help.**

## People who need extra thought

- **Children:** give each child a whistle and a light, and practice when to use them. Keep irritant sprays and extinguishers out of reach.
- **Older adults and people who live alone:** a personal alarm or medical alert device, and lights along the path from bed to bathroom.
- **People who are deaf or hard of hearing:** strobe or bed-shaker smoke and CO alarms, and find out whether your area supports texting 911.
- **People with limited mobility or grip:** check that you can work every lock, latch and alarm yourself.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a reach test tonight. Lie on your bed with your eyes closed and, without getting up, put your hand on a working light and your phone. Then walk the house: press the test button on each smoke and CO alarm, lock and unlock every outside door from the inside, and blow your whistle once outdoors so everyone in the household knows the sound. Write down anything that failed and fix it this week.

**What would you do?**

1. *It is 2 a.m. and something crashes in the back yard. You have a bright flashlight by the bed, and you are wide awake and annoyed.*

   **Answer:** Stay inside. The flashlight is for looking, not for going out to meet whatever made the noise. Check that the doors are locked, turn on outside lights, and look from a window. If someone is in the yard and won't leave, or is trying a door, call 911 and stay behind the locked door with your household. Most night noises are animals, wind or neighbors, and you can confirm that in daylight.

2. *A man across a parking lot starts shouting at you and walking your way. You have pepper spray on your key ring. Your car is three steps behind you.*

   **Answer:** Get in the car, lock the doors and drive away. Call 911 if he follows. The spray does not change the best move, which is to put distance and a locked door between you. Standing your ground to use it means waiting for him to get close, in wind you haven't checked, with a tool that may not stop him.

3. *The hallway smoke alarm starts chirping at 3 a.m. You are on a chair with the cover open and no spare battery in the house.*

   **Answer:** Don't leave it disabled. The chirp means the battery is nearly dead, and missing or disconnected batteries are the most common reason smoke alarms fail to sound in a fire. If you must silence it to sleep, put a note on the door or your phone and replace the battery first thing in the morning, then press the test button. If the alarm is more than 10 years old, replace the whole unit.
