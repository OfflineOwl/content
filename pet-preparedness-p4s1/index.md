# Pet Preparedness & Care

> **If it isn't safe for you, it isn't safe for them.** Pets left behind in a disaster are easily injured, lost, or killed, and you may not be allowed back for days or weeks. A little planning now decides whether your animals leave with you.

## Start here

1. **Put ID on every pet today.** A collar and tag with your cell number, plus a microchip registered in your name with a current phone, address, and an emergency contact outside your area.
2. **Get one carrier per pet** and label it with the pet's name, your name, and your phone numbers. Leave it out and open so it becomes a familiar place.
3. **Pack a pet go-bag** (list below) and keep it by the exit with the leashes.
4. **Decide where you would go.** Many public shelters and hotels do not accept pets. List pet-friendly hotels, boarding kennels, veterinary clinics, and friends or relatives outside your area now.
5. **Set up a buddy.** Give a nearby neighbor a key and agree to evacuate each other's animals if one of you is away.
6. **Take a photo of yourself with each pet.** It proves ownership and makes a "lost" flyer in seconds.

## Get emergency help if

**People first.** For a human emergency call 911 or your local emergency number. For an animal, call your veterinarian or the nearest emergency animal hospital. Save both numbers in your phone now, with a backup clinic outside your area.

Get a pet to a veterinarian right away for:

- Trouble breathing or choking
- Bleeding that will not stop
- Seizures, collapse, or unconsciousness
- Suspected poisoning, even with no symptoms yet
- Heatstroke: heavy panting, drooling, weakness, or collapse in heat
- Broken bones or serious burns

**Poisoning:** ASPCA Animal Poison Control, (888) 426-4435, answers 24 hours a day in the US. A consultation fee may apply. Outside the US, ask your veterinarian for the local service.

**If a person is bitten** by any animal, wash the wound and tell medical staff promptly. Rabies is the concern.

## The pet kit

Official lists agree on the items and differ on the amounts. Ready.gov says several days of food and water. The ASPCA says 7 to 10 days of food, at least 7 days of water, and 2 weeks of medicine, because you may not be allowed home for weeks. A week is a sound minimum. Two is better if you have room.

Keep two versions: a larger supply for sheltering at home and a light bag for leaving.

**Food and water**

- [ ] Your pet's usual food in an airtight, waterproof container (pop-top cans or dry)
- [ ] Drinking water for each pet
- [ ] Bowls, and a manual can opener if you need one
- [ ] One extra gallon (4 L) of water for rinsing a pet that gets into floodwater or chemicals

**Medical and records**

- [ ] Two weeks of any medicine, in a waterproof container
- [ ] Flea, tick, and heartworm preventives
- [ ] Copies of vaccination records (rabies above all), prescriptions, and medical history
- [ ] Microchip number and the chip company's phone number
- [ ] Your veterinarian's name and number
- [ ] Pet first aid kit: ask your veterinarian what to put in it

**ID and control**

- [ ] Collar with ID tag, plus a spare collar, tag, and leash
- [ ] Harness for dogs that slip collars
- [ ] Labeled carrier or crate for each pet
- [ ] Photo of you with your pet, and a written description

**Sanitation and comfort**

- [ ] Litter and a litter box or disposable trays
- [ ] Newspapers, paper towels, plastic bags, dish soap, household bleach
- [ ] A familiar blanket, toy, and treats
- [ ] A note on feeding times, medicines, medical problems, and behavior, for whoever may care for your pet

Swap stored food and water for fresh every two months, and check medicine dates.

### How much water?

No agency gives a per-pet figure, and needs vary enormously with size, diet, and heat. Measure it: fill the bowl with a known amount in the morning and measure what is left 24 hours later. Do this on a warm day, add half again as a margin, and multiply by the days you are storing for. Pets on dry food drink more than pets on canned food.

## Evacuating with pets

- **Go early.** Do not wait for a mandatory order. Late departures are rushed, and rushed is when animals get left.
- **Bring pets inside at the first warning** so you can find them. Know where each one hides when frightened.
- **Dogs on leashes, cats and small animals in carriers.** A scared animal bolts through an open car door.
- **Take the kit and the records.**
- **Never leave a pet in a parked vehicle,** and never leave one chained outdoors.
- **Mark the house.** A rescue sticker near the front door tells responders what animals live there. If you leave with your pets, write EVACUATED across it.

### Where you can go

- **Public shelters** often cannot take pets. Ask your local emergency management office which shelters are pet-friendly before you need one.
- **Service animals** are different. Under US law a service animal is a dog trained to do tasks for a person with a disability. Staff may ask only whether the dog is required because of a disability and what task it performs. They may not demand papers or a vest, and service animals are generally allowed in emergency shelters. Emotional support animals are not service animals under that law and may be treated as pets.
- **Hotels:** call ahead and ask about limits on number, size, and species.
- **Boarding kennels and veterinary clinics** outside the danger area.
- **Friends and relatives** who agreed in advance.

### If you truly cannot take them

This is the situation your buddy plan exists to prevent. The ASPCA and Humane World for Animals say the same thing: do not leave pets behind. If it happens anyway:

- Never tie or chain an animal. It cannot escape rising water or fire.
- Leave pets indoors with plenty of dry food and water.
- Post a notice where responders will see it, listing the animals inside and your phone number.
- Tell local animal control or emergency management as soon as you can. In the US and Canada, RedRover at (800) 440-3277 helps with emergency sheltering.
- Do not go back into a closed area on your own.

## Sheltering in place with pets

- Bring every animal inside and keep them in one safe room with you: an interior room with few or no windows.
- Remove houseplants, cleaners, and other toxic items from that room.
- Block gaps where a frightened cat can wedge itself: vents, and spaces behind or under heavy furniture and appliances.
- Keep dogs leashed and cats in carriers when the situation is unstable, so you can leave in seconds.
- In a flood, move pets to the highest part of the home. Fill tubs and sinks with clean water ahead of a storm in case the supply fails.
- Keep feeding times and routines as normal as you can.
- For toilet needs when going outside is unsafe, use litter, newspaper, or pads, and bag the waste.

See the Shelter In Place and Tornado Safety guides for the human side.

## Human food that can poison pets

In an emergency you may be feeding pets from your own supplies. These are the common dangers.

| Food or item | What it does |
|--------------|--------------|
| Chocolate, coffee, caffeine | Vomiting, abnormal heart rhythm, seizures |
| Grapes and raisins | Kidney damage in dogs |
| Onions, garlic, chives | Damage red blood cells and cause anemia; cats are most at risk |
| Xylitol (sugar-free gum, candy, some toothpaste and cough syrup) | Low blood sugar, liver damage, and seizures in dogs |
| Macadamia nuts | Weakness, tremors, and overheating in dogs |
| Alcohol and raw bread dough | Vomiting, tremors, breathing trouble, painful bloating |
| Avocado | Can be deadly to birds and rabbits |
| Very salty food | Excess thirst, tremors, seizures |
| Bones and corn cobs | Blockage or injury to the gut |
| Human pain relievers (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin, acetaminophen) | Can be harmful; acetaminophen is fatal to cats |
| Empty chip, cereal, and snack bags | Suffocation |

Also keep pets away from antifreeze, rodent bait, ice-melt, paints and solvents, and cleaners, and never use a dog flea or tick product on a cat. Trail mix is a common trap: raisins, chocolate, and macadamia nuts in one bag.

If your pet eats any of these, call your veterinarian or poison control straight away. Do not try to make an animal vomit, and do not give human medicine, unless a veterinarian or poison specialist tells you to.

## Health risks during and after a disaster

Crowding, floodwater, and contact with strays and wildlife spread disease to pets and from pets to people.

- **Rabies:** keep vaccinations current and carry the proof. Stressed animals bite. Keep yours leashed or in a carrier and away from other animals.
- **Floodwater and standing water:** do not let pets drink or play in it. It can carry leptospirosis, which also infects people. Wash your hands after cleaning up urine.
- **Fleas, ticks, and mosquitoes:** keep preventives going.
- **Hygiene:** wash hands after handling pets, food, or waste, and clean carriers and litter boxes regularly.

**When you return home:**

- Keep pets leashed or in carriers at first. Familiar scents and landmarks may be gone, and pets get lost in their own neighborhood.
- Check the house and yard for broken glass, nails, spilled chemicals, exposed wiring, and downed power lines. After a flood, look for snakes and other displaced wildlife.
- Expect clinginess, hiding, or irritability for a while. Go back to normal routines, and see a veterinarian if it does not settle or the animal seems unwell.

**If a pet is lost:** make sure your family is safe first. Then contact animal control, local shelters, and the microchip company, and post flyers with your photo once conditions allow.

## Handling an injured pet

First aid is a bridge to a veterinarian, not a replacement.

- **Any animal in pain may bite or scratch,** including your own gentle one. Do not hug it. Keep your face away from its mouth, move slowly, and stop if it becomes more agitated.
- **Bleeding:** press a clean cloth firmly on the wound and keep the pressure on while you travel.
- **Overheating:** move the animal to shade or a cool room, wet its coat with cool water, offer small drinks if it is alert, and go to a veterinarian at once.

## Notes by species

- **Dogs:** practice short stays in the crate and car rides before you need them.
- **Cats:** keep the carrier out all year with a blanket and treats inside. A pillowcase can move a panicked cat a short distance. Pack scoopable litter and disposable trays.
- **Birds:** travel in a secure cage or carrier, kept in a quiet spot. Cover the cage with a blanket in cold weather. In hot weather, mist the feathers with a spray bottle. Leave leg bands on and keep photos.
- **Reptiles:** a snake can travel in a pillowcase but needs secure housing on arrival. Bring a sturdy soaking bowl and a heating pad or hot water bottle.
- **Small mammals** (hamsters, gerbils, mice, guinea pigs): a secure carrier with bedding, food, and a water bottle, plus a hide box and a week of spare bedding.
- **Horses and livestock:** make sure every animal has identification. Leave earlier than you would with pets. Map two routes, line up trailers and experienced handlers, and confirm the destination has feed, water, and veterinary care. Practice loading.
- **If you live alone, are older, or have a disability:** name a temporary caregiver who has a key, and a permanent one in case you cannot take the animal back.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a timed pet evacuation. Start a stopwatch, then get every animal into its carrier or onto its leash and to the front door with the pet go-bag. Stop the clock. Write down the time and what went wrong: the cat under the bed, the missing leash, the carrier in the attic. Fix one of those things today, and run it again next month.

**What would you do?**

1. *A wildfire evacuation warning, not yet an order, arrives at 2 p.m. Your cat is hiding somewhere in the house. You think about waiting, since the cat always comes out at dinner time.*

   **Answer:** Find and confine the cat now. Put it in its carrier or shut it in a small room with the carrier ready. Leaving early is what gets animals out. An order may come with minutes of notice and no way back in.

2. *You reach a public shelter with your dog and are told no pets. A bystander suggests tying the dog to the fence outside with a bowl of water, or leaving it in your car overnight.*

   **Answer:** Neither. A tied dog cannot escape danger, and a parked car can turn deadly hot or cold. Ask the shelter staff and the local emergency management office where the nearest pet-friendly shelter is, then work down your list: boarding kennels, veterinary clinics, hotels, friends outside the area.

3. *Your dog gets into the go-bag and eats a pack of sugar-free gum. An hour later she seems completely normal, and roads are bad.*

   **Answer:** Call your veterinarian or ASPCA Animal Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 now, and read them the ingredient list. Xylitol in sugar-free gum can drop a dog's blood sugar and damage the liver, so do not wait for symptoms. Do not induce vomiting unless they tell you to.
