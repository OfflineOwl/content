# Offline Maps & Navigation

> **GPS gives you a dot. The map has to be on the phone already.** Your phone can find its position from satellites with no cell service at all, but without a downloaded map that dot sits on a blank screen. Ten minutes on Wi-Fi today fixes that, and a paper map covers the day the battery dies.

## Start here

1. **Download your home region in your phone's map app now,** while you have Wi-Fi. Make the area big enough to include work, school, every meeting place, and your evacuation routes to the next large town.
2. **Test it.** Switch on airplane mode for two minutes, open the map, zoom in on your street, and ask for directions to a meeting place. Then switch airplane mode off again.
3. **Put a paper road map in each vehicle and go-bag.** Mark home, meeting places, and at least two ways out.
4. **Write down your meeting places as plain descriptions and as coordinates.** See "Sharing a meeting point" below.
5. **Keep a charged power bank and a car charging cable** with the maps.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if you are lost or stranded and in danger: someone is hurt, water is rising, it is very cold or very hot, or night is coming and you have no shelter.

- **Say where you are first.** An address, a road and the nearest cross street or mile marker, or coordinates read from your phone.
- **If you can't get a call through, text 911** where that service exists. Put the location in the first message.
- **Then stay where you are** unless staying is dangerous. A person who keeps moving is much harder to find. In a vehicle, stay with it. See the Extreme Cold Survival guide for a stranded car in winter.
- Some newer phones can message emergency services by satellite when there is no cell signal. Check your phone maker's instructions before you need it.

## How your phone finds itself

Three things are easy to mix up:

| Piece | What it does | Needs cell service or internet? |
|-------|--------------|--------------------------------|
| **GPS** | Works out where you are from satellite signals | No |
| **The map** | Shows roads, names and terrain around your position | Yes, unless you downloaded it beforehand |
| **Live extras** | Traffic, closures, rerouting, search | Yes |

**GPS is a one-way broadcast.** The satellites send signals and track nothing on the ground. Your phone only listens, so it needs no connection to do it.

**What changes without a network.** Phones normally blend GPS with Wi-Fi and cell tower signals to get a faster, steadier fix, especially indoors and among tall buildings. With no network the phone falls back on GPS and its own sensors. Expect the first fix to take longer, and give the phone a clear view of the sky.

**How accurate.** GPS.gov's figure for phones is typically within about 16 feet (4.9 m) under open sky. Accuracy gets worse near buildings, bridges and trees, and GPS works poorly or not at all indoors and underground. In Google Maps, a light blue circle around the blue dot shows the uncertainty: you could be anywhere inside it.

**The map can be wrong even when the dot is right.** A mislabeled road is a map data error, not a satellite error, and a downloaded map is frozen on the day you downloaded it.

## Download maps before you need them

Menus change between app versions. If the labels below don't match yours, look for "Offline maps" in the app's menu or settings.

### Google Maps

From Google's help page for Android phones (the iPhone app is similar):

1. Open Google Maps and tap your profile picture or initial.
2. Tap **Offline maps**, then **Select your own map**.
3. Drag and zoom until the box covers the area you want, then tap **Download**.

What Google documents about using it offline:

- **Driving directions work,** as long as the whole route lies inside the downloaded area.
- **Transit, bicycling and walking directions are not available offline.**
- **No traffic information and no alternate routes.**
- **Downloaded maps expire.** The app tries to update them automatically over Wi-Fi when they are within 15 days of expiring. Turn on **Auto-update offline maps** in the Offline maps settings, and open the list before storm season to check.
- Some countries and regions don't allow offline downloads at all.

### Apple Maps (iPhone)

From Apple's iPhone User Guide:

1. Open Maps and tap your picture or initials next to the search field.
2. Tap **Offline Maps**, then **Add Offline Map**, and enter a place or tap **Current Location**.
3. Adjust the area and tap **Download**.

You can also touch and hold the map until a pin appears, then tap **Download**.

What Apple documents about using it offline:

- **Turn-by-turn directions for driving, walking, cycling and transit,** with an estimated arrival time.
- **Automatic Updates** is on by default.
- **Optimize Storage automatically deletes maps you haven't used.** For a map you are keeping for emergencies, make sure this setting is off, or you may find it gone on the day you need it.
- Downloads use Wi-Fi only unless you change the setting. Offline maps aren't offered in every country or region.

### Other apps and devices

Other map apps, car navigation systems and standalone GPS units can work offline too. The same two rules apply to all of them: download before you need it, and test it with the network off.

### What to download

- [ ] Home, and a wide margin around it. An evacuation can take you well beyond your own county.
- [ ] Work, school, daycare and every family meeting place
- [ ] Each evacuation route, all the way to the destination
- [ ] Anywhere you are traveling, before you leave

## What offline routing can't see

An offline map knows nothing about today. It can't see the flooded underpass, the fire across the highway, the collapsed bridge or the contraflow lanes police have opened.

- **Official evacuation routes beat the app.** Ready.gov's instruction is to follow recommended evacuation routes and not take shortcuts, because they may be blocked.
- **Signs, barricades and officers beat the app.** Never drive around a barrier or into water because the screen shows a road there.
- **Local radio fills the gap** with closures and open routes. See the Radio Use for Emergency Updates guide.
- **Before you set off, look at the whole route,** not just the next turn. If the phone dies halfway, you should already know the road numbers and the towns you pass through.

## Paper maps

Ready.gov lists local maps in its basic emergency kit, and the National Park Service tells visitors to carry a paper map in case their electronics fail. Paper has no battery, shows the whole region at once, and can be written on.

**What to keep:**

- **A state or regional road map** for the big picture and long evacuations.
- **A local street map or county map** for detours around your own area. If you can't buy one, print your neighborhood and your routes from an online map while you have power.
- **Your official evacuation route or zone map,** if your state or county emergency management office publishes one. Print it.
- **A topographic map** if you live, travel or might walk in rural or wild country.

**Mark them up in pen:** home, work, school, meeting places, hospitals, fuel stops, high ground, and at least two routes out in different directions. See the Evacuation Routes & Rally Points guide.

**Store them** flat or folded in a zip-top bag, one set per vehicle and one in the go-bag. Replace road maps every few years, since roads change.

### USGS topographic maps

The US Geological Survey (USGS) publishes the standard topographic maps of the United States, called US Topo, and they are free to download.

- Each sheet is a "7.5-minute quadrangle" at a scale of about 1:24,000.
- They show contour lines (elevation), water, roads, place names, boundaries and structures.
- They come as PDF files that open in any PDF reader with no connection, so save them to your phone too.
- Download current maps from The National Map Downloader (apps.nationalmap.gov/downloader). Historical maps back to 1884 are at ngmdb.usgs.gov/topoview.
- The USGS Store sells printed copies. A full sheet is far bigger than home printer paper, so print only the section you need or order the sheet.

### Reading the scale

The scale tells you how much ground one unit on the map covers. At 1:24,000, one inch on the paper is 24,000 inches on the ground.

| Scale | 1 inch on the map | 1 cm on the map | Typical use |
|-------|-------------------|-----------------|-------------|
| 1:24,000 | 2,000 feet | 240 m | USGS topo: walking, fine detail |
| 1:62,500 | about 1 mile | 625 m | Older topo maps |
| 1:100,000 | about 1.6 miles | 1 km | County-scale planning |
| 1:250,000 | about 4 miles | 2.5 km | Regional driving |

Road maps vary, so use the scale bar printed on the map.

For using a compass with a map, see the Compass & Map Navigation guide.

## Sharing a meeting point

"Meet at the school" fails more often than you would think. Which school, which entrance, which side of the road?

### Plain description first

A good description has four parts:

1. **The named place:** "Lincoln Elementary"
2. **The street and cross street:** "on Oak Street at 3rd Avenue"
3. **The exact spot:** "by the flagpole at the north entrance"
4. **The time, and what happens if you miss it:** "at 4 pm. If I'm not there by 5, go on to Grandma's"

Agree on these in advance and write them on everyone's contact card. On a highway, give the road number, the direction of travel and the nearest exit or mile marker.

### Coordinates

Coordinates pin down a spot that has no address.

- **Use decimal degrees,** the plain-number format that Google Maps shows: `38.8895, -77.0353`
- **Latitude comes first, then longitude.** Latitude runs from -90 to 90, longitude from -180 to 180.
- **Mind the minus sign.** In the continental United States, latitude is positive and longitude is negative. Drop the minus and the point lands in Asia.
- **Use a period, not a comma, as the decimal point.**
- **Give four or five decimal places.** One degree of latitude is about 69 miles (111 km), so the fourth decimal place is about 36 feet (11 m) and the fifth about 4 feet (1 m). A phone's GPS is no finer than that, so extra digits add nothing.
- **Don't mix formats.** The same place can be written three ways, and all of these are one point: `41.40338, 2.17403`, or `41°24'12.2"N 2°10'26.5"E`, or `41 24.2028, 2 10.4418`. Reading the last one as "41.242028" puts you miles off. If someone gives you coordinates, ask which format they are in.

**Finding your coordinates:**

- **Google Maps on a computer:** right-click the spot. The latitude and longitude appear at the top of the menu, and clicking them copies them.
- **Google Maps on Android:** tap the blue dot, swipe up on the "Your location" panel, and copy the **Plus Code**, a short code such as `JJXX+HR8, Seattle` that Google Maps can turn back into a location. Offline, it appears as a longer code such as `9C5M8QQ7+V8`. The other person needs Google Maps to read it.
- **iPhone:** the Compass app shows your coordinates and elevation, if Location Services is switched on for it. Apple cautions that the compass itself can be thrown off by magnets and interference.

**Passing them on:**

- By voice or radio: say each digit separately ("three eight point eight eight nine five") and have the other person read the whole thing back.
- On paper: write the coordinates next to the plain description, never instead of it.

## Battery habits

A lit screen is one of the biggest drains on a phone. A phone that is checked now and then lasts far longer than one left on the navigation screen.

- **Download and charge before the storm.** Ready.gov's advice is to charge every device before severe weather and keep a backup power source. Keep a charger in the car.
- **Switch on low power mode.** On an iPhone it dims the screen, shortens auto-lock to 30 seconds and stops background activity.
- **Check, then lock.** Look at the map at junctions, memorize the next two turns, and turn the screen off.
- **Write the route on paper** when the battery is under about 20 percent: road numbers, turns, town names, distances.
- **Airplane mode saves power but cuts you off.** A phone in airplane mode gets no calls, no texts and no emergency alerts. Use it only when you have a reason, and find out in your Start here test whether your phone still shows your position with it on.
- **Charge from the car while driving.** Never run an engine in a closed garage to charge a phone: carbon monoxide kills.

See the Battery Management & Rotation guide.

## When the phone is gone

Paper and attention are enough to get most people where they are going.

- **Orient the map.** Turn it until the roads on the paper line up with the roads in front of you.
- **Keep your thumb on where you are,** and move it at every junction. Finding yourself again after ten unmarked turns is much harder than keeping track as you go.
- **Follow big, obvious lines:** a highway, a river, a rail line, a ridge. They are hard to lose and easy to find on any map.
- **Tell someone your route and when you expect to arrive** before you leave.
- **If you are truly lost on foot, stop.** Stay where you are, make yourself visible, and signal. Three whistle blasts is the distress signal.

## People who need extra thought

- **Children and teens:** give them a paper card with the home address, the meeting places and a phone number. A child who can read a street sign and say an address can be found.
- **Older adults and anyone who doesn't use a smartphone:** paper maps with the routes highlighted and large-print directions.

## Emergency navigation kit

- [ ] Offline map of your home region downloaded, auto-update on, tested in airplane mode
- [ ] State or regional road map in each vehicle
- [ ] Local street or county map, with routes and meeting places marked
- [ ] Printed evacuation route or zone map
- [ ] Topographic map (paper or saved PDF) if you are in rural country
- [ ] Meeting places written as descriptions and as coordinates
- [ ] Pencil, pen and a small notebook
- [ ] Compass, if you know how to use one
- [ ] Power bank and car charging cable
- [ ] Zip-top bag for the maps
- [ ] Whistle

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Download your home region now, using the steps above. Then put the phone in airplane mode and try three things: zoom in until you can read the name of your own street, ask for driving directions to your out-of-neighborhood meeting place, and walk to the end of the block to see whether the blue dot follows you. Switch airplane mode off again. Last, find the coordinates of your main family meeting place and write them, with a four-part plain description, on your contact card. If any step failed, you have found the problem on a quiet day.

**What would you do?**

1. *A wildfire evacuation has been ordered. The official route is the highway, which is crawling. Your offline map shows a forest road over the ridge that would cut 25 minutes off the trip, and two cars ahead of you have just turned onto it.*

   **Answer:** Stay on the official route. Your offline map has no traffic, closure or fire information, so it is showing you where a road was, not whether it is open or where the fire is now. Officials choose evacuation routes knowing both. Crawling traffic on the right road is still moving away from danger.

2. *Cell service is fading in and out after a storm. Your brother texts: "Roads are bad here, come get us, we're at the gas station by the highway." There are four gas stations along that stretch of highway, and your next text may not go through.*

   **Answer:** Use the one message you may get to remove every doubt. Ask for the station's name, the exit number and which side of the highway, and ask him to send his coordinates in decimal degrees as well. Tell him to stay exactly there and name a time. Then write it all down before you drive, because you may have no signal when you arrive.

3. *You are driving unfamiliar back roads to a relative's house, two hours away. The downloaded map is working, but the phone is at 9 percent, the car charger is at home, and you have been leaving the navigation screen on.*

   **Answer:** Stop somewhere safe and spend one minute of battery wisely. Look at the whole remaining route and write down every turn: road numbers, town names and rough distances. Switch on low power mode and lock the screen. From here on, navigate from your notes and the road signs, and wake the phone only at a junction you are unsure about. Keep the last few percent for a call if something goes wrong.
