# Pediatric Emergency Care

> Children aren't small adults. Their airways block more easily, they get dehydrated and cold faster, and they can't always tell you what's wrong. This guide covers the emergencies most likely to hit a child, and when to get help right away.

## Do this now

**Child unresponsive and not breathing normally (or only gasping)?**
1. Shout for help. **Call 911** (or your local emergency number, such as 112 or 999) on speaker. Send someone for an AED.
2. **Start CPR.** Child: two hands (one for a small child), center of the chest, about 2 inches (5 cm) deep. Infant: the heel of one hand or two thumbs (not two fingers), just below the nipple line, about 1.5 inches (4 cm) deep. 100–120 compressions a minute.
3. Give **2 breaths after every 30 compressions** (15:2 if two rescuers). Breaths matter more for children than adults.
4. **Alone without a phone,** and you didn't see the child collapse: do about 2 minutes of CPR, then go call 911.

**Choking (can't cough, cry, breathe, or speak)?**
- **Child over 1:** 5 back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts. Repeat.
- **Infant under 1:** face down on your forearm, 5 back blows; turn face up, 5 chest thrusts with the heel of one hand. Repeat. Never abdominal thrusts.
- Becomes unresponsive: start CPR.

**Severe bleeding?** Press hard on the wound with both hands. For an arm or leg that won't stop bleeding, use a tourniquet 2–3 inches above the wound.

**Swallowed something poisonous?** If the child has collapsed, can't be woken, has trouble breathing, or is seizing, call 911. Otherwise call **Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222** (US). Don't make them vomit. **A swallowed button battery is an emergency**; call right away.

## Call 911 Right Away If a Child

- Isn't breathing, is struggling to breathe, or has blue or gray lips or skin
- Can't be woken, or is limp or unresponsive
- Has a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or doesn't wake up afterward
- Has signs of a severe allergic reaction (trouble breathing, swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat, faintness)
- Has severe bleeding that won't stop with pressure
- Has a head injury with danger signs (see Head Injuries)
- Swallowed something and has collapsed, can't be woken, has trouble breathing, or is seizing
- Has a serious burn
- Is an infant **under 3 months with a temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher**
- Has a fever and looks very ill, is unusually drowsy, has a stiff neck, an unexplained rash, or trouble breathing
- Shows signs of severe dehydration (very sleepy, sunken eyes, urinating only once or twice a day)

**Trust your instincts.** You know your child. If something seems seriously wrong, get help.

## How Children Are Different

| Factor | What it means |
|--------|---------------|
| Small airway | Blocks easily with food, small objects, or swelling |
| Faster breathing and heart rate | Normal rates are higher than in adults, especially in babies |
| Larger skin area for their size | Lose heat fast; burns cover more of the body |
| Less fluid reserve | Get dehydrated quickly from vomiting, diarrhea, or fever |
| Can't describe symptoms | Watch behavior, breathing, color, and feeding |

### Normal resting rates

How the child looks and acts matters more than the exact number, and published ranges vary.

| Age | Heart rate (beats per minute) |
|-----|-------------------------------|
| Newborn to 1 month | 70–190 |
| 1–11 months | 80–160 |
| 1–2 years | 80–130 |
| 3–4 years | 80–120 |
| 5–6 years | 75–115 |
| 7–9 years | 70–110 |
| 10 years and older | 60–100 |

Babies normally breathe about 30–60 times a minute; adults about 12–20 at rest. Count for a full minute while the child is calm.

## Assessing a Sick or Injured Child

### Look before you touch

Watch from across the room for a few seconds:

**1. Appearance**
- Alert and looking around, or listless and unresponsive?
- Recognizes parents? Can be comforted?
- Strong cry, or weak or high-pitched?
- Moving normally, or floppy?

**2. Work of breathing**
- Breathing very fast or very slow?
- Skin sucking in between or below the ribs, or at the base of the throat?
- Grunting, wheezing, or high-pitched noise when breathing in (stridor)?
- Nostrils flaring, or head bobbing with each breath?

**3. Circulation (skin color)**
- Pink, or pale, mottled, blue, or gray?
- Press a fingernail or the skin of the chest for a few seconds; color should come back within about 2 seconds.

**If any of these look wrong, treat the child as seriously ill** and get help.

### Approaching a scared child

- Get down to their eye level and speak calmly.
- Keep them with their parent or caregiver.
- Start with the least scary part (feet, hands) before the head or the painful spot.
- Tell them what you're about to do, honestly.
- Let them hold a comfort item.

## Fever

Fever is the body fighting infection. How the child looks and acts matters more than the number.

### When to get care

| Age or situation | Action |
|------------------|--------|
| **Under 3 months, 100.4°F (38°C) or higher** | Get medical care right away, even if the baby seems well |
| **Any age: temperature rises above 104°F (40°C) repeatedly** | Get medical care right away |
| **Any age: fever plus** looking very ill, unusual drowsiness or fussiness, a stiff neck, severe headache, severe sore throat or ear pain, trouble breathing, an unexplained rash, repeated vomiting or diarrhea, a seizure, or a child with immune or heart problems | Get medical care right away |
| **Under 2 years, fever more than 24 hours** | Call a doctor |
| **2 years and older, fever more than 3 days** | Call a doctor |

### Comfort care

- **Fluids:** offer breast milk or formula often to babies, and water or other fluids to older children.
- **Light clothing** and a comfortable room. Don't bundle a feverish child.
- **Don't** use cold baths, ice, or rubbing alcohol on the skin.
- **Fever medicine** is for comfort, not required. Use only children's products and follow the label, dosing by the child's weight (by age only if you don't know the weight). Use the measuring device that comes with that medicine, never a kitchen spoon or a device from another product.

### Medication safety

- **Never give aspirin** to children or teenagers with a viral illness such as flu or chickenpox. It can cause Reye's syndrome, a rare but serious illness.
- **Children under 2:** ask a doctor before giving acetaminophen. Any fever in a baby under 12 weeks needs a doctor right away.
- **Ibuprofen** isn't for babies under 6 months unless a doctor says so.
- Don't give two products that both contain acetaminophen (many cold medicines do).
- **Don't give honey to babies under 12 months** (risk of infant botulism).
- If you aren't sure of a dose, ask a pharmacist, doctor, or Poison Help (US: 1-800-222-1222).

## Dehydration

### Warning signs

- Dry mouth, fewer tears when crying
- Fewer wet diapers than usual (fewer than six a day in a baby)
- A sunken soft spot on a baby's head

**Severe signs:** sunken eyes, very sleepy or hard to wake, urinating only once or twice a day.

**Get care urgently** for any severe sign, or if the child can't keep fluids down, has bloody diarrhea, or is a young baby with vomiting or diarrhea.

### Treating mild dehydration at home

**Best:** a store-bought oral rehydration solution (ORS), such as Pedialyte, or ORS packets mixed exactly as directed.

**If you have no ORS at all**, the WHO home recipe is: **1 liter of clean water + 6 teaspoons of sugar + ½ teaspoon of salt**, stirred until dissolved. Use level spoonfuls and measure carefully; mistakes in homemade mixes are common, and too much salt is dangerous. Use it only until you can get proper ORS.

**Breastfed babies:** keep breastfeeding.

**Avoid** soda, sports drinks, full-strength juice, and broth for a young child with diarrhea; they have the wrong amounts of sugar and salt.

**How to give it:**
- Small amounts every few minutes, by teaspoon, syringe, or medicine cup.
- Increase the amount as they keep it down.

## Breathing Emergencies

### Choking: infant (under 1 year)

**Conscious infant who can't cry, cough, or breathe:**
1. Sit or kneel. Lay the baby face down along your forearm, head lower than the chest, supporting the jaw with your hand. Rest your arm on your thigh.
2. Give **5 firm back blows** between the shoulder blades with the heel of your other hand.
3. Turn the baby face up along your other forearm, supporting the head, head still lower than the chest.
4. Give **5 chest thrusts** with the heel of one hand on the center of the breastbone, just below the nipple line.
5. **Repeat** until the object comes out or the baby becomes unresponsive.

Never give abdominal thrusts to an infant.

**If the baby becomes unresponsive:**
1. Put the baby on a firm surface. Call 911 if no one has.
2. Start infant CPR, beginning with compressions.
3. Before each set of breaths, look in the mouth. Remove an object only if you can see it. **Never sweep blindly with a finger.**

### Choking: child (over 1 year)

**Conscious child who can't breathe, cough, or speak:**
1. Kneel or stand behind the child.
2. Give **5 back blows** between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, with the child leaning forward.
3. Give **5 abdominal thrusts:** fist just above the belly button, other hand over it, quick inward-and-upward pulls.
4. **Keep alternating** until the object comes out or the child becomes unresponsive.

**If the child becomes unresponsive:** call 911, start CPR, and look in the mouth before each set of breaths.

### Preventing choking

Until about age 4, hold off on common choking foods: hot dogs, whole grapes, nuts and seeds, popcorn, hard, gooey, or sticky candy, gum, chunks of raw carrot or apple, chunks of peanut butter, marshmallows, and chunks of meat or cheese. Chop round foods into pieces no bigger than ½ inch.

**Button batteries** stuck in the throat can cause severe damage in as little as 2 hours. If you think a child swallowed one, call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) or the National Battery Ingestion Hotline (1-800-498-8666) right away; the child will usually need an X-ray immediately. Don't make them vomit.

### Croup

**Signs:** a barking, seal-like cough, a hoarse voice, and sometimes stridor (a harsh, high-pitched sound when breathing in). Often worse at night, after a cold.

**At home:**
1. Stay calm and keep the child calm; crying makes it worse. Hold them upright on your lap.
2. Some parents use warm mist (sitting with the child in a closed bathroom with the shower running) or cool night air. Studies haven't proven either helps. If you try mist, keep the child well away from hot water and steam, which can burn.
3. Offer fluids.

**Call 911 if the child:**
- Is struggling to breathe, or can't speak or cry for lack of breath
- Has stridor (noisy breathing in) while resting, or it gets louder with each breath
- Has blue lips, mouth, or fingernails
- Is drooling or has trouble swallowing

### Asthma attack

**Signs:** wheezing, coughing, fast breathing, chest tightness, trouble speaking.

1. Sit the child upright and stay calm.
2. Give their quick-relief (rescue) inhaler, with a spacer if they have one, **exactly as their asthma action plan or prescription says.**
3. Move them away from smoke and other triggers.

**Get emergency help (call 911) if:**
- They have trouble walking or talking because they're so short of breath
- Lips or fingernails turn blue, pale, or gray
- The skin between the ribs sucks in with each breath
- They're still struggling about 15–20 minutes after using the inhaler, or getting worse

## Injuries

### Head injuries

**Call 911 or go to the emergency room right away if the child:**
- Passed out, even briefly
- Has one pupil larger than the other
- Is getting drowsier or can't be woken
- Has a headache that gets worse
- Vomits repeatedly
- Has a seizure
- Has slurred speech, weakness, numbness, or poor coordination
- Is confused, acting strangely, or doesn't recognize people or places
- (Babies and toddlers) cries and can't be consoled, or won't nurse or eat

**After a minor bump with none of these signs:**
- A cold pack wrapped in cloth for the swelling, up to 20 minutes at a time.
- Watch the child closely over the next day or two. If any warning sign appears, get care.
- **You don't need to wake the child every few hours** to check on them if they have no danger signs. Letting them sleep is fine.
- Keep them out of rough play and sports until they're back to normal; if they had concussion symptoms, until a doctor clears them.

### Burns

- **Cool the burn** under cool, clean running water for 5 to 20 minutes (aim for 20). Don't put ice on it.
- **Keep the rest of the child warm**; children can get dangerously cold while a burn is being cooled. Watch for shivering.
- Cover loosely with plastic wrap or a clean, non-stick dressing.
- **Get care** for any burn on the face, hands, feet, genitals, or across a joint, any burn larger than the child's palm, any blistered or deep burn, and any electrical or chemical burn.

See the Treating Burns, Cuts, and Fractures guide.

### Poisoning

**If you think a child swallowed, breathed in, or touched something poisonous:**
1. **Collapsed, can't be woken, having trouble breathing, or seizing?** Call 911.
2. Otherwise, **call Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222** (US; free, 24/7), or use webPOISONCONTROL online. Outside the US, call your local poison center or emergency number.
3. Remove anything left in the mouth. Rinse skin or eyes with plenty of running water if exposed.
4. **Don't make the child vomit.** Syrup of ipecac is no longer recommended. Ask Poison Help before giving anything to eat or drink.
5. Keep the container or label to read to them.

**Have ready:** the child's age and weight, what they took, how much, when, and any symptoms.

## Pediatric CPR

### Child (1 year to puberty)

- **Compressions:** two hands (one for a small child) on the lower half of the breastbone, about 2 inches (5 cm) deep (one-third of the chest's depth), 100–120 a minute, full recoil.
- **Breaths:** tilt the head back slightly, pinch the nose, seal your mouth over theirs, and breathe just enough to make the chest rise.
- **Ratio:** 30:2 alone, 15:2 with two rescuers.
- **AED:** use child pads if available (recommended under 8 years); if not, use adult pads, making sure the pads don't touch. On a small child, put one on the chest and one on the back.

### Infant (under 1 year)

- **Compressions:** the heel of one hand on the breastbone just below the nipple line, or two thumbs on the same spot with your hands wrapped around the chest. The old two-finger method is no longer recommended. About 1.5 inches (4 cm) deep (one-third of the chest's depth), 100–120 a minute, full recoil.
- **Breaths:** head in a neutral position. Cover the mouth **and** nose with your mouth and give small puffs, just enough to make the chest rise.
- **Ratio:** 30:2 alone, 15:2 with two rescuers.

See the CPR & Rescue Breathing guide for full steps.

## Comfort and Communication

**Do:**
- Get down to their eye level and use simple, honest words.
- Let them keep a comfort item and stay with a parent.
- Explain what you're doing before you do it.
- Offer small choices ("This arm or that arm?").
- Praise their bravery, and stay calm yourself.

**Avoid:**
- "This won't hurt" if it will
- Medical jargon
- Separating them from their parent unless you must
- Forcing cooperation if you can wait

**Distraction:** counting, singing, stories, bubbles, a flashlight on the ceiling, "blowing out birthday candles" breathing.
