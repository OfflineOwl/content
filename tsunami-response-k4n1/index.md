# Tsunami Response

> **Minutes matter.** A tsunami from a nearby earthquake can reach the shore in minutes, before any official warning. If you're on the coast and nature warns you, go to high ground immediately.

## Do this now

If you're near the coast and you **feel a strong or long earthquake**, see the **ocean suddenly drain away or rise**, or hear a **loud roar from the ocean**. Even one of these signs is enough:

1. **If the ground is shaking, Drop, Cover, and Hold On** until it stops.
2. **As soon as you can stand, leave for high ground or inland.** Don't wait for an official warning, and don't stop to pack.
3. **Go on foot if you can.** Roads can be damaged, closed, or jammed. Follow tsunami evacuation route signs (a wave with an arrow) if there are any.
4. **Get out of the tsunami hazard zone.** If you don't know where it ends, go to ground at least 100 feet (30 m) above sea level or at least 1 mile (1.6 km) inland. If you can't get that far, go as high and as far inland as you can.
5. **Stay there until local officials say it's safe.** Tsunamis are a series of waves, the first is often not the largest, and the danger can last for hours or days.

## Natural Warning Signs

Any one of these is reason enough to leave the shore right away:

- **A strong or long earthquake.** If the shaking makes it hard to stand, or goes on for a long time (some coastal agencies use 20 seconds or more as a guide), a tsunami may be coming. **If you're on the beach or near the water, any earthquake is your signal to move**, no matter how big or how long. **"Long or strong, get gone."**
- **The ocean suddenly drains away**, exposing the sea floor, reefs, and fish like a very low tide, or **rises fast** as a flood or a wall of water.
- **A loud roar from the ocean**, like a train or an airplane.

A drained beach is not something to explore. The water can come back within minutes, faster than you can run.

## Official Alerts

In the US, tsunami alerts come from NOAA's Tsunami Warning Centers through Wireless Emergency Alerts, NOAA Weather Radio, local sirens, and media. Other countries have their own warning systems.

| Alert | What it means | What to do |
|-------|---------------|------------|
| **Tsunami Warning** | A tsunami that may cause widespread flooding is expected or happening. Dangerous flooding and powerful currents can last for hours or days. | Move to high ground or inland now. Follow evacuation orders. |
| **Tsunami Advisory** | Strong currents or waves dangerous to people in or very near the water are expected or happening. Beaches and harbors may flood. | Get out of the water and away from beaches, harbors, marinas, and waterways. |
| **Tsunami Watch** | A distant earthquake has occurred and a tsunami is possible. | Stay alert for more information and be ready to act. |
| **Information Statement** | An earthquake has occurred, but there's no threat, or it was very far away and the threat hasn't been determined yet. | Stay informed. |

There is no "all clear" alert level. When a warning is canceled, that doesn't mean the danger has passed. Stay away until local officials say it's safe.

**Don't wait for an official alert if you notice a natural warning sign.** A tsunami from a nearby earthquake can arrive within minutes, before any warning is issued. A tsunami from a distant earthquake takes longer to arrive, which gives more time for official warnings.

## Evacuating

### Where to Go

- Out of the tsunami hazard zone, to the safe place you planned in advance.
- No plan? Follow evacuation signs, or go at least 100 feet above sea level or 1 mile inland, or as high and far as you can.
- A designated tsunami evacuation site or assembly area, if your community has one.
- **Last resort, if you can't get out of the hazard zone in time:** some strong, tall, reinforced-concrete buildings may protect you on their upper floors or roof. Some coastal areas have designated vertical evacuation structures. This isn't recommended everywhere, so learn your local plan.

### What Not to Do

- Don't go to the beach to watch the waves.
- Don't drive if you can safely walk to high ground in time.
- Don't go back for belongings.
- Don't return after the first wave. More waves are coming, and later ones can be larger.
- Don't assume a small first wave means the danger is over.

### People Who Need Help

- Plan in advance how you'll help children, older adults, and people with disabilities or limited mobility get to high ground quickly, especially if you live, work, or vacation in a hazard zone.
- Don't let helping someone become a reason to wait. Leave together, right away.
- Bring pets if you can carry or leash them without delay.

## If You're Caught

### In the Water

- Grab onto something that floats, such as a raft, tree trunk, or other large debris, and hold on.
- Get out of the water and to high ground as soon as you can.

### In a Building

- If you can't get out of the hazard zone, go to the highest floor or the roof of a strong, tall building.
- Expect debris in the water around the building, and stay there until officials say it's safe.

### On a Boat

- **In a harbor or marina:** leave the boat and move quickly to a safe place on land.
- **Already at sea:** move to deep water, at least 30 fathoms (180 feet / 55 m) deep (safe depths vary by region), and stay away from harbors under a warning until officials say the threat has passed. Be ready to stay at sea for a day or more. In the US, warnings are broadcast on VHF marine radio channel 16.

## After a Tsunami

### Waiting It Out

- **Stay on high ground until local officials say it's safe to return.** Waves can arrive anywhere from about 5 minutes to 2 hours apart, and dangerous currents can continue for hours or days.
- Listen to a battery or hand-crank radio, or your phone, for official updates.
- Text rather than call so lines stay open for emergencies.

### Returning Home

- Go back only when officials say it's safe.
- Stay out of floodwater. It can hide debris and sharp objects, carry sewage and chemicals, and be electrified by downed power lines.
- Treat every downed power line as live.
- Stay out of damaged buildings until they've been inspected. If you smell gas, leave and report it from a safe distance.
- Expect aftershocks if the tsunami followed a local earthquake. Drop, Cover, and Hold On when the ground shakes.
- Use stored water until officials say tap water is safe, and throw out food that touched floodwater.
- Wear sturdy shoes, gloves, and long pants for cleanup, and photograph damage for insurance.

## Before: If You Live, Work, or Visit on the Coast

- **Find out if you're in a tsunami hazard zone.** Look for tsunami zone and evacuation route signs, or check with local emergency management.
- **Know your route to high ground** from home, work, school, and places you visit, and practice walking it. Plan to reach your safe place on foot.
- **Sign up for local alerts** and keep emergency alerts turned on on your phone.
- **Keep a go bag ready,** but never let packing delay you.
- **When traveling,** check where high ground is when you arrive at a coastal hotel or campground.
