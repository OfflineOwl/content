> **Life-Saving Skills for Grid-Down Situations** — When hospitals are overwhelmed and power is out, knowing basic first aid can mean the difference between life and death. This guide covers essential techniques that require no electricity.

## The First Aid Mindset

In an emergency without power:
- **You may be the only help available**
- Professional medical care may be delayed hours or days
- Basic interventions save lives
- Stay calm — panic helps no one

> **You don't need to be a doctor.** You need to know enough to stabilize someone until help arrives — or keep them alive if it doesn't.

---

## Scene Safety First

**Before touching anyone, protect yourself.** If you become a casualty, you can't help anyone.

| Step | Action | Why |
|------|--------|-----|
| 1 | **Stop and assess** | Is the scene safe for *you*? |
| 2 | **Identify hazards** | Downed power lines, fire, unstable structures, violence |
| 3 | **Protect yourself** | Gloves on, barriers up — don't become victim #2 |
| 4 | **Then approach** | Only when you're sure it's safe |

### BSI — Body Substance Isolation

> Always assume blood and body fluids are infectious. This isn't paranoia — it's standard practice for every first responder.

- Wear **nitrile gloves** if available (keep multiple pairs stashed)
- Use a **barrier** (plastic bag, clothing) between you and fluids
- **Wash hands** thoroughly after — soap and water for 20+ seconds
- If no gloves: use plastic bags, extra clothing layers, or anything waterproof

## Primary Assessment: The ABCs

Check in this exact order. Each letter is more critical than the next — don't skip ahead.

### A — Airway

**Is the airway open and clear?**

| Check | What to look for |
|-------|-----------------|
| Responsive? | Tap shoulders, ask "Are you okay?" loudly |
| Can they speak? | Speech means airway is open |
| Choking? | Universal sign: hands clutching throat |

**If unconscious:**
1. **Head-tilt, chin-lift** — Place one hand on forehead, two fingers under chin, tilt head back gently
2. **Look, listen, feel** — Watch chest for rise/fall, listen at mouth, feel for breath on your cheek
3. **Clear obstructions** — Only remove what you can *see* — never blindly sweep the mouth

> **Suspected spinal injury?** Use the **jaw-thrust maneuver** instead: kneel above the head, place fingers behind the jaw angles, and push the jaw forward without tilting the neck.

### B — Breathing

**Are they breathing normally?**

| Normal | Abnormal |
|--------|----------|
| Chest rises and falls rhythmically | Gasping, gurgling, or wheezing |
| 12–20 breaths per minute in adults | Fewer than 8 or more than 30 |
| Quiet, effortless | Labored, using neck/shoulder muscles |

**If not breathing:**
- Begin **rescue breathing** — 1 breath every 5–6 seconds
- If no pulse either, begin **CPR** (30 compressions : 2 breaths)
- Recheck for obstructions if air isn't going in

### C — Circulation

**Is blood moving? Is there severe bleeding?**

| Check | How |
|-------|-----|
| Pulse | Feel the **carotid** (neck) for 10 seconds — use two fingers, not your thumb |
| Skin | Pale, blue, or gray = poor circulation |
| Temperature | Cool and clammy = possible shock |
| Bleeding | Scan the entire body — blood pools underneath |

> **Severe bleeding kills in minutes.** If you see it, control it *immediately* — before everything else except airway. Apply direct pressure with whatever you have.

## Common Emergency Situations

### Unconscious but Breathing — Recovery Position

If someone is unconscious but breathing normally, the **recovery position** prevents them from choking on vomit or fluids.

**Steps:**
1. Kneel beside the person
2. Place the arm nearest you at a **right angle** to their body, elbow bent
3. Bring the **far arm** across the chest — hold their hand against their near cheek
4. With your other hand, pull the **far knee** up, keeping foot flat on the ground
5. **Roll them toward you** by pulling the bent knee — they should roll smoothly onto their side
6. **Tilt head back** slightly to keep the airway open
7. **Monitor breathing continuously** — reassess every 2 minutes

> **Do not use the recovery position if you suspect spinal injury.** Keep them still and support the head in a neutral position.

### Shock

Shock is a life-threatening condition where the body isn't getting enough blood flow. It can follow any major injury, blood loss, severe infection, or allergic reaction.

**Recognition — look for the combination:**

| Sign | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Skin | Pale, cold, clammy, possibly bluish |
| Pulse | Rapid and weak (thready) |
| Breathing | Rapid and shallow |
| Mental state | Confused, anxious, or unresponsive |
| Other | Weakness, dizziness, nausea, thirst |

**Treatment:**
1. **Lay the person down** — flat on their back
2. **Elevate legs** 8–12 inches (if no spinal/leg injury suspected)
3. **Keep warm** — blankets above and below, protect from wind
4. **Do NOT give food or drink** — they may need surgery; vomiting risk is high
5. **Treat the underlying cause** — stop bleeding, splint fractures, etc.
6. **Monitor and reassure** — calm, confident tone helps

> **Shock can develop slowly.** After any significant injury, monitor for these signs even if the person initially seems fine. Reassess every 5 minutes.

### Choking — Conscious Adult

**Heimlich Maneuver (Abdominal Thrusts):**

1. Stand behind the person, wrap your arms around their waist
2. Make a **fist** with one hand
3. Place fist **just above the navel**, well below the breastbone
4. Grasp your fist with the other hand
5. Give **quick, firm, upward thrusts** — inward and up
6. Repeat until the object is expelled or they lose consciousness

**If they become unconscious:**
1. **Lower carefully to the ground**
2. Begin **CPR** — chest compressions may expel the object
3. **Check the mouth** before each set of breaths — remove any visible object
4. Continue until object clears or help arrives

> **For pregnant or obese individuals:** Use **chest thrusts** instead — same technique but with your fist against the center of the breastbone.

### Diabetic Emergency

If you know or suspect someone is diabetic and they're acting confused or unwell:

| Condition | Signs | Action |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| **Low blood sugar** (hypoglycemia) | Confusion, shakiness, sweating, irritability, pale skin, rapid onset | If conscious: give sugar — juice, candy, glucose tablets, honey. If unconscious: **do NOT give anything by mouth**, place in recovery position |
| **High blood sugar** (hyperglycemia) | Extreme thirst, frequent urination, confusion, fruity-smelling breath, gradual onset | Needs professional medical attention. Keep hydrated with water if conscious |

> **When in doubt, give sugar.** If blood sugar is low, sugar will help immediately. If it's high, a small amount of sugar won't make things significantly worse — but withholding sugar from someone who needs it can be fatal.

### Seizure

**During the seizure:**
1. **Clear the area** of anything they could hit — furniture, sharp objects
2. **Protect the head** — place something soft underneath (jacket, towel)
3. **Do NOT restrain** — you can't stop a seizure, and you may cause injury
4. **Do NOT put anything in their mouth** — they will not swallow their tongue
5. **Time the seizure** — this information is critical for medical providers

**After the seizure:**
1. Place in **recovery position**
2. **Stay with them** — they'll be confused and exhausted (postictal state)
3. Speak calmly and reassuringly — "You had a seizure, you're safe"
4. Note the duration and what happened

**Seek emergency help if:**
- Seizure lasts **more than 5 minutes**
- Person doesn't regain consciousness
- Another seizure follows quickly
- Person is injured, pregnant, or diabetic
- This is their first known seizure
- Seizure occurred in water

### Allergic Reaction / Anaphylaxis

**Mild reaction** — Hives, itching, localized swelling:
- Give **antihistamine** (diphenhydramine/Benadryl) if available
- Monitor closely for 1+ hours — reactions can escalate

**Severe reaction (anaphylaxis)** — This is life-threatening:
- Difficulty breathing, wheezing
- Swelling of throat, tongue, or lips
- Rapid, weak pulse
- Dizziness, fainting
- Full-body hives or flushing

**Treatment:**
1. **Use an epinephrine auto-injector** (EpiPen) immediately if available
   - Remove the safety cap
   - Press firmly against the **outer mid-thigh** (through clothing is fine)
   - Hold for **10 seconds**
2. Have the person **sit up** if breathing is difficult, **lie down** with legs elevated if dizzy
3. Call for emergency help
4. Be prepared for a **second reaction** (biphasic response) — can occur 4–12 hours later
5. Give a **second EpiPen** after 5–15 minutes if symptoms return or don't improve

> **An EpiPen buys time — it is not a cure.** The person still needs emergency medical care. The effects wear off in 15–20 minutes.

## Wound Care Without Modern Supplies

### Basic Wound Cleaning

**What you need:**
- Clean water (boiled and cooled is ideal, but any clean water works)
- Clean cloth or gauze
- Mild soap if available

**Procedure:**
1. **Wash your hands** first — you are the biggest infection risk
2. **Control bleeding** with firm, direct pressure (5–10 minutes without peeking)
3. **Irrigate the wound** — flush with clean water using gentle pressure
4. **Remove visible debris** gently with clean tweezers if needed
5. **Wash around the wound** with soap — not *in* the wound
6. **Pat dry** with a clean cloth
7. **Apply bandage** — keep clean and dry, change daily

### Improvised Bandages

When you don't have sterile supplies, improvise:

| Material | Best used for | Notes |
|----------|---------------|-------|
| Clean cotton t-shirt | General wound dressing | Boil first if possible |
| Sanitary pads/tampons | Wound packing, pressure | Excellent absorbency, often individually wrapped |
| Coffee filters | Small wound dressing | Clean and lint-free |
| Duct tape + cloth | Securing bandages | Don't apply tape directly to wound |
| Honey (raw) | Wound covering | Genuine antimicrobial properties — used in medicine |
| Super glue | Small, clean cuts | Pinch edges together, apply thin line on top |

**Securing bandages without tape:**
- Strips of cloth tied firmly (not tight enough to restrict circulation)
- Belt or rope for limbs — check for pulse beyond the bandage
- Safety pins
- Bandana tied around the wound

### Watch for Infection

Signs usually appear **2–3 days** after injury:

| Warning sign | What it means |
|-------------|---------------|
| Increasing pain | Infection is building |
| Spreading redness | Cellulitis — bacteria spreading in skin |
| Increased swelling and warmth | Inflammatory response |
| Pus or cloudy discharge | Active infection |
| **Red streaks extending from wound** | **Lymphangitis — seek help urgently** |
| Fever | Systemic infection — the body is fighting hard |

**If infection develops:**
- Clean the wound more frequently (2–3 times daily)
- Keep elevated if possible
- Use any available antibiotics appropriately (full course)
- **Red streaks or high fever = urgent** — seek professional help by any means

## Pain Management Without Medication

When painkillers aren't available, these techniques genuinely help:

### Cold Therapy (first 48 hours)
- Reduces swelling and numbs the area
- **20 minutes on, 20 minutes off** — never longer
- Wrap ice/cold pack in cloth — never apply directly to skin
- Improvise: frozen food, cold water in a sealed bag, snow in cloth

### Heat Therapy (after 48 hours)
- Relaxes muscles, increases blood flow, reduces stiffness
- Use for muscle pain, cramps, and chronic pain
- Warm water bottle wrapped in cloth
- Heated rice or bean sock (microwave or warm near fire)
- Warm, wet cloth

### Other Techniques

| Method | How | Best for |
|--------|-----|----------|
| **Elevation** | Raise injured area above heart level | Swelling, limb injuries |
| **Immobilization** | Splint the area to prevent movement | Fractures, sprains, joint injuries |
| **Distraction** | Talking, counting, singing, puzzles | All pain, especially in children |
| **Breathing** | Slow, deep breaths — in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4 | Acute pain, anxiety |
| **Compression** | Snug (not tight) elastic wrap | Sprains, swelling |
| **Massage** | Gentle pressure around (not on) the injury | Muscle pain, tension |

## First Aid Kit Essentials

### Basic Kit

- [ ] Nitrile gloves (multiple pairs)
- [ ] Adhesive bandages (assorted sizes)
- [ ] Sterile gauze pads (4×4 inch)
- [ ] Gauze rolls
- [ ] Medical tape (cloth and waterproof)
- [ ] Elastic bandage (ACE wrap)
- [ ] Triangular bandage / sling
- [ ] Scissors (trauma shears preferred)
- [ ] Tweezers
- [ ] Antiseptic wipes / povidone-iodine
- [ ] Antibiotic ointment
- [ ] Pain relievers (acetaminophen + ibuprofen)
- [ ] Antihistamine (diphenhydramine)
- [ ] Emergency blanket (mylar)
- [ ] CPR face shield

### Advanced Additions

- [ ] Tourniquet (CAT or SOFTT-W — **get training first**)
- [ ] Hemostatic gauze (QuikClot or Celox)
- [ ] Israeli bandage (emergency pressure dressing)
- [ ] SAM splint (moldable aluminum splint)
- [ ] Chest seals (vented, pair)
- [ ] Nasopharyngeal airway (NPA)
- [ ] Blood pressure cuff + stethoscope
- [ ] Suture kit (**requires training**)
- [ ] Oral rehydration salts
- [ ] Irrigation syringe

> **Buy quality, train with it, and check expiration dates every 6 months.** A kit you don't know how to use is just a bag of supplies.

## Documentation

In an extended emergency, written records can save lives — especially when patients are handed off between caregivers.

**Track for each patient:**

| Field | Example |
|-------|---------|
| Name and age | Jane Doe, 34 |
| Time of injury/illness | March 15, 2:30 PM |
| What happened | Fall from ladder, ~8 feet |
| Injuries found | Possible left wrist fracture, 3cm laceration on forehead |
| Treatments given + times | Splinted wrist 2:45 PM, cleaned/bandaged laceration 2:50 PM |
| Vital signs | Pulse 88, breathing 16, alert and oriented |
| Allergies / medications | Penicillin allergy, takes lisinopril |
| Response to treatment | Pain reduced after splinting, bleeding controlled |

> **Use any paper available.** Write on the patient's arm with a marker if you must. When professional help finally arrives, this record could change the course of treatment.

## When You Cannot Get Professional Help

If professional medical care is truly unavailable for an extended period:

**Prioritize by threat to life:**
1. **Life-threatening conditions** — airway, breathing, bleeding, shock
2. **Infections** — can become life-threatening within days
3. **Pain management** — suffering impairs recovery and judgment
4. **Preventing complications** — keep wounds clean, monitor for changes

**Accept your limitations:**
- You cannot treat everything — and that's okay
- Some conditions require surgery or advanced care that you simply can't provide
- Focus on what you *can* do well
- **Comfort care always matters** — even when you can't fix the problem

**Seek help wherever it exists:**
- Medical professionals in the community (retired counts)
- Veterinarians (extensive medical training)
- Pharmacists (medication expertise)
- Nurses, EMTs, paramedics, combat medics
- Telemedicine when communications are restored
- Medical reference books (keep physical copies)

## Key Takeaways

| Priority | Principle |
|----------|-----------|
| 1 | **Scene safety first** — don't become another victim |
| 2 | **ABCs** — Airway, Breathing, Circulation, every time |
| 3 | **Stop severe bleeding** — it kills in minutes |
| 4 | **Keep wounds clean** — infection is the long-term killer |
| 5 | **Monitor and reassess** — conditions change, catch it early |
| 6 | **Stay calm** — your composure directly helps the patient |
| 7 | **Know your limits** — seek help when it becomes available |
