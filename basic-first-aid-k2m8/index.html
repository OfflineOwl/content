Basic First Aid Without Power

When the power is out and help is slow to come, a few basic skills keep people alive: checking the scene, calling for help, stopping bleeding, CPR, clearing a choking airway, and knowing what to do next. None of them need electricity.

Do this now

  1. Check the scene. Downed power lines, fire, traffic, gas, rising water, unstable buildings, violence. Don't become a second victim.
  2. Check the person. Tap their shoulders and shout, "Are you okay?"
  3. Call 911 (or your local emergency number, such as 112 or 999), or point at one person and tell them to call and bring an AED and a first aid kit. Put the phone on speaker. If a call won't go through, try texting 911.
  4. Severe bleeding? Press hard on the wound, or use a tourniquet on an arm or leg. See "Severe bleeding" below.
  5. Unresponsive and not breathing normally (or only gasping)? Start CPR: push hard and fast in the center of the chest. Use an AED as soon as one arrives.
  6. Unresponsive but breathing normally? Roll them onto their side (recovery position) and keep checking their breathing.
  7. Choking (can't breathe, cough, or speak)? Give 5 back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts, and repeat. See "Choking" below.
  8. Stay with them and keep checking until help takes over.

The First Aid Mindset

In a grid-down emergency:

  • You may be the only help available, and professional care may be hours or days away.
  • Simple actions save lives: pressure on a wound, CPR, the recovery position, keeping someone warm.
  • Stay calm and work step by step. Your calm helps the person you're helping.

You don't need to be a doctor. You need to keep someone alive and stable until help arrives, and to recognize when something is beyond you.

Scene Safety and Protecting Yourself

  1. Stop and look before you rush in. Is it safe for you?
  2. Watch for hazards: downed power lines, fire, smoke, gas, traffic, rising water, collapsing structures, violence.
  3. Protect yourself from blood and body fluids: wear gloves if you have them, or use a plastic bag or layers of cloth as a barrier.
  4. Wash your hands with soap and water afterward.

Checking an Injured or Ill Person

Work through this in order. Life-threatening bleeding and breathing come first.

  1. Responsive? Tap and shout. If they answer, ask what happened and what hurts.
  2. Call for help if they're unresponsive or seriously hurt or ill.
  3. Severe bleeding? Look quickly over the whole body, including underneath. If blood is spurting, pooling, or soaking through clothing, control it now.
  4. Breathing normally? Look at the chest for up to 10 seconds. Gasping isn't normal breathing.
    • Not breathing normally: start CPR.
    • Breathing normally: check for other injuries, then put them in the recovery position if they're unresponsive.
  5. Keep reassessing. Conditions change. Recheck breathing and responsiveness every few minutes.

Suspected head, neck, or back injury (a fall, crash, diving accident): keep them still in the position you found them unless they're in danger, vomiting, or you need to start CPR.

CPR (Quick Version)

For someone who's unresponsive and not breathing normally:

  1. Call 911 and send someone for an AED.
  2. Kneel beside them. Put the heel of one hand in the center of the chest, the other hand on top.
  3. Push hard and fast: at least 2 inches (5 cm) deep, 100–120 times a minute. Let the chest come all the way back up.
  4. If you're trained and willing, give 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions. If not, keep doing compressions without stopping.
  5. Turn on the AED as soon as it arrives and follow its voice prompts.
  6. Don't stop until they start breathing normally, someone takes over, or you physically can't continue.

See the CPR & Rescue Breathing guide for children, infants, and details.

Severe Bleeding

  1. Press hard on the wound with a cloth or gauze, using both hands and your body weight.
  2. Don't lift it to check. If blood soaks through, don't remove the first pad; press harder, adding another pad on top if needed.
  3. Arm or leg bleeding that won't stop? Put a tourniquet 2–3 inches above the wound (not on a joint), tighten until the bleeding stops, and note the time.
  4. Deep wound in the neck, armpit, or groin? Pack it tightly with gauze or cloth and press hard.
  5. Keep them lying down and warm.

See the Bleeding Control & Tourniquet guide.

Choking

Signs: can't speak, cough, cry, or breathe; may clutch the throat; a weak, silent cough or high-pitched sounds; lips turning blue.

If they can cough forcefully or speak, encourage them to keep coughing and stay with them.

Adult or child (over 1 year) who can't breathe, cough, or speak

  1. Ask, "Are you choking? Can I help?" Have someone call 911.
  2. 5 back blows: stand slightly behind them, support their chest with one hand, lean them forward, and give 5 firm blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your other hand.
  3. 5 abdominal thrusts: stand behind them, wrap your arms around their waist, put a fist just above the belly button (well below the breastbone), grab it with your other hand, and pull sharply inward and upward 5 times. For a small child, kneel behind them.
  4. Keep alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or they become unresponsive.

Pregnant, or you can't get your arms around them: alternate 5 back blows with 5 chest thrusts instead, with your hands on the center of the breastbone.

Alone and choking? Call 911, then give yourself abdominal thrusts with your fist, or press your upper belly hard against the back of a chair or a railing.

Infant (under 1 year)

  1. Hold the baby face down along your forearm, head lower than the chest, supporting the jaw and head with your hand. Rest your arm on your thigh.
  2. Give 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
  3. Turn the baby face up along your other forearm, head still lower than the chest.
  4. Give 5 chest thrusts with the heel of one hand on the center of the breastbone, just below the nipple line.
  5. Repeat back blows and chest thrusts until the object comes out or the baby becomes unresponsive.

Never give abdominal thrusts to an infant.

If they become unresponsive

  1. Lower them carefully to the floor. Call 911 if no one has.
  2. Start CPR, beginning with chest compressions.
  3. Each time you open the airway to give breaths, look in the mouth. Remove an object only if you can see it. Never do a blind finger sweep.
  4. Continue CPR until help arrives or they start breathing.

Common Emergencies

Unconscious but breathing normally: recovery position

The recovery position keeps the airway open and lets vomit drain out instead of being inhaled.

  1. Kneel beside the person.
  2. Place the arm nearest you at a right angle to their body, elbow bent, palm up.
  3. Bring the far arm across their chest and hold the back of their hand against their near cheek.
  4. With your other hand, pull the far knee up so the foot is flat on the ground.
  5. Pull on the bent knee to roll them toward you onto their side.
  6. Tilt the head back slightly to keep the airway open.
  7. Keep checking breathing. Start CPR if they stop breathing normally.

If you suspect a spine injury and they're breathing normally, leave them as they are unless they vomit or you have to leave them alone.

Shock

Shock means the body isn't getting enough blood flow. It can follow major bleeding, burns, severe infection, dehydration, or a severe allergic reaction.

Signs: pale, cold, clammy skin; fast, weak pulse; fast breathing; thirst; confusion, anxiety, or drowsiness; nausea.

What to do:

  1. Call 911.
  2. Treat the cause: stop bleeding, use an epinephrine auto-injector for anaphylaxis.
  3. Lay them on their back. If they're not injured and it doesn't cause pain, you can raise their feet about 6–12 inches. If they're vomiting or drowsy, or you have to leave them, roll them onto their side instead.
  4. Keep them warm with a blanket under and over them.
  5. Give nothing to eat or drink.
  6. Reassure them and watch their breathing.

Diabetic emergency: low blood sugar

Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia) can come on fast in someone with diabetes, especially after missing a meal, extra exercise, or too much insulin.

Signs: shaky, sweaty, pale, confused, irritable, acting drunk, hungry, headache.

If they're awake and able to swallow:

  1. Give sugar. Glucose tablets are best. Otherwise: about 6–8 ounces (a small glass) of fruit juice or regular (not diet) soda, or a tablespoon of table sugar.
  2. Call 911 if they aren't better within 10 minutes, can't swallow, or have a seizure.
  3. Once they feel better, give them a snack or meal.

If they're unresponsive or can't swallow: give nothing by mouth; they could choke. Call 911 and place them in the recovery position.

High blood sugar (hyperglycemia) develops more slowly: extreme thirst, frequent urination, fruity-smelling breath, confusion. It needs medical care. If they're awake, give water.

Seizure

During the seizure:

  1. Stay with them and time it.
  2. Clear the area of hard or sharp objects.
  3. Cushion the head with something soft.
  4. Loosen anything tight around the neck.
  5. Don't hold them down and don't put anything in their mouth. They can't swallow their tongue.

After the seizure:

  1. Roll them onto their side (recovery position).
  2. Stay with them until they're fully awake; confusion and sleepiness afterward are common.
  3. Speak calmly: "You had a seizure. You're safe."

Call 911 if:

  • The seizure lasts more than 5 minutes
  • Another seizure follows before they return to normal
  • They have trouble breathing or choke, or are injured
  • It happened in water
  • They're pregnant, or a baby under 6 months
  • It's their first seizure, or you don't know their history
  • They aren't back to normal 5–10 minutes after the seizure stops

Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)

Signs (often within minutes of a food, sting, or medicine): trouble breathing, wheezing, swelling of the tongue, lips, or throat, a hoarse voice, tightness in the throat, dizziness or fainting, widespread hives or flushing, vomiting.

What to do:

  1. Help them use their epinephrine auto-injector right away if they have one. Don't wait to see if it gets worse.
    • Take off the safety cap and press the tip firmly into the outer thigh, through clothing if needed, until it clicks.
    • Hold it in place for the time printed on the device (for example, 3 seconds for EpiPen, 2 seconds for Auvi-Q).
  2. Call 911, even if they feel better.
  3. Position: let them sit comfortably if breathing is hard. If they feel faint or show signs of shock, have them lie down.
  4. If the first dose hasn't helped and help is more than 5–10 minutes away, a second dose may be given if a second auto-injector is available.
  5. Start CPR if they become unresponsive and stop breathing normally.

Antihistamines (like diphenhydramine) can ease itching and hives, but never use one instead of epinephrine for anaphylaxis.

Mild reaction (hives or itching only, no breathing or throat symptoms, no faintness): an oral antihistamine as labeled, and watch closely for any of the severe signs above.

Wound Care Without Modern Supplies

Cleaning a wound

You need: clean water (safe drinking water, or boiled and cooled), a clean cloth, and mild soap if you have it.

  1. Wash your hands first.
  2. Stop the bleeding with firm, direct pressure.
  3. Rinse the wound with plenty of clean running water until you can't see any dirt. Running water works better for this than antiseptics such as iodine.
  4. Remove visible dirt gently with tweezers cleaned with alcohol.
  5. Wash around the wound with soap. Try to keep soap out of the wound.
  6. Cover it with a clean dressing. A thin layer of petroleum jelly keeps a minor wound moist. Change the dressing daily, or whenever it's wet or dirty.
  7. Animal or human bites should be checked by a clinician as soon as possible.

Improvised bandages

Material Use Notes
Clean cotton T-shirt, pillowcase, or towel Dressing or pressure pad Boil and dry first if you can
Sanitary pads Pressure dressing Very absorbent, often individually wrapped
Coffee filters Small dressings Clean and lint-free
Strips of cloth, bandanas, safety pins Holding dressings in place Snug, not tight
Duct or medical tape Holding dressings in place Never directly on the wound

After wrapping a limb, check that fingers or toes stay warm and pink. Loosen the wrap if they turn pale, blue, cold, or numb.

Signs of infection

Usually appear 1–3 days after the injury:

  • Increasing pain, swelling, warmth, or redness
  • Redness spreading from the wound
  • Pus or cloudy drainage, or a bad smell
  • Red streaks spreading from the wound (get care urgently)
  • Fever or chills

If infection develops: clean the wound 2–3 times a day, keep it covered with fresh dressings, and raise it if you can. Don't take leftover or someone else's antibiotics unless a clinician tells you to. Red streaks, fever, confusion, or a fast heart rate mean get medical care by any means.

Pain Relief Without Medication

Cold (sprains, bruises, fractures):

  • Ice and water in a bag, wrapped in a damp cloth, or a cold pack wrapped in cloth. Never put ice directly on skin.
  • Up to 20 minutes at a time.
  • Improvise with frozen food, cold water in a sealed bag, or snow in a cloth.

Warmth (muscle aches and stiffness, not fresh injuries or burns):

  • A warm (not hot) water bottle or warm, damp cloth wrapped in a towel.

Other techniques:

Method How Best for
Elevation Raise the injured part above heart level Swelling, limb injuries
Immobilization Splint or sling to keep it still Fractures, sprains
Compression A snug (not tight) elastic wrap Sprains
Slow breathing Breathe in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4 Pain, panic
Distraction Talking, counting, singing Everyone, especially children

First Aid Kit Essentials

Basic kit

  • Nitrile gloves (several pairs)
  • Adhesive bandages (assorted sizes)
  • Sterile gauze pads (4 x 4 inch) and gauze rolls
  • Medical tape
  • Elastic bandage
  • Triangular bandage (sling)
  • Trauma shears and tweezers
  • Antiseptic wipes
  • Petroleum jelly or antibiotic ointment
  • Pain relievers (acetaminophen, ibuprofen) and an oral antihistamine
  • Glucose tablets
  • Emergency (mylar) blanket
  • CPR face shield or pocket mask
  • Flashlight or headlamp with spare batteries

Advanced additions (get training first)

  • Commercial windlass tourniquet (CAT, SOF-T Wide, or SAM XT)
  • Hemostatic gauze (QuikClot or Celox)
  • Pressure (emergency) bandage
  • SAM splint
  • Vented chest seals
  • Oral rehydration salts
  • Irrigation syringe

If anyone in your household has a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector, inhaler, or glucagon, keep a spare in the kit and check expiration dates every 6 months.

When Professional Help Is Far Away

Prioritize by threat to life:

  1. Breathing, bleeding, and shock
  2. Infection, which can become life-threatening within days
  3. Pain and comfort
  4. Preventing complications: clean wounds, fluids, warmth, rest

Know your limits. Some problems need surgery or hospital care you can't provide. Do what you can, keep the person comfortable, and keep trying to reach help.

Look for help nearby: doctors, nurses, paramedics, EMTs, military medics (retired counts), pharmacists, and veterinarians.

Key Takeaways

  1. Scene safety first. Don't become another victim.
  2. Call for help early.
  3. Stop severe bleeding: pressure, tourniquet, packing.
  4. Not breathing normally? Start CPR. Use an AED.
  5. Choking: 5 back blows, 5 abdominal thrusts, repeat.
  6. Keep wounds clean. Infection is the slow killer.
  7. Keep checking. Conditions change.
