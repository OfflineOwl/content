# Safe Room Setup

> **"Safe room" means three different rooms.** A storm safe room is an engineered box tested against a 15-pound timber fired at 100 mph. A refuge room for a break-in is any room with a solid door, a lock and a phone. A sealed room for a chemical release is upstairs, with plastic and tape. Know which one you are setting up, because the best place for one can be the worst place for another.

## Start here

1. **Name your threats.** Tornadoes or hurricanes where you live? Then you need a storm plan. Everyone needs a plan for an intruder and for a shelter-in-place order.
2. **Pick tonight's storm refuge:** the lowest floor, in a small interior room with no windows. This is your "best available refuge area" until you have something better.
3. **Pick the break-in refuge room:** one room with a solid door that locks, which everyone can reach quickly, with a phone charger in it.
4. **Agree on one phrase** that sends everyone there, no questions asked. "Safe room now" works.
5. **Put a small kit in each spot** (list below) and clear the clutter, so the room can be entered at a run.
6. **Run the drill** in the Practice section this week.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **Someone is breaking in or is inside your home.** Call from the refuge room, give your address first, and stay on the line.
- **You are trapped in your shelter after a storm,** or someone is hurt. Call or text, then use a whistle or air horn to guide rescuers.
- **Anyone has trouble breathing** in a sealed room.

A Tornado Warning is not a 911 call. Go to shelter.

## Three rooms, three jobs

| | Storm safe room or refuge | Break-in refuge room | Chemical sealed room |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Protects against** | Extreme wind and flying debris | A person inside your home | Contaminated outside air |
| **Best location** | Lowest floor, interior, no windows. A purpose-built safe room can be above or below ground. | A room everyone can reach fast, behind a solid locking door | An interior room above ground level |
| **How it protects** | Mass and structure | A locked door and a few minutes for police to arrive | Still air, plastic and tape |
| **How long** | Minutes for a tornado, a day or more for a hurricane | Until police tell you it is clear | A few hours |
| **Standard** | FEMA P-361 and ICC 500 | None covered here | Ready.gov and CDC guidance |
| **Full guide** | Tornado Safety | Lockdown Procedures | Shelter in Place |

One room rarely does all three jobs. A basement corner is the best refuge from a tornado and the wrong place in a chemical release, because many toxic gases sink. An upstairs bedroom may be the best place to lock in and call 911, and a poor place to be when a tornado hits. Give each threat its own spot, and teach everyone which is which.

## Storm safe rooms: what FEMA means

FEMA uses "safe room" for one thing only: a hardened structure built to its criteria to give near-absolute protection in tornadoes and hurricanes. "Near-absolute" means the people inside have a very high probability of escaping injury or death.

**What it is built to take.** FEMA recommends that home safe rooms be designed for 250 mph (400 km/h) tornado winds wherever they are built. The debris test is a 15-pound (6.8 kg) 2x4 board hitting end-on at 100 mph (160 km/h), roughly what a 250 mph wind can do with a piece of someone's house. Since 1998 tens of thousands have been built, and FEMA says it has received no reports of a safe room built to its criteria failing. FEMA documents an above-ground safe room in a garage that performed well when the home was hit by the EF5 tornado in Joplin, Missouri, in 2011.

**What it is not.** A house built to code is not a safe room. FEMA warns that even homes with wind-resistant features do not give reliable life-safety protection from strong tornadoes. A closet lined with extra plywood is a slightly better closet. The criteria apply to the whole box: walls, roof, door and anchoring to the foundation.

### If you are thinking of building or buying one

- **Types:** site-built from FEMA's published plans or prefabricated, above ground, in the ground or in a basement, inside the house or in the yard. Properly designed and installed, above-ground and in-ground rooms both protect lives. That matters if anyone in the home uses a wheelchair or cannot manage stairs quickly.
- **The door is the hard part.** Ordinary steel doors cannot pass the tests. Only a door assembly that has been tested, listed and labeled to the ICC 500 standard qualifies, and a homeowner or contractor cannot build one. Check the label for 250 mph and a 15-pound 2x4 at 100 mph.
- **Anchoring matters as much as walls.** Most house slabs were not designed for the loads. A retrofit often means cutting out a section of slab and pouring a thicker, reinforced one.
- **Where:** somewhere you can reach quickly from every part of the home, kept free of clutter.
- **Not in flood or surge areas.** Safe rooms are not allowed in floodways and should be outside high-hazard coastal flood zones and storm surge areas. A basement is not a suitable place when there is a flood hazard. If officials order an evacuation, go, even if you own a safe room.
- **Size:** at least 3 square feet (0.28 m²) of usable floor per person for a tornado, and 7 square feet (0.65 m²) for a hurricane, when stays are longer.
- **Cost:** FEMA's 2024 estimates put a small prefabricated steel unit of 4 by 6 feet (1.2 by 1.8 m) at about $5,500 to $6,700 plus the foundation. A site-built room of 8 by 8 feet (2.4 by 2.4 m) in a new home runs about $12,700 to $16,100 before the door, and a tested door costs about $2,500 to $3,200. Retrofits in existing homes cost more.
- **Help with the cost:** ask your state hazard mitigation officer or local emergency management office about grants.
- **Who to trust:** FEMA does not certify or endorse contractors or products, so "FEMA certified" on an advertisement is a warning sign. Talk to your local building official about permits, and use a licensed engineer or architect. FEMA's Building Science Helpline is 866-927-2104.
- **Tell rescuers where it is.** Register the location of the entrance with your local emergency management office or fire department as soon as it is built. Debris can block the door, and they need to know where to dig.

The plans are free in FEMA P-320, *Taking Shelter from the Storm*. Outside the US, ask your national building authority which storm shelter standard applies.

### No safe room? Use the best you have

- A community safe room or public storm shelter, if you can get there before the storm.
- Otherwise the best available refuge area: the lowest floor, a small interior room or hallway, as many walls as possible between you and the outside, head and neck covered.
- Mobile homes are never a refuge. Leave for a sturdy building.

See the Tornado Safety guide for exactly where to go in each kind of building.

## A refuge room for a break-in

This room has one job: put a locked door between your family and an intruder, and keep it there for the minutes it takes police to arrive.

**Getting out comes first.** If you can leave the house safely, away from the intruder, do that and call from a neighbor's home. The refuge room is for when you cannot.

**Choosing the room**

- Everyone can reach it without passing the likely entry points. A main bedroom often works because children can run to it.
- It has a solid-core or metal door. Fit it the way police recommend for an exterior door: a single-cylinder deadbolt with a thumb turn inside, and a heavy-duty strike plate held by 3-inch (7.5 cm) screws. If the door opens outward, use hinges with non-removable pins. See the Home Hardening Basics guide.
- A phone works in there, and a charger lives there.
- It must not trap you in a fire. Keep a window or second door you can open from the inside.

**What this guide does not cover.** There are no bullet-resistant or forced-entry ratings here. Those are engineered products with their own test standards, and they are work for a qualified professional. Do not trust improvised versions. Ordinary interior walls and doors should be treated as concealment only.

**When it happens**

1. Say the phrase. Everyone goes, with no stopping for belongings.
2. Lock the door. Push heavy furniture against it if you can do it quickly.
3. Lights off. Get everyone low and out of sight of the door, behind something solid if there is anything.
4. Call 911. Give your address first, then "someone is breaking into my home," where in the house you are, how many of you there are, and anything you know about the intruder. Stay on the line.
5. If speaking would give you away, leave the line open so the dispatcher can listen, or text 911 where that service exists.
6. Stay put. Do not go out to check on a noise or to protect property. Open the door only when the dispatcher confirms the officers outside it are real, and meet them with empty, visible hands.

Laws on defending yourself and your home vary by state and country. Learn the ones that apply to you. This guide does not give legal advice.

## The sealed room for a chemical release

This one is the odd room out: above ground, interior, with as few windows and vents as possible. Keep plastic sheeting, duct tape and scissors there. You seal it only when officials say to, and only for a few hours. The Shelter in Place guide has the steps and the reasons.

## Stock the room

The storm list below follows FEMA's. For a tornado, plan on a day of supplies. For a hurricane, three.

- [ ] Water: 1 gallon (about 4 liters) per person per day
- [ ] Food that needs no cooking, and a manual can opener
- [ ] First aid kit and a few days of essential medications
- [ ] Flashlights, at least one for every four people, with spare batteries
- [ ] Battery radio and a NOAA Weather Radio
- [ ] Phone charger and power bank
- [ ] Whistle or air horn to signal rescuers
- [ ] A pry bar or similar tool, in case the door is jammed by debris
- [ ] Wrench or pliers for utility shutoffs
- [ ] Helmets and sturdy shoes for everyone
- [ ] Blankets or sleeping bags
- [ ] Sanitation: bucket or portable toilet, bags, wipes, toilet paper
- [ ] Dust masks, and hearing protection for a storm safe room
- [ ] Copies of ID and insurance papers in a waterproof bag
- [ ] Supplies for babies and pets, and something to keep children busy

The break-in refuge room needs far less: a charger, a flashlight, and the door hardware.

Keep heavy storage out of both rooms. Things on shelves fall, and boxes on the floor slow you down.

## People who need extra thought

- **Limited mobility:** choose a ground-level spot with no stairs, and decide who helps whom.
- **Children:** practice it as a game with a clear signal. Tell them the rule is to go to the room, not to find a parent first.
- **Deaf or hard of hearing:** add a warning that does not depend on sound, such as a weather radio with a strobe or bed shaker, and phone alerts set to vibrate.
- **Medical equipment:** keep spare batteries or a power bank in the room.
- **Pets:** keep a leash or carrier in the storm spot. In a break-in, do not delay to find a pet.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run both routes, timed. First the storm drill: from wherever people are, someone calls "tornado," and everyone gets to the storm refuge and crouches with head and neck covered. Then the break-in drill at bedtime with the lights off: someone says the phrase, and everyone gets to the refuge room, locks the door and sits low, out of sight of it. Check that the phone has signal in there and that a child can work the lock. Write both times down and try to beat them next month.

**What would you do?**

1. *A Tornado Warning sounds for your area. Your family's "safe room" is the upstairs main bedroom, where you fitted a solid door and a deadbolt last year.*

   **Answer:** Not that room. A strong door does nothing against wind, and upstairs is the worst place to be in a tornado. Go to the lowest floor, into a small interior room or hallway with no windows, and cover your head and neck. The bedroom is your break-in refuge. Storms have their own spot.

2. *At 2 a.m. you hear glass break downstairs. Your partner picks up a flashlight to go down and see what it was.*

   **Answer:** Nobody goes down. Meeting an intruder on the stairs is the most dangerous option, and nothing downstairs is worth it. Say the phrase, get everyone into the refuge room, lock the door and call 911 with your address first. Stay there with the line open until the dispatcher says the officers at your door are real.

3. *A hurricane evacuation is ordered for your zone because of storm surge. You have a FEMA-standard safe room in the garage and wonder if that means you can stay.*

   **Answer:** Go. A safe room protects against wind and debris, not water, which is why FEMA says they should not be built where surge can reach and that people should follow evacuation orders even if they have one. Take your kit and leave by the official route.
