EMERGENCY CHILDBIRTH
====================

Most babies are born without problems. If a birth is happening before help can arrive, your job is to call for help, keep mother and baby warm and safe, let the birth happen on its own, and watch for danger signs.


DO THIS NOW
-----------

1. CALL 911 (or your local emergency number, such as 112 or 999). Say a baby is being born and where you are. Put the phone on speaker. The dispatcher can coach you through the birth.
2. WASH YOUR HANDS and put on gloves if you have them.
3. Get her somewhere warm and private. Put clean towels or sheets under her hips. Let her choose a comfortable position: half-sitting propped up with knees bent and apart, on her side, or on hands and knees.
4. Gather clean, dry towels or blankets for the baby, and something to cover the baby's head.
5. Let her body do the work. When the head appears, support it gently with your palm. NEVER pull on the baby or the cord.
6. As soon as the baby is out, dry the baby well, then put the baby skin-to-skin on her chest and cover both with a dry blanket. Cover the baby's head.
7. Check that the baby is breathing or crying. If not, see "IF THE BABY IS NOT BREATHING" below.
8. There's no rush to cut the cord. Wait at least 1 minute, and if help is on the way, leave the cord for them.
9. Note the time of birth.


CALL 911 IMMEDIATELY / TELL THE DISPATCHER IF
---------------------------------------------

- The cord comes out of the vagina before the baby (prolapsed cord)
- The baby's bottom, feet, an arm, or anything other than the head comes first
- Heavy bleeding before or after the birth
- The baby is not breathing, is limp, or is blue or very pale after you dry and rub them
- More than one baby is expected
- The baby is very premature
- The mother has a seizure, severe headache with vision changes, chest pain, trouble breathing, or passes out
- Green, brown, or bad-smelling fluid when the waters break


SIGNS BIRTH IS CLOSE
--------------------

- Strong contractions coming every 2 to 3 minutes
- She feels a strong urge to push or to have a bowel movement
- Bulging at the vaginal opening, or the baby's head is visible (crowning)
- She says the baby is coming

If you see the head, the birth is happening now. Don't try to travel; stay put, call 911, and prepare.


GET READY
---------

SUPPLIES (whatever you can find quickly):
- Clean towels, sheets, or blankets (several)
- Gloves, if available
- A plastic sheet, shower curtain, or trash bags to protect the surface
- Something to cover the baby's head (hat, sock, cloth)
- A bowl or bag for the placenta
- Pads, towels, or cloths for the mother's bleeding afterward
- Clean shoelaces or string and clean scissors (only in case the cord must be cut later; see below)

PREPARE:
1. Wash your hands with soap and water.
2. Make the room as warm as you can. Newborns lose heat fast.
3. Lay down plastic, then clean sheets or towels under her hips.
4. Keep calm and talk her through each contraction.


DURING THE BIRTH
----------------

THE HEAD
1. Don't pull. Let the head come out slowly.
2. As the head crowns, gently support it with the flat of your hand so it doesn't pop out suddenly.
3. Encourage her to breathe through the contractions (short panting breaths) rather than pushing hard as the head comes out, so it comes out slowly.
4. Once the head is out, feel at the baby's neck for the cord.
   - If the cord is loosely around the neck, gently slip it over the baby's head.
   - If it's too tight to slip over, don't pull on it. Tell the dispatcher and follow their instructions.
5. Wipe any mucus from the baby's nose and mouth with a clean cloth.

THE SHOULDERS AND BODY
1. The head will turn to one side on its own.
2. With the next contraction, the shoulders usually come one at a time. Support the head and let them come; don't pull.
3. The rest of the body slides out quickly. The baby will be slippery. Hold the baby securely, supporting the head and neck.


RIGHT AFTER THE BIRTH: THE BABY
-------------------------------

1. DRY the baby right away, rubbing the back, body, and head with a clean dry towel. This warms the baby and helps breathing.
2. Remove the wet towel. Put the baby skin-to-skin on the mother's chest or belly, and cover both with a dry blanket. Cover the baby's head.
3. CHECK breathing. Most babies cry or breathe within the first minute. Their color starts out bluish and turns pinker over the first few minutes; blue hands and feet are normal at first.
4. Don't suction a baby who is breathing. Only wipe the mouth and nose if mucus or fluid is blocking them.
5. Don't wash the baby. The white coating (vernix) protects the skin.
6. Help her start breastfeeding within the first hour.


IF THE BABY IS NOT BREATHING
----------------------------

Tell the dispatcher right away. Then:

1. Keep drying and rubbing the baby's back, body, and head firmly. You can also gently flick the soles of the feet.
2. Lay the baby on their back on a firm, warm surface. Put the head in a neutral "sniffing" position: not tipped back, not tucked forward. A small folded cloth under the shoulders can help.
3. Clear the mouth and nose with a cloth if there's mucus blocking them.
4. If the baby still isn't breathing (or is only gasping) within about a minute of birth, despite drying and rubbing, start breaths:
   - Cover the baby's mouth AND nose with your mouth.
   - Give small, gentle puffs, just enough to make the chest rise.
   - Give about 1 puff every 1 to 2 seconds (30 to 60 a minute).
   - If the chest doesn't rise, reposition the head and try again.
5. Keep giving breaths until the baby breathes or cries, or help arrives. For a newborn, breathing for the baby is the most important step.
6. If the baby still shows no signs of life (no breathing, no movement, limp) after you've given breaths that make the chest rise, the dispatcher may tell you to start chest compressions. Follow their instructions.


THE UMBILICAL CORD
------------------

- There's no rush. For a healthy baby, wait at least 1 minute before tying or cutting the cord. If help will arrive soon, you can leave the cord attached for them to deal with.
- Keep the baby on the mother's chest or belly while the cord is attached.
- Never pull on the cord.
- If the placenta comes out while the cord is still attached, put it in a bowl or bag and keep it with the baby.

IF YOU HAVE TO CUT THE CORD (help is many hours or days away):
- Wait until the cord has stopped pulsing.
- Tie it very tightly in two places with clean string or shoelaces: one tie about 2 cm (1 inch) from the baby's belly and the other about 5 cm (2 inches) from the belly.
- Cut between the ties with scissors or a blade that has been boiled.
- Check the baby's end often for bleeding. If it oozes, add another tie between the skin and the first tie.
- Keep the stump clean and dry.


RIGHT AFTER THE BIRTH: THE MOTHER
---------------------------------

1. THE PLACENTA comes out on its own some time after the baby, often with a gush of blood. She may feel cramps and an urge to push.
   - Wait for it. DO NOT pull on the cord to get it out.
   - Put it in a bowl or bag and give it to the responders when they arrive.
2. Help her start breastfeeding within the first hour.
3. Put pads or folded towels between her legs. Don't put anything inside the vagina.
4. Keep her and the baby warm. Give her water or a drink if she wants it. Don't leave her alone for the first 24 hours.

HEAVY BLEEDING IS AN EMERGENCY. Call 911 again or get help by any means if:
- She soaks through one or more pads in an hour, or passes clots the size of an egg or bigger
- She becomes dizzy or faint, or her heart is racing

While you wait, if she's bleeding heavily after the placenta is out, firmly rubbing her lower belly (just below the belly button) can help the uterus contract and slow the bleeding. Keep her lying down and warm.


WARNING SIGNS AND WHAT TO DO
----------------------------

PROLAPSED CORD (cord comes out before the baby):
- Call 911 immediately.
- Don't push the cord back or handle it more than you must.
- Help her get on her knees with her chest down on the floor and hips in the air (knee-chest position), or lie on her left side with a pillow under her left hip. This takes pressure off the cord.

BABY COMING BOTTOM OR FEET FIRST (breech):
- Call 911 and follow the dispatcher.
- Don't pull on the baby's legs or body. Support the baby's body as it comes and let the birth happen.

MOTHER: get help urgently for heavy bleeding, a seizure, a severe headache, vision changes, chest pain or a racing heart, trouble breathing, or fainting.

BABY: get help urgently if the baby isn't breathing well, is limp, stays blue or pale, grunts or sucks the chest in with each breath, is very small or premature, or feels cold.


THE FIRST DAYS
--------------

MOTHER: these are urgent warning signs for up to a year after birth. Get care right away for:
- Heavy bleeding (soaking one or more pads an hour, or clots bigger than an egg), or bad-smelling discharge
- A fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher
- A headache that won't go away or gets worse, dizziness or fainting, or vision changes
- Trouble breathing, chest pain, or a fast-beating heart
- Severe belly pain, or severe nausea and vomiting
- Severe swelling, redness, or pain in a leg or arm
- Extreme swelling of the hands or face
- Overwhelming tiredness
- Thoughts of harming herself or the baby

BABY:
- Keep the baby warm, ideally skin-to-skin, and feed on demand (breast milk; no water or other liquids).
- Keep the cord stump clean and dry.
- Get care urgently if the baby feels hot or cold, feeds poorly, is very sleepy or floppy, breathes fast or with the chest sucking in, has a seizure, or has a red, oozing cord stump.


APGAR SCORE (FOR YOUR NOTES)
----------------------------

Medical staff score the baby at 1 and 5 minutes after birth. Writing down what you saw helps them. Score each item 0, 1, or 2:

A - APPEARANCE (color): 0 = blue or pale all over; 1 = body pink, hands and feet blue; 2 = pink all over
P - PULSE (heart rate): 0 = none; 1 = under 100 per minute; 2 = 100 or more
G - GRIMACE (reaction to stimulation): 0 = none; 1 = grimace; 2 = cries, coughs, or sneezes
A - ACTIVITY (muscle tone): 0 = limp; 1 = some bending of arms and legs; 2 = active movement
R - RESPIRATION (breathing): 0 = not breathing; 1 = weak or irregular; 2 = strong cry

7 or higher is normal. Below 7 means the baby needs medical attention. Don't stop to score a baby who isn't breathing; start helping instead.


WHAT NOT TO DO
--------------

- Don't pull on the baby or the cord
- Don't try to push a baby back in or tell her to hold her legs together
- Don't put anything inside the vagina
- Don't cut the cord unless you have to, and never with an unboiled blade
- Don't wash off the vernix or bathe the baby
- Don't leave the baby wet or uncovered
- Don't give the baby water, sugar water, or other liquids
- Don't separate mother and baby unless one of them needs care


STAY CALM AND SUPPORTIVE
------------------------

- Your calm helps her. Speak quietly and confidently.
- Tell her what you see and that she's doing well.
- Help her breathe through contractions.
- Protect her privacy and dignity.
- Keep the room warm and quiet.
