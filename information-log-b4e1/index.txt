EMERGENCY INFORMATION LOG
=========================

Write it down while it is happening. In a long emergency, "when
did the power go out?", "who had the last dose?", and "what did
the insurance company say?" all have answers that matter, and
nobody will remember them. A notebook and a pencil will.


START HERE
----------

1. PICK THE LOG TODAY. A bound notebook and two pencils or pens,
   kept with your emergency kit. Bound pages stay in order and
   don't get lost.
2. WRITE THE FIRST LINE as soon as an emergency starts: date,
   time, where you are, who is with you, and what happened.
3. GIVE EVERY ENTRY FOUR PARTS: the TIME, WHAT happened or what
   you were told, the SOURCE, and what you DID about it.
4. KEEP A SEPARATE PAGE FOR MEDICINES. Every dose: who got it,
   what, how much, when, and who gave it.
5. PHOTOGRAPH DAMAGE BEFORE YOU CLEAN, MOVE, OR THROW OUT
   ANYTHING, and note in the log what you photographed.
6. PHOTOGRAPH THE LOG PAGES ONCE A DAY, so the record survives if
   the notebook does not.

One line per entry is enough. A short log you keep beats a
detailed one you abandon.


GET EMERGENCY HELP IF
---------------------

The log never comes before a life. Call 911 (or your local
emergency number) first, act, and write afterward.

- Once help is on the way, the log becomes useful to the
  responders. Write down when the symptoms or injury started, what
  medicines the person has had and when, and the time any
  tourniquet went on. Hand the page to the crew. See the Bleeding
  Control & Tourniquet Use guide.
- If you find from the log that someone has had a double dose of a
  medicine, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 (US, free, 24
  hours) right away. Outside the US, call your local poison center
  or emergency number.


WHY BOTHER
----------

Professional responders log everything. FEMA's Incident Command
System gives every team an Activity Log form (ICS 214) for the
date, the time, and "notable activities." Its stated purpose is
basic documentation of what was done and a reference for the
review afterward. A household needs the same thing for the same
reasons.

DURING THE EMERGENCY, a log lets you:

- Hand over cleanly. When one adult sleeps and another takes over,
  the page says what has been done.
- Avoid medicine mistakes. When several people care for one
  patient, a log kept with the medicine is how you prevent a
  double dose.
- Run the clocks that keep you safe (see the next section).
- Tell confirmed facts from rumor, because every entry names its
  source.
- Make better decisions. Writing down "decided to stay, because
  the road is flooded" makes you check that the reason is still
  true tomorrow.

AFTER THE EMERGENCY, the same pages support:

- Your insurance claim: what was damaged, when you found it, what
  you spent.
- Disaster assistance. FEMA tells survivors to keep receipts and
  records of all repair, cleanup, and disaster-related costs for
  at least three years.
- Tax relief. The IRS notes that good records of a loss are needed
  for tax purposes, federal assistance, and insurance, and that
  the more accurately a loss is estimated, the more loan and grant
  money may be available.
- Your own lessons for next time.


THE CLOCKS WORTH RUNNING
------------------------

Some safety rules depend on a start time. If you don't write the
start time down, you can't use the rule.

- POWER WENT OUT. With the door kept closed, a refrigerator keeps
  food safe for up to 4 hours. A full freezer holds for up to 48
  hours, a half-full one up to 24. Log the minute the power failed
  and the "safe until" time next to it. See the Food Safety
  Without Refrigeration guide.
- A MEDICINE WAS GIVEN. The label says how long to wait before the
  next dose. Only the log says when the last one was.
- A TOURNIQUET WENT ON. Responders need the time.
- SYMPTOMS STARTED. "Vomiting since 14:00, three times" is what a
  doctor will ask for.
- A WARNING WAS ISSUED. Note when it expires. Sirens do not sound
  an all-clear.
- A WATER NOTICE WAS ISSUED (boil water, do not drink). Note who
  issued it and when, and log the time it is lifted.
- THE NEXT CHECK-IN with family or your out-of-town contact. See
  the Emergency Communications Plan (Offline) guide.


HOW TO WRITE AN ENTRY
---------------------

TIME - WHAT - SOURCE - ACTION

- TIME: use the 24-hour clock (14:30, not 2:30), as responders do.
  It removes the a.m./p.m. mistake. Write the date at the top of
  every page.
- WHAT: the fact, in a few words. Numbers where you have them.
- SOURCE: how you know. Mark each item as something you SAW
  yourself, HEARD from a named person, or got from an OFFICIAL
  channel (radio station, alert, agency). Mark rumors
  "unconfirmed." See the Verifying Information During Emergencies
  guide.
- ACTION: what you did or decided, and why, in one phrase.

GOOD HABITS:

- Write at the time, not that evening from memory.
- Stick to facts. "Water at second porch step" is useful next
  week. "Flooding is terrible" is not.
- Cross out mistakes with a single line. Don't erase or tear out
  pages. A record that has clearly never been tidied up is easier
  for others to trust.
- Log quiet periods too: "06:00 no change overnight."
- Name one log keeper at a time, and say so out loud when you hand
  over. An older child can do this job well.

SAMPLE:

Tue 14 Oct  Day 1  Home. All 4 of us + dog.

17:52 Power out, whole street. SAW.
      Fridge + freezer shut. Fridge
      food safe until 21:52.
18:05 County alert (text): flash flood
      warning until 23:00. OFFICIAL.
      Moved car to top of hill.
18:40 Water in back yard, 10 ft from
      door. SAW. Photo taken.
19:15 Neighbor (Dana): bridge on Mill
      Rd closed. HEARD, unconfirmed.
19:30 Radio 1010 AM: Mill Rd bridge
      closed. OFFICIAL. Decided to stay:
      only other route floods first.
20:00 Texted Aunt Rosa (out of town):
      all OK, staying. Next check 08:00.
21:50 Fridge hits 4 h. Moved milk and
      meat to cooler with ice.


THE MEDICINE PAGE
-----------------

Poison centers recommend exactly this: decide who gives each dose,
keep a log with the medicine, and mark every dose as it is given.

- Columns: date, time, person, medicine and strength, amount
  given, given by.
- Keep the page with the medicines, not in a drawer across the
  room.
- Write the dose down when you give it, not later.
- If you don't know when the last dose was given, don't guess. Ask
  whoever else has been caring for the person. If you still can't
  tell, call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222 in the US), a pharmacist,
  or a doctor before giving more.
- On the inside cover, list each person's regular medicines,
  doses, allergies, and conditions. Take the notebook with you to
  a clinic, shelter, or hospital.

For long-term conditions, see the Managing Chronic Conditions in
Emergencies guide.


THE DAMAGE AND MONEY PAGES
--------------------------

Start these the moment it is safe to look around. See the
Post-Event Cleanup Safety and Legal & Insurance Readiness guides
for the full process.

BEFORE YOU CLEAN UP:

- Take photos and video of every damaged room and item, wide shots
  and close-ups, outside and inside.
- After a flood, photograph the water line on walls, furniture,
  and appliances.
- Write down or photograph the make, model, and serial number of
  damaged appliances and electronics.
- Keep small samples of damaged carpet, flooring, drywall, or
  curtains for the insurance adjuster.
- Make a written list of what was damaged as you go, room by room.
- Then throw out anything that is a health risk, such as spoiled
  food and moldy cushions. Photograph first, discard second.
- No photos of how things were before? The IRS suggests sketching
  each room and marking where the furniture and belongings stood,
  and checking old phone photos for items in the background.

THE EXPENSE LIST:

- Save every receipt for repairs, cleanup, supplies, and replaced
  items. Keep them in one envelope taped inside the notebook.
- Log each expense: date, what, where bought, amount.
- Keep contractors' written contracts, invoices, and receipts.
- If you receive disaster assistance, record what you spent it on,
  and keep everything for at least three years.

THE CONTACT LIST:

- Log every call or visit with your insurer, FEMA, the utility, a
  contractor, or a landlord: date, time, the person's name, what
  they told you, and any claim or reference number.
- Report the loss to your insurance company as soon as you can,
  and log when you did.
- After a call that matters, ask for it in writing.

This is record-keeping, not legal advice. For a dispute, talk to
your insurer, your state insurance regulator, or a lawyer.


PAPER, PHONE, OR BOTH
---------------------

Use both, with paper as the main record.

- PAPER needs no battery, works in the dark with a flashlight, and
  can be handed to someone. A pencil never runs dry. Keep the
  notebook in a zip-top bag.
- THE PHONE is for photos and video, which carry their own date
  and time. A voice memo works when your hands are busy: say the
  date and time first.
- BACK UP. Photograph the day's pages each evening. When you get a
  connection, send the photos to yourself or your out-of-town
  contact, or save them to cloud storage.
- PROTECT IT. The log holds health details, names, and claim
  numbers. Keep it with you. Don't write passwords, PINs, or full
  account numbers in it.


COMMON MISTAKES
---------------

- RECONSTRUCTING LATER. Times and order are the first things
  memory loses.
- NO SOURCE. A week on, you can't tell what you saw from what
  someone said.
- CLEANING UP BEFORE PHOTOGRAPHING. The evidence goes out with the
  carpet.
- LOSING RECEIPTS. Envelope, taped inside the cover.
- PHONE ONLY. A dead battery takes the whole record with it.
- WRITING TOO MUCH. If an entry takes more than a minute, shorten
  it.

PRACTICE
--------

10-MINUTE DRILL: Set up the notebook tonight. Copy the
inside-cover block and fill it in. Then run a pretend outage for
ten minutes: write the first line (date, time, place, who is
home), and log each thing you would do, with its time and source.
Include one clock: "power out 18:40, fridge safe until 22:40."
Finish by photographing the page. You now know whether your pencil
is sharp, whether the notebook is where you thought it was, and
how short an entry can be.

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

1. It is 02:00 on the second night of an outage. Your child's
   fever is back. Your partner, now asleep, gave a dose of fever
   medicine "sometime after dinner." Nothing was written down, and
   the child is miserable.

   ANSWER: Don't guess and give another dose. Wake your partner
   and ask what was given, how much, and when, then check the
   label for how long to wait between doses. If you can't work it
   out, call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222 in the US) or a
   pharmacist before giving more. Then start the medicine page
   with what you now know, so nobody has to do this again tomorrow
   night.

2. The flood water has gone down. The carpet and sofa are soaked
   and already smell, the adjuster can't come until Thursday, and
   a neighbor with a truck offers to haul everything to the dump
   right now.

   ANSWER: Take twenty minutes first. Photograph and film each
   room wide and close, including the water line on the walls.
   Record serial numbers from the appliances. Cut and bag a small
   piece of the carpet and keep it. List the items. After that,
   get the wet, moldy things out: flood insurance guidance is to
   discard health-risk items once they are documented, not to live
   with them until the adjuster comes. Tell your insurer what you
   are doing if you can reach them, log the date, and keep the
   dump receipt.

3. Day five. An insurance representative tells you by phone that
   your hotel costs are covered. You are driving and tired, and
   you say thanks and hang up. Two weeks later a different
   representative says there is no record of that.

   ANSWER: The fix is at the time of the first call, which is why
   it belongs in a drill. Pull over or call back the same day, and
   write down the date, time, the person's name, what they said,
   and the claim or reference number. Ask them to confirm it by
   email or letter. A dated note made the same day is far stronger
   than a memory two weeks old. If the answers still conflict, ask
   for a supervisor and, if needed, contact your state's insurance
   regulator.
