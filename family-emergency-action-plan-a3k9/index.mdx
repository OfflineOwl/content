# Family Emergency Action Plan (All Hazards)

> Your family may not be together when a disaster strikes. A written plan answers the questions there's no time for in the moment: how you'll get alerts, where you'll shelter, how you'll leave, and how you'll find each other.

## Start Here: The 30-Minute Version

Sit down with everyone in your household and decide:

1. **An out-of-town contact.** Someone outside your area (ideally out of state) whom everyone checks in with. Long-distance calls and texts often get through when local lines are jammed.
2. **Text first.** Unless you're in danger, send a text instead of calling. Texts need far less network capacity and often get through when calls fail.
3. **Meeting places:** inside (for tornadoes), right outside your home (for fires), outside your neighborhood, and outside your town.
4. **A wallet card** for every person with phone numbers, the out-of-town contact, and meeting places. Phones die and memories fail.
5. **ICE in every phone:** save at least one contact as "ICE" (In Case of Emergency).
6. **How you'll get alerts:** make sure every phone receives Wireless Emergency Alerts, sign up for local alerts, and keep a battery or hand-crank radio.

**In any emergency, if someone's life is in danger, call 911** (or your local emergency number) first. Make sure children know how and when to call 911.

---

## Why You Need a Written Plan

During emergencies, stress makes it hard to think. A written plan:
- Takes the guesswork out when seconds count
- Makes sure everyone knows their role
- Works even when phones and the internet don't
- Gives children and caregivers clear instructions

## Step 1: Identify Your Risks

### Common Emergency Categories

| Category | Examples | Likelihood in Your Area |
|----------|----------|------------------------|
| **Weather** | Hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, winter storms, extreme heat | Varies by region |
| **Geological** | Earthquakes, landslides, tsunamis, volcanoes | Varies by region |
| **Fire** | House fires, wildfires | House fire: everywhere |
| **Infrastructure** | Power outages, water contamination, gas leaks | Everywhere |
| **Hazardous materials** | Chemical spills, train derailments, nuclear plant accidents | Near industry, rail, highways, plants |
| **Health** | Pandemics, outbreaks | Everywhere |
| **Security** | Active threats, terrorism | Varies |

### Assess Your Specific Risks

1. **Research local history:** what disasters have happened in your area?
2. **Check flood maps:** FEMA's Flood Map Service Center shows flood zones.
3. **Ask your local emergency management office** which hazards they plan for.
4. **Consider what's nearby:** chemical plants, rail lines, highways, nuclear plants, fault lines, wildland.
5. **Learn your local warning systems** and evacuation routes.

## Step 2: Set Up Communication

### Out-of-Town Contact

Pick one person **outside your community, ideally in another state**, as the family's central point of contact. In a disaster, it can be easier to reach someone far away than across town because local lines get jammed. Everyone checks in with the same person.

```
FAMILY COMMUNICATION CHAIN
1. Are you in danger? Call 911.
2. Text family directly (group text)
3. No reply? Text or call the out-of-town contact
4. Leave your status: "I'm OK. At library."
5. Check in again at agreed times
```

### Text, Don't Talk

- **Text first.** A text needs far less network capacity than a call and may send automatically once capacity frees up.
- **Keep calls short** and only for vital information.
- **Wait 10 seconds before redialing** a failed call; redialing too fast adds to network congestion.
- Make sure everyone, including your out-of-town contact, knows how to text, or has another way to communicate.
- **Text-to-911** works in many but not all areas. Call if you can; text if you can't.

### Put the Plan in Every Phone

- Enter household members and emergency contacts in every phone
- Save at least one contact as **"ICE"** (In Case of Emergency) so responders can find it
- Create a **group text** of everyone you'd need to reach
- Make sure every phone receives **Wireless Emergency Alerts**, and sign up for alerts from schools, workplaces, and your local government

### Contact Information Card

Make a wallet-sized paper card for each person (phones die and memories fail under stress):

| Information | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| Household members | Names, cell phones, email |
| Out-of-town contact | Name, phones, email |
| Local emergency contact | Name, phone |
| Meeting places | Neighborhood, out-of-neighborhood, out-of-town (with addresses) |
| Schools, childcare, workplaces | Address, emergency phone, pick-up plan |
| Medical | Doctors, pharmacy, allergies, medications, insurance |
| Family password | A word to verify someone is sent by the family |

Also post a copy at home, such as on the refrigerator.

### Communication Methods, in Order

1. **Text messages:** often work when calls don't
2. **Short phone calls**
3. **Social media and safe-check features**, if the internet works
4. **Landline** (a traditional copper line can work in a power outage; many "landlines" are now internet phones that need power)
5. **Two-way radios** (see the Emergency Communications Plan guide)
6. **Meeting places and notes** when nothing else works

### Accessibility

- Include relay service information for household members who are Deaf, hard of hearing, or have speech disabilities
- Keep a TTY, amplified phone, or caption phone with battery backup if you rely on internet-based services
- Plan how to reach family members who don't have phones

## Step 3: Choose Meeting Places

Pick safe, familiar places that everyone, including people with disabilities, can reach. If you have pets or service animals, choose animal-friendly places.

### Indoor (Shelter Spot)
- Where you go for tornadoes or high winds: a small interior room on the lowest floor, a basement, or a safe room
- Know the right room for other dangers too: a sealed room **above ground** for a chemical release, the **basement or center** of the building for radiation

### In Your Neighborhood
- Right outside your home: a big tree, a mailbox, a neighbor's house
- Use for: house fires, gas leaks, any time you need to get out fast

### Outside Your Neighborhood
- A library, community center, house of worship, or family friend's home
- Use when disaster strikes while you're away and you can't get home

### Outside Your Town or City
- A relative's or friend's home, ideally out of the area
- Use when you can't reach the other meeting places, or your community is told to evacuate and you're not together
- Make sure everyone knows the address and how they'd get there (car, transit, paratransit)

---

## Step 4: Know Your School, Childcare, and Work Plans

- Find out the emergency plans of your children's schools and childcare, your workplaces, and any care facility a family member uses
- Know where they would shelter or evacuate to, and how they'll notify you
- Tell schools who is authorized to pick up your children
- **Don't rush to a school during a shelter-in-place emergency** (such as a chemical release or nuclear emergency). Schools have their own plans, and going out can put you and your children at risk. Follow their instructions.
- Children without phones should know to follow instructions from a teacher or other responsible adult

## Step 5: Assign Family Roles

### Sample Role Assignments

| Role | Responsibilities | Primary | Backup |
|------|-----------------|---------|--------|
| **Communications** | Text the out-of-town contact, send the group text about where to meet | Adult 1 | Adult 2 |
| **Supplies** | Grab go-bags and the household kit | Adult 2 | Teen |
| **Children** | Account for and stay with the children | Adult 1 | Adult 2 |
| **Pets** | Secure pets, carriers, and pet supplies | Teen | Adult 2 |
| **Utilities** | Know how to shut off water and electricity, and gas if needed | Adult 1 | Adult 2 |
| **Documents** | Grab the documents kit | Adult 2 | Teen |
| **Helping others** | Assist an older or disabled family member or neighbor | Adult 2 | Teen |

**Gas safety:** turn off gas only if you smell gas, hear a hissing or blowing sound, or officials tell you to. Get everyone out first. Once it's off, only the gas company or a qualified professional should turn it back on.

### Children's Roles (Age Appropriate)

- **Ages 3-5:** stay with their assigned adult; know their full name, address, and a parent's name
- **Ages 6-10:** know how and when to call 911, the family's out-of-town contact, and meeting places; carry their own small go-bag
- **Ages 11+:** help younger siblings and take on specific tasks

## Step 6: Plan for Each Scenario

### Fire

```
1. Alert everyone: yell "FIRE"
2. Get out using the nearest safe exit (know two ways out of every room)
3. Stay low under smoke; feel doors before opening
4. Meet at the neighborhood meeting place
5. Call 911 from outside
6. Never go back inside
```

### Shelter-in-Place

```
1. Get everyone and pets inside
2. Close and lock doors and windows; turn off fans and HVAC
3. Go to the right room:
   - Tornado: lowest floor, interior room
   - Chemical: interior room ABOVE ground, sealed with plastic and tape
   - Radiation: BASEMENT or CENTER of the building, no sealing needed
4. Text the out-of-town contact
5. Listen to the radio and alerts
6. Don't leave until the all-clear
```

### Evacuation Order

```
1. Leave as soon as you're told to
2. Grab go-bags, documents, medications (assigned people)
3. Take pets with you
4. Turn off utilities if officials say to and there's time
5. Follow the routes officials give; don't take shortcuts
6. Text the out-of-town contact with your destination
7. Go to the out-of-town meeting place or a shelter
```

### Family Separated

```
1. Get to the nearest safe place
2. Text the out-of-town contact: where you are, how you are
3. Check in again at agreed times (for example, every 2 hours)
4. Go to the outside-the-neighborhood meeting place if you can't get home
5. Go to the out-of-town meeting place if the area is evacuated
6. Don't search for family in dangerous areas
```

## Step 7: Document Everything

### Emergency Plan Binder Contents

1. Contact list and out-of-town contact
2. Meeting places with addresses and maps
3. Evacuation routes (more than one)
4. Utility shutoff instructions with photos
5. Medical information for each person (conditions, medications, allergies, devices with model and serial numbers)
6. Insurance documents (copies)
7. Pet information (vet, medications, records)
8. School, childcare, and workplace emergency plans
9. Scenario plans

### Digital Backup

- Save encrypted copies to secure cloud storage
- Share copies with your out-of-town contact
- Keep a USB drive in your go-bag

---

## Step 8: Practice Your Plan

### Drill Schedule

| Drill Type | How Often |
|------------|-----------|
| Review the plan and update contacts | At least once a year, or when anything changes |
| Home fire drill | Twice a year |
| Text and call the out-of-town contact; send a group text | Every few months |
| Gather at the indoor and neighborhood meeting places | Every few months |
| Talk through getting to the other meeting places | Once a year |
| Go-bag check | Every 6 months |

### Practice Ideas

- Have each person text the out-of-town contact and practice short messages like "I'm OK. At library."
- Ask everyone to recite key phone numbers from memory, then imagine doing it in an emergency. This is why paper cards matter.
- Make sure children know how and when to call 911

### After Each Drill

- Did everyone know their role?
- Where did things slow down?
- Did we have what we needed?
- What needs updating? Print new cards if anything changed.
