# Off-Grid Lighting Options

> **Flashlights, not candles.** In an outage the dark causes falls, and the flames people light against it cause fires. A few LED lights and a simple battery plan solve both problems.

## Start here

1. **Put a light by every bed tonight.** A flashlight or headlamp within arm's reach, plus one by the main door and one at the top of the stairs. If a fire starts at night, that light is how you get out.
2. **Count your lights.** Aim for one personal light per person (a headlamp is best) and one lantern for each room you would actually live in during an outage.
3. **Test every light and check the spares.** Switch each one on. Write down the battery sizes you need and keep at least one full spare set per light.
4. **Demote the candles.** Decide now that candles and fuel lanterns are the last resort, not the plan. Read the rules below before you ever need them.
5. **Press the test button on your smoke alarms.** Any flame you light later depends on them.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **Something is on fire and it isn't out in seconds.** Get everyone out, close doors behind you, and call from outside. See the Fire Safety During Blackouts guide.
- **A carbon monoxide (CO) alarm sounds, or anyone gets a headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion** while a fuel lamp, lantern or heater is burning. Get everyone into fresh air first, then call.
- **Someone swallows lamp oil, torch fuel or kerosene and is coughing, choking or struggling to breathe.** Coughing means the oil may have reached the lungs. Do not make them vomit.
- **A child swallows a button or coin battery** (from a flameless candle, key-ring light or remote). This is an emergency even if the child seems fine: a battery stuck in the throat can do severe damage within about 2 hours. Go to an emergency room now.

For any swallowed fuel or battery with no symptoms yet, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 (US, free, 24 hours). The US National Battery Ingestion Hotline is 1-800-498-8666. Outside the US, call your local emergency number or poison center.

## Why battery light comes first

The fire agencies agree, and they are blunt about it. The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) says to use a flashlight, not a candle, during a power outage. The U.S. Fire Administration says never to use candles for emergency lighting. The CDC says to use battery-powered flashlights and lanterns rather than candles, gas lanterns or torches.

The numbers behind that advice:

- US fire departments were called to about 21 home candle fires a day (2014 to 2018 average).
- Three in five of those fires started because something that can burn was too close to the candle.
- When NFPA studied fatal candle fires, the home was without power in about a quarter of the cases where the power status was known.

An LED light has no flame, makes no fumes, stays cool, and runs for days on a set of batteries.

## Pick the right light for the job

| Light | Best for | Good to know |
|-------|----------|--------------|
| **Headlamp** | Anything needing two hands: cooking, first aid, carrying a child, repairs, stairs | The most useful light you can own. One per person. |
| **Handheld flashlight** | Moving around, checking outside, signaling | A narrow beam reaches far but lights a room poorly. |
| **LED lantern** | Lighting a table or a whole room | Hang it high or stand it under a white ceiling to spread the light. |
| **Night lights, flameless candles, glow sticks** | Marking stairs, hallways and the bathroom all night | Very low drain. Glow sticks are single-use and can't be switched off. |
| **Solar or hand-crank light** | Long outages when batteries run low | Slow to recharge, and cranking gives little light for the effort. A backup. |
| **Phone flashlight** | The first minute, until you reach a real light | Drains the battery you need for calls and alerts. |

Buy lights that take the same battery size where you can (AA or AAA are easiest to find), so every spare fits every light.

## How much light: lumens and hours

**Lumens** measure how much light a lamp puts out. For scale, an old 60-watt incandescent bulb gives about 800 lumens and a 100-watt bulb about 1,600.

You need far less than that in an outage. A lantern on a low setting of a few tens of lumens looks feeble next to a ceiling light, but once your eyes adjust it is enough to eat, talk and move around by. Save the bright settings for jobs that need them: first aid, reading small print, a repair, checking outside.

**Brightness costs hours.** On almost every light, the brightest mode empties the batteries many times faster than the lowest. The lowest mode that does the job is the right one.

**Read the runtime on the package with care.** Under the flashlight industry's FL 1 standard, runtime is measured until the light has faded to 10 percent of its starting brightness. A light sold as "40 hours" may spend much of that time quite dim. Cold and old batteries shorten it further. For planning, treat the package figure as best case and count on about half.

### Plan it in three steps

1. **Hours per night.** In midwinter it may be dark for 14 hours, but you are only awake for 5 or 6 of them.
2. **Nights.** Plan for at least 3. A week is better in places with ice storms or hurricanes.
3. **Battery sets.** Hours per night, times nights, divided by the runtime you can count on.

**Worked example.** Your lantern's package says 100 hours on low with 3 D cells. Count on 50. You want it on 5 hours a night for 7 nights: 35 hours. One set of batteries covers the week, and one spare set covers a second week. Each headlamp says 40 hours on low with 3 AAA cells. Count on 20. At 2 hours a night for 7 nights you need 14 hours: one set in the lamp and one spare set each is plenty.

## Batteries and recharging

- **Replace all the cells in a light together,** with the same type. Follow the markings in the battery compartment.
- **Store spares in their packaging,** in a cool, dry place indoors, not loose in a drawer where metal objects can short them.
- **Check stored lights every few months.** Alkaline cells left in for years can leak and ruin the contacts, and a light that doesn't switch on is no light.
- **Rechargeable lights and power banks use lithium-ion cells.** Charge them on a hard, flat surface, never on a bed or sofa, with the cable that came with them. Stop using any that swell, get hot, smell odd or make popping sounds. Store them at room temperature, not in a hot or freezing car.
- **A power bank recharges USB lights and phones.** Top everything up when a storm is forecast.
- **Solar garden path lights make free night lights.** Leave them in the sun by day and carry them indoors at dusk. Any solar light needs hours of direct sun, and gets less in winter and cloud.
- **Don't raid the smoke alarms** for batteries. Ever.

Used batteries: lithium and rechargeable types go to a battery recycling or household hazardous waste point, not the trash. Tape the terminals first.

## Use the light you have well

- **Light the fall hazards first:** stairs, the route to the bathroom, and the way out. A dim light left on all night in those spots matters more than a bright one in the living room.
- **Live in one lit room** in the evening instead of lighting the house.
- **Do the fiddly jobs in daylight:** cooking, cleaning up, sorting medicines, refueling anything.
- **Bounce the light.** A lantern aimed at a white ceiling or wall lights a room more evenly than one sitting on the floor.
- **Give each child their own light** or a glow stick. It keeps them calm and easy to find.

## Candles: the last resort

If you have truly run out of battery light and use candles anyway, every one of these rules applies:

- **Keep candles at least 12 inches (30 cm) from anything that can burn:** curtains, bedding, paper, furniture, hair and sleeves.
- **Never in bedrooms, bathrooms or anywhere people sleep.** Candles in bedrooms cause a disproportionate share of candle fires and deaths.
- **Never leave a burning candle.** Put it out when you leave the room or go to bed. Falling asleep is a factor in about 1 in 10 candle fires.
- **Use a sturdy, heat-resistant holder** that can't tip, on a clear, level surface, and shield the flame with a glass chimney or jar.
- **Don't walk with a lit candle.** Carry a flashlight to move around.
- **Keep candles, matches and lighters out of reach of children and pets.**
- **Put the candle out before it burns down to the holder.**
- **Never use a candle or any flame where medical oxygen is in use.**

Skip homemade lamps made from cooking oil, shortening or butter in a jar. They are an open dish of hot fuel with no tested holder, easy to knock over and hard to put out. And a candle under a flowerpot is not a heater; it is a candle with something hot and heavy balanced over it.

## Oil lamps and fuel lanterns

**Wick lamps (lamp oil or kerosene)** carry every candle risk plus a reservoir of fuel:

- Burn only the fuel the lamp was made for. Never gasoline, and never a mix.
- Fill the lamp when it is cold, away from any flame, and wipe up spills before lighting.
- Follow all the candle rules: stable surface, 12 inches clear, never unattended, never while you sleep.
- Have a working CO alarm, and let some fresh air into the room.
- **Lamp oil is a poison hazard.** Colored oil looks like juice and clear oil looks like water. Keep it in its original container, locked away from children.

**Pressurized camping lanterns (propane, butane or white gas) are for outdoors only.** They burn very hot and give off carbon monoxide. The CDC says never to use a lantern or portable camping stove inside a home, tent or camper, and the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says never to use fuel-burning lanterns while sleeping in tents, campers or vehicles. Use them on a porch or in the yard if you use them at all.

Store all lamp fuel the way the Fuel Storage Safety guide describes: outside the living space, in its proper container.

## People who need extra thought

- **Older adults and anyone unsteady on their feet:** light the route from bed to bathroom all night, and keep a headlamp on the bedside table.
- **People who use medical oxygen:** battery light only. No flame in the home.
- **Young children:** no flame within reach, and tape or screw shut the battery doors on coin-cell lights. The liquid in a chewed glow stick is an irritant, not a serious poison: rinse the mouth, skin or eyes with water.
- **People who are deaf or hard of hearing:** light the whole room well enough to see faces and hands, so conversation still works.
- **Pets:** a wagging tail or a jumping cat is enough to tip a candle.

## Lighting kit checklist

- [ ] Headlamp or flashlight for every person
- [ ] LED lantern for each room you would use (at least one)
- [ ] At least one spare set of batteries per light, more for long outages
- [ ] Night lights, flameless candles or glow sticks for stairs and the bathroom
- [ ] Charged power bank and cables for USB lights and phones
- [ ] A solar or hand-crank light as backup
- [ ] Working smoke alarms on every level, and a CO alarm if you own anything that burns fuel
- [ ] Fire extinguisher you know how to use
- [ ] If you keep candles anyway: sturdy holders, and matches stored out of children's reach

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a lights-out evening. After dark, leave the power on but switch off every light in the house. Each person has to reach a working flashlight or headlamp from wherever they are, without help, then walk together to the main exit, the bathroom and the breaker panel. Don't use stairs until you have a light in your hand. Time it, then fix what you found: a dead battery, a light nobody could find, a dark stairway that needs a night light.

**What would you do?**

1. *The power has been out for two hours. Your only flashlight is fading, there is a drawer full of candles, and the children want to read in their bedroom.*

   **Answer:** No candles in the bedroom, and none left with children. Bring them to the room you are in and read there, by the lantern or a headlamp, and swap in fresh batteries if you have them. If candles really are all you have left, they stay in one room with an awake adult, in sturdy holders, 12 inches from anything that burns, and they go out before anyone goes to bed.

2. *Day three of an ice-storm outage. The batteries are nearly gone. There is a propane camping lantern in the garage, far brighter than anything else you own.*

   **Answer:** It stays outside. A fuel-burning lantern gives off carbon monoxide and runs hot enough to start a fire, and "just for an hour with a window open" is how people get poisoned. Use it on the porch if you need it. Indoors, stretch what you have: one lit room, lowest setting, chores by daylight, solar path lights brought in at dusk, and a recharge from a power bank.

3. *You are refilling an oil lamp when the phone rings. When you come back, your toddler is holding the open bottle and coughing a little, but seems cheerful.*

   **Answer:** Treat the cough as the warning it is. Lamp oil is slippery and easily slides into the lungs, where it can cause pneumonia that shows up hours later. Do not make the child vomit. A coughing child needs to be seen at an emergency room, so go now or call 911 if breathing is difficult, and call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222 in the US) on the way. Take the bottle with you.

## Quick Reference

```
OFF-GRID LIGHTING

FIRST CHOICE: LED, every time
- Headlamp or flashlight per person
- Lantern per room in use
- Light by every bed and the exit
- Night light on stairs + bathroom

RUNTIME
- Lowest mode that does the job
- Package hours = best case;
  plan on half
- Hours/night x nights / runtime
  = battery sets. Keep a spare set.

CANDLES = LAST RESORT
- 12 in (30 cm) from anything
  that burns
- Never in bedrooms
- Never unattended, out before bed
- Sturdy holder, no carrying lit
- Never near medical oxygen

FUEL LANTERNS (propane, white gas)
- OUTDOORS ONLY: carbon monoxide

EMERGENCY (US numbers)
- Fire or CO alarm: get out, 911
- Swallowed lamp oil + cough: ER,
  do NOT induce vomiting
- Swallowed button battery: ER now
- Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222
```
