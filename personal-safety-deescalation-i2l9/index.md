# Personal Safety & De-Escalation

> **The goal is to leave, not to win.** De-escalation means using your voice, your distance and your patience to lower the temperature of a confrontation long enough for you to get away or for help to arrive. Being right does not matter. Getting home does.

## Do this now

If an angry person is in front of you:

1. **Find your exit** and keep the way to it clear. Don't let yourself be backed into a corner.
2. **Make space.** Step back out of reach and stand slightly to one side, not squared up in front of them.
3. **Show your hands.** Keep them down, open and visible. Move slowly.
4. **Slow down and quiet down.** Speak more slowly and more softly than they do.
5. **Listen, and say back what you hear:** "I can see that you are upset."
6. **Leave as soon as you can.** If they threaten to hurt you or show a weapon, stop talking, get away, and call 911.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **Someone shows a weapon or threatens to hurt you or anyone else.**
- **Someone is injured.**
- **A person blocks your way out, follows you, or won't let you leave.**
- **Someone talks about killing themselves or another person and has the means at hand.**

**For a mental health or suicidal crisis that is not an immediate danger to life, call or text 988** (US), the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. It is free, confidential and open 24 hours a day, every day, with chat at 988lifeline.org, services in Spanish, and options for people who are deaf or hard of hearing. You can call about someone else.

**If the person frightening you is a partner or family member,** the US National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788, any time.

Other countries have their own emergency numbers and crisis lines. Find yours now and save them in your phone.

## When to talk and when to go

De-escalation works on people who are angry, frightened, humiliated or overwhelmed. That is most confrontations. It does not work on someone who has already decided to hurt you, and it is not your job to find out which kind of person you are facing by staying close.

The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), which publishes de-escalation guidance for ordinary workers, puts it in three rules:

- **Your safety comes first.** Keep a safe distance, and avoid being alone with someone who is combative. If violence looks imminent, remove yourself.
- **Know your limits.** Some people are better at this than others. Sometimes the best intervention is getting someone else.
- **Get help.** If things are escalating, call security or the police and move to a safe place.

| It may be working | Stop talking and leave |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| Their voice drops or slows | They threaten to hurt you or someone else |
| They start explaining instead of shouting | They show or reach for a weapon |
| They step back or sit down | They keep closing the distance as you back away |
| They accept an option you offered | Clenched jaw or balled fists, with pacing or shaking |
| They let you move toward the door | They move to block your exit |

One sign alone means little. People have bad days, and someone pacing and loud may be asking for help in the only way they can manage. Several signs together, or any threat or weapon, mean go.

## Start with your own body

A person who is losing control reads your body before your words. And your own alarm system is running too: fast heart, tight voice, tunnel vision.

- **Breathe out slowly** before you speak. Tell yourself something simple, such as "I can handle this."
- **Keep a relaxed, alert stance off to the side** of the person. Standing rigid and square in front of someone reads as a challenge.
- **Hands down, open and visible** the whole time. Don't point. Don't cross your arms.
- **Move slowly and deliberately.** No sudden gestures, no pacing.
- **Keep a neutral, attentive face.** Don't fake a smile.
- **Respect their space and don't touch them.** Crisis trainers suggest at least 1.5 to 3 feet (0.5 to 1 m) even with someone you know. With a stranger, stay well beyond arm's reach.
- **Leave them a way out too.** People who feel cornered are much harder to calm.

## Then your voice and words

**How you say it** matters more than what you say. Keep your tone calm, your volume low, and your pace slow. Watch which words you stress: "What do YOU want?" lands very differently from an even "What do you want?"

**Listen first.** Give your full attention, nod, ask questions, and don't interrupt or change the subject. Listen for the feeling under the words (fear, disrespect, loss) and name it without judging. Their feelings are real to them whether or not you think they are justified.

| Instead of | Try |
|------------|-----|
| "Calm down." | "I can see that you are upset." |
| "I can't help you." | "I want to help. What can I do?" |
| "I know how you feel." | "I understand that you feel..." |
| "Come with me." | "May I speak with you?" |
| "You're wrong." | "Help me understand what happened." |

More things that work:

- **Use "please" and "thank you."** Courtesy costs nothing and is hard to fight with.
- **Don't take the bait.** If they insult you or challenge you ("What are you going to do about it?"), ignore the challenge but not the person. Steer back to the problem.
- **Offer choices,** simple and reasonable ones, with the good option first: "We can sort this out over there, or I can step away and give you some room."
- **Let silence work.** A pause gives them time to think. Don't rush to fill it.
- **Give them time to decide.** Pressure raises stress. A few moments lets it fall.
- **Thin out the audience.** If you can, move the conversation away from onlookers, or ask others to step back. People climb down more easily when nobody is watching.

Things that make it worse: arguing about the facts, raising your voice to match theirs, threats, sarcasm, ultimatums, and "calm down," which almost everyone hears as "your feelings don't count."

A way to remember it is **SLOW: Space, Listen, Options, Way out.**

## Common situations

**A stranger shouting at you in public.** You don't owe a reply. Keep walking toward people, light and open businesses. If you must answer, make it short and neutral ("Sorry, can't help") and keep moving.

**A dispute in a line, a shelter or a parking lot.** In emergencies people are tired, scared and short of things. A place in a queue, a parking space or a can of fuel is never worth an injury. Give it up out loud ("Go ahead, it's yours") and leave.

**Road rage.** AAA's advice is to avoid eye contact, make no gestures, and not answer aggression with aggression. Stay in your locked car. If you feel at risk, don't drive home: drive to a police station, fire station or hospital and call 911. See the Emergency Defensive Driving guide.

**Someone at your door.** Talk through the closed, locked door. You don't have to open it to be polite. If they won't leave or try to get in, call 911. See the Lockdown Procedures guide.

**Someone you know.** Arguments with neighbors and relatives escalate because both sides want to win. Suggest a break ("Let's pick this up tomorrow") and physically leave the room. Come back to it when everyone has slept and eaten.

**Violence at home.** De-escalation is a tool for a moment, not a fix for a person who repeatedly frightens or hurts you. That pattern is not your fault and not yours to manage alone. The National Domestic Violence Hotline (above) helps with safety planning.

**Children nearby.** Move them out of the room or behind you first, calmly, before you say anything else.

## A person in a mental health crisis

Someone who is confused, hearing or seeing things, panicking or talking about suicide is usually frightened, not hostile. The same tools apply, with a few additions:

- **Fewer people, less noise, more space.** One calm person speaking is better than three.
- **Don't argue with what they believe or perceive.** You don't have to agree. "That sounds frightening" is true and enough.
- **If you think they may be considering suicide, ask directly:** "Are you thinking about suicide?" The US National Institute of Mental Health says studies show that asking does not increase suicidal thoughts or behavior.
- **Follow the five steps the 988 Lifeline teaches:** ask, be there, help keep them safe, help them connect, follow up. Listening without judgment and staying with them are the core of it. Offer to call or text 988 together.
- **If there is immediate danger, call 911.** Say clearly that this is a mental health crisis, and ask whether responders trained in crisis intervention are available.

See the Mental Health Crisis guide for more.

## If it does not work

You tried, and it is getting worse. Talking is over.

1. **Go.** Move toward people, light and staffed places. Put things between you: a car, a counter, a door you can lock.
2. **Be loud and specific.** "Call 911" works better than a scream. If others are in danger, warn them in a loud, clear voice as you move.
3. **Call 911** as soon as you have distance. Say where you are first.
4. **If you can't get away, get behind a locked or blocked door** and call from there.

Official US guidance for an attack in a public place is to get away first, hide if you can't, and treat physical resistance only as a last resort. This guide does not teach fighting skills. Laws about self-defense vary widely by place; for those questions, check your local law.

**Afterward:** tell the police what happened while you remember details such as what the person looked like, what they said, and which way they went. Shaking, crying or feeling sick once you are safe is a normal stress reaction. Talk to someone you trust, and see the Mental Health Crisis guide if it doesn't settle.

## People who need extra thought

- **Children and teens:** teach the short version. Walk away, go to a trusted adult, and tell. They are never required to be polite to an adult who frightens them.
- **Older adults and people with limited mobility:** leaving fast may not be possible, so leave early, at the first sign of tension, and keep a phone or personal alarm in reach.
- **People who are deaf or hard of hearing, or who don't share a language with the other person:** open hands, distance and a calm face still carry the message. Find out whether texting 911 works where you live.
- **People with dementia or brain injury who become agitated:** a quiet room, a slow voice, one simple request at a time, and no arguing about what is real.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Rehearse out loud, alone or with a partner. Stand slightly side-on with your hands low and open, and say the "Try" lines from the table in a slow, quiet voice. Hearing yourself say them once makes them far easier to find under stress. Then practice your exit line, "I'm going to step away now," while actually stepping back. If you have a partner, take turns playing the upset person, without touching and without shouting in anyone's face.

**What would you do?**

1. *There is a long line for fuel after a storm. A man accuses you of cutting in, steps in close and starts shouting. You know you were there first, and people are watching.*

   **Answer:** Don't argue about who was first. That argument can't be won, and the audience makes it harder for him to back down. Step back, hands open, and say calmly, "I can see you're upset. Go ahead." Then let him have the spot or leave the station. If he follows you to your car, lock the doors, drive to a police station or busy place, and call 911. Ten minutes in a fuel line is a cheap price.

2. *Your neighbor has been pacing his yard since the flood took his house. Tonight he tells you there is no point in going on. He is not threatening anyone.*

   **Answer:** Don't brush it off and don't tell him to cheer up. Ask him directly whether he is thinking about suicide; asking does not put the idea in his head. Listen without judging, and stay with him. Offer to call or text 988 together, and help him reach family or friends. If he has a plan and the means at hand, or you can't keep him safe, call 911. Check on him again tomorrow.

3. *You have been talking quietly with an angry stranger for a few minutes, and he seemed to be settling. Then he says, "You're going to be sorry," and moves between you and the door.*

   **Answer:** Stop talking. A threat plus blocking your exit is the signal that de-escalation has ended, even though it was working a moment ago. Don't try one more calming line. Get away by any other route, put a barrier between you, be loud ("Call 911"), and call as soon as you have distance. If there is no other way out, get behind a door you can lock or block and call from there.

O - Options: offer simple choices
W - Way out: yours AND theirs

BODY
- Side-on, relaxed, not squared up
- Hands down, open, visible
- Slow movements, neutral face
- Don't touch, don't point

- Lower, slower, softer
- "I can see that you are upset."
- "I want to help. What can I do?"
