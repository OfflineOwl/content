# Home Security During Civil Unrest

> **Inside, away from the windows, ready to leave.** When unrest is near your home, your job is not to guard the building. It is to keep everyone out of sight and unhurt, follow official instructions, and get out early if staying stops being safe.

## Do this now

If a disturbance is close to your home:

1. **Get everyone inside,** pets included. Do not go out to watch, film or argue.
2. **Lock doors and windows, close blinds and curtains,** and move everyone away from windows and street-facing rooms.
3. **Get official information.** Check local alerts, the city or police channels, and local radio or TV. Obey curfews and any instruction from police or emergency officials.
4. **Get ready to leave.** Shoes on. Keys, phone, charger, medications, IDs and an overnight bag by the exit that faces away from the trouble.
5. **Leave if staying feels unsafe and a route away from the trouble is open.** Go early, calmly, and away from the crowd. Nothing in the house is worth a person.
6. **Text your household and your out-of-town contact** to say where you are and what you plan to do.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number: 112 in the EU, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia) if:

- **Someone is trying to get into your home.**
- **There is fire or smoke in your building or the one next to it.** Get out first, then call.
- **Someone is injured,** or you can see someone in danger.
- **You are trapped** and cannot leave safely.

Give your address first, then what is happening. Lines may be busy during unrest, so stay on the line. If speaking would put you in danger, many US areas accept a text to 911: put your location and the emergency in the first message. If texting is not available where you are, you should get an automatic reply telling you to call.

Do not call 911 for news or to ask what is going on. Use alerts, radio and official channels for that.

## Stay or go

Staying inside is the right first move in most cases. Local emergency agencies say the same thing in different words: stay away from the area, lock up, keep clear of windows and doors, stay informed, and know your exit routes in case you have to leave.

| Stay in when | Leave when |
|--------------|-----------|
| The trouble is a few blocks away or passing by | Officials tell you to evacuate |
| A curfew or a stay-indoors instruction is in force | There is fire or heavy smoke in or next to your building |
| Your building is not a target | Your building is being broken into or attacked |
| Every route out leads through the crowd | You no longer feel safe and a route away from the trouble is clear |

**A curfew** is an order from public safety officials to stay indoors between set hours, usually overnight. Follow it. It never means you must stay in a burning or unsafe building. If you have to leave to stay alive, leave, call 911, and do what the first officers you meet tell you.

**If you go:**

- Go on foot or by car in the direction away from the disturbance. Head for a friend or relative outside the area, or one of the meeting places in your Family Emergency Action Plan.
- Take one bag. Lock up behind you.
- Tell your out-of-town contact where you are going and when you arrive.

**If you stay:** keep the bag by the door and keep checking. The decision is not final.

## Where to wait inside

- **Pick a room away from the street,** or an interior hallway. Sit below window level.
- **Keep blinds and curtains closed** so no one can see who or what is inside.
- **Keep everyone together,** dressed, with shoes on. Charge phones and power banks while the power is on.
- **In an apartment building,** stay out of the lobby and off the balconies, do not let people you don't know into the building, and follow the building management's emergency plan.
- **Keep two exits usable.** If you move furniture against the front door, make sure it can be shifted in seconds and that a second way out stays clear.

## Fire is the danger that makes you leave

A locked door protects you from most things that happen in the street. It does not protect you from fire. Ready.gov says a home fire can become life-threatening in about two minutes.

- If your building, or one attached to it, is on fire: **get out, stay out, and call 911** from outside.
- Know two ways out of every room. During unrest, plan on the exit that opens away from the crowd.
- Stay low under smoke. Feel doors before opening them. If a door is hot, use your other way out.
- Security bars and extra locks must open from the inside without a key or tool.
- Test your smoke alarms now, before you need them.

## Tear gas and smoke drifting in

Police may use riot control agents such as tear gas or pepper spray. They irritate the eyes, nose, throat, lungs and skin. The CDC says the effects usually last 15 to 30 minutes once a person is away from the source and cleaned off.

- **If it is outside:** close windows and doors, and turn off fans, air conditioners and any ventilation that pulls in outside air.
- **If you are caught in it outdoors:** leave the area for fresh air. Go to higher ground, because these agents form a cloud that hangs low.
- **If the agent is inside your home,** get out into clean air.
- **Clean off:** take off clothing that has the agent on it. Cut off anything you would have to pull over your head. Wash skin with plenty of soap and water. If your eyes burn or your vision is blurry, rinse them with plain water for 10 to 15 minutes. Take out contact lenses and do not put them back in. Wash glasses with soap and water.
- Seal the clothing in a plastic bag, and put that bag inside a second one.
- **Call 911** for trouble breathing or symptoms that do not ease. Poison Help in the US is 1-800-222-1222.

See the Decontamination Basics guide for the full washing steps. Heavy exposure in a closed space is more dangerous than a whiff outdoors.

## If someone comes to your door or onto your property

- **Do not open the door.** Speak through it, or stay silent and out of sight.
- **Do not go outside to confront, argue with or film people.** Official guidance for bystanders is the same everywhere: don't become a spectator, and don't get involved by arguing or trying to interfere.
- **If people are damaging your car, fence or yard:** stay inside, call 911, and note descriptions if you can do it without standing at a window. Damage can be photographed, repaired and claimed on insurance later. An injury cannot.
- **If someone is trying to get in:** move everyone away from that entry. Leave by another exit if you can do it safely. If you cannot, go to a room that locks, lock it, call 911 and stay on the line. See the Lockdown Procedures and Safe Room Setup guides.
- **If you come face to face with someone:** stay calm, keep your voice low, do not threaten, and give them a clear way to leave. See the Personal Safety and De-Escalation guide.

Laws on defending yourself and your home vary widely between states and countries. Learn what applies where you live before you ever need to know. This guide does not give legal advice.

## If you are caught outside or in a car

- **On foot:** move to the edge of the crowd and away from it. Protect your head. If you fall, curl up to protect your head and neck, and get back on your feet as soon as you can. Stay calm and non-confrontational.
- **In a car:** never drive through a crowd. If a crowd is in your path, turn down the nearest side road or turn around, and drive away calmly.
- **If you cannot drive away:** park, lock the car and walk to a safe place. If there is no time for that, turn off the engine, lock the doors, and stay calm without showing hostility.
- Go home only if home is away from the trouble. Otherwise go to a friend, relative or meeting place and text your household.

The Crowd Safety and Avoidance guide covers crowds in more detail.

## Neighbors and rumors

Good neighbors make a street safer during unrest, as long as they stay within clear limits. One city's guidance for block clubs and neighborhood watch groups sums it up: share information, report suspicious activity, and look out for each other. Do not display or use weapons, do not take the law into your own hands, and do not spread rumors.

- Swap phone numbers now and agree on one group chat.
- Check on older neighbors and people who live alone by phone or text.
- Before you act on a social media post or pass it on, look for the same information from an official source.

## Prepare on a calm day

- [ ] Sign up for your city or county alerts, and keep a battery or hand-crank radio
- [ ] Keep enough food, water and medication at home that nobody has to go out for a few days
- [ ] Pack an overnight bag you can grab when unrest is threatened
- [ ] Plan routes to work, school and out of town that avoid places where crowds gather
- [ ] Agree on meeting places and an out-of-town contact (see the Family Emergency Action Plan guide)
- [ ] Check that your home, renters and car insurance are current
- [ ] Film a walk-through of every room for insurance, and store a copy away from home
- [ ] Reinforce exterior doors and check every lock (see the Home Hardening Basics guide)
- [ ] Test smoke alarms and walk both exit routes
- [ ] Take a first aid class

## People who need extra thought

- **Children:** keep them away from windows and from a constant stream of news video. Tell them plainly what is happening and what the plan is.
- **Older adults and people with limited mobility:** leaving takes longer, so decide earlier. If unrest is expected, consider staying with family or friends elsewhere before it starts.
- **Medical needs:** keep a week of medication and spare batteries for devices at home, and pack them first.
- **Pets:** bring them in, and keep carriers and leashes by the door.

## Afterward

- Wait until officials say the area is safe and any curfew has been lifted before you go out to look.
- Check on everyone in the household, then on neighbors. Get first aid or medical care where it is needed.
- Wear sturdy shoes and gloves around broken glass.
- Photograph or film any damage before you clean up. Report it to the police and to your insurer, and keep receipts for repairs.
- Text family and friends to say you are safe.
- Fear, anger and poor sleep are common for days afterward. If they do not ease, talk to someone. In the US you can call or text 988 at any hour. Other countries have their own crisis lines.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a back-door drill. Gather the household and agree on three things out loud: which room you would wait in (away from the street), which exit you would use if the trouble was at the front, and where you would go. Then time it. Everyone puts on shoes, picks up keys, phone, charger and medications, and meets at that exit. Open the door and step out, so you know the lock, the gate and the path all work in the dark. Fix what slowed you down.

**What would you do?**

1. *You hear shouting and breaking glass two blocks away. Neighbors are out on the sidewalk filming, and your teenager wants to walk down and see what is going on.*

   **Answer:** Everyone stays in. Going to watch turns a bystander into part of the crowd, with nothing gained that a local alert or radio report won't tell you. Lock up, close the blinds, move to a room away from the street, and put shoes and the overnight bag by the back door in case the disturbance moves your way.

2. *A curfew started at 8 p.m. At 9 you smell smoke, and you can see flames in the shop that shares a wall with your building.*

   **Answer:** Leave now. Fire outranks a curfew, and it can become deadly in about two minutes. Take people, pets and the bag, use the exit away from the crowd, and call 911 once you are out. Walk away from the disturbance and do what the first officers you meet tell you. Do not go back in for belongings.

3. *From an upstairs room you see two people smashing the windows of your car in the driveway. You are furious, and a baseball bat is right by the door.*

   **Answer:** Stay inside and call 911. Walking out starts a fight, possibly against more people than you can see, over something insurance can replace. Keep the household away from the windows and note what you can for the police from where you are. Photograph the damage when the street is quiet. The car is not worth a life, yours or anyone else's.
