Cooling Strategies in Extreme Heat

Air conditioning is the best protection in a heat wave. When you don't have it, these steps can lower your risk, but they have limits. If your home stays above 90°F (32°C) or anyone shows signs of heat illness, get to a cooler place.

Do this now

  1. Get to air conditioning if you can: a cooling center, library, mall, or a friend's home, at least for the hottest hours. In the US, call 2-1-1 or your local health department to find cooling centers.
  2. Take a cool shower or bath, or wet your skin and clothing and let it evaporate.
  3. Drink water regularly, before you feel thirsty.
  4. Use a fan only if the room is below 90°F (32°C). Above that, a fan blowing on you can make you hotter.
  5. Watch for heat illness. Confusion, a very high temperature, or passing out means heat stroke: call 911 and cool the person immediately (see "Heat Illness" below).

Fans: Know the Limit

  • Below 90°F (32°C) indoors: fans help by moving air over your skin. Wetting your skin first helps more.
  • Above 90°F (32°C) indoors: don't point a fan at yourself. It can blow hot air on you and raise your body temperature. Get to a cooler place instead.
  • Use fans to move air, not just to blow it on you: at night, put a fan in a window to pull in cooler outside air, or to push hot air out.
  • Keep a thermometer indoors so you know which side of 90°F you're on.

Keep the Heat Out

Windows and Sun

  • Close curtains, blinds, or shades on windows that get sun during the day. Light-colored or reflective coverings work best, and coverings outside the glass (awnings, shutters, shade cloth) block more heat than ones inside.
  • Keep windows closed during the day when it's hotter outside than inside.
  • Open windows at night and early morning when the outside air is cooler.

Ventilation

  • When it's cooler outside, open windows on opposite sides of the house to get a cross-breeze.
  • Opening a high window or upstairs window lets hot air escape, while a low or downstairs window lets cooler air in.
  • Close doors to hot rooms you aren't using.

Reduce Heat Indoors

  • Avoid using the oven and stove. Eat cold meals, or cook outdoors, away from the house.
  • Turn off lights and electronics you don't need. They all give off heat.
  • Run hot appliances like dryers and dishwashers at night, or not at all.

Cool Your Body

Water Cooling

Water on your skin is one of the most effective ways to cool down without air conditioning:

  • Cool showers or baths. Repeat as often as you need.
  • Wet cloths on your skin, especially the neck, face, armpits, and groin. Rewet them when they warm up.
  • Spray bottle: mist your skin and let it evaporate.
  • Damp clothing: wet a T-shirt, bandana, or hat and wear it.
  • Soak your feet in cool water.
  • In low humidity, water evaporates faster and cools you more. In high humidity it works less well, so cool water contact (baths, showers, wet cloths) matters more.

Drink Enough

  • Drink water regularly. Don't wait until you're thirsty.
  • Limit drinks high in sugar, salt, caffeine, or alcohol.
  • Light yellow or clear urine usually means you're drinking enough.
  • If your doctor limits your fluids or you take water pills, ask how much to drink in hot weather.
  • If you're sweating heavily for several hours, add sports drinks with electrolytes. Don't drink more than about 48 oz (1.5 quarts) an hour.

Dress for It

  • Lightweight, loose, light-colored clothing
  • A wide-brimmed hat and sunscreen outdoors

Pace Yourself

  • Do chores and errands in the coolest part of the day, early morning or evening.
  • Rest often. Avoid heavy exertion in the heat.
  • Eat lighter, cooler meals. Fruits and vegetables with lots of water help.

Sleeping in the Heat

  • Sleep in the coolest room in your home. In a multi-story home, that's usually the lowest floor.
  • Use only a light cotton sheet, or none.
  • Take a cool shower before bed, and keep a spray bottle or damp cloth nearby.
  • Open windows at night if it's cooler outside, and use a window fan to pull in the cooler air.
  • If your bedroom stays above 90°F at night, don't rely on a fan. Find somewhere cooler to sleep if you can, especially for older adults, babies, and anyone who is ill.

Protect People at Higher Risk

Older Adults

  • Check on them at least twice a day during a heat wave, in person if you can.
  • They may not feel thirsty or notice they're overheating. Remind them to drink, and offer cool drinks.
  • Many medicines affect how the body handles heat. Ask a doctor or pharmacist.
  • Help them get to a cooling center if their home is hot.

Babies and Children

  • Dress them in light, loose clothing, and keep them in shade or air conditioning.
  • Offer fluids often. Avoid very cold or very sugary drinks. For babies, ask a pediatrician how to keep them hydrated.
  • Limit outdoor play to cooler times of day.
  • Never leave a child in a parked car, even for a minute. A car can heat up almost 20°F in 10 minutes, even with a window cracked.

Others at Higher Risk

  • Pregnant people
  • People with heart disease, lung disease or asthma, diabetes, kidney disease, or mental illness
  • People who take medicines that affect sweating, fluids, or alertness
  • People who live alone, have disabilities, or have no air conditioning or cool place to go
  • Outdoor workers and athletes

Pets

  • Give plenty of water and shade, and bring them inside if you can.
  • Watch for heavy panting, drooling, weakness, or collapse.
  • Never leave pets in a parked car.

Heat Illness

These signs and first aid steps match the Extreme Heat Survival guide, which has full details.

Heat Stroke: Call 911

Signs: body temperature of 103°F (39.4°C) or higher; confusion, slurred speech, strange behavior, or passing out; hot, red skin that can be dry or sweaty; fast, strong pulse; headache; dizziness; nausea.

Sweating doesn't rule out heat stroke. Confusion is the key warning sign.

  1. Call 911.
  2. Move them to a cooler place and remove extra clothing.
  3. Cool them immediately. Best: put their body, neck down, in cool or cold water (a tub or kiddie pool) for about 15 minutes or until they're alert again, supporting their head. If you can't, use a cold shower or hose, ice-water-soaked sheets or towels, wet skin plus fanning, and ice packs on the neck, armpits, and groin.
  4. Don't give them anything to drink or any aspirin or acetaminophen.
  5. Stay with them. If they're unconscious but breathing, lay them on their side. If they stop breathing, start CPR.

Heat Exhaustion

Signs: heavy sweating; cold, pale, clammy skin; fast, weak pulse; nausea or vomiting; muscle cramps; tiredness or weakness; dizziness; headache; fainting.

  1. Move to a cool place.
  2. Loosen or remove tight and extra clothing.
  3. Put cool, wet cloths on the body or take a cool bath.
  4. Sip water.
  5. Get medical help right away if they vomit, get worse, become confused, or don't improve within 1 hour.

Heat Cramps

Signs: painful muscle cramps or spasms, usually with heavy sweating.

Stop activity, move to a cool place, and drink water or a sports drink. Get medical help if cramps last over 1 hour, or if the person has heart problems or is on a low-sodium diet.

When to Go to a Cooling Center

Leave for an air-conditioned place if:

  • Your home is above 90°F (32°C) indoors
  • Cooling strategies aren't bringing relief
  • Anyone in the home is older, very young, pregnant, or has a chronic illness, and the heat will last
  • Anyone has signs of heat exhaustion (and call 911 for heat stroke)
  • The power is out during a heat wave

Options include public cooling centers, libraries, community centers, malls, and friends or family with air conditioning.

Cooling Supplies Checklist

Essentials

  • Indoor thermometer
  • Battery-powered or rechargeable fan, plus batteries or a power bank
  • Spray bottles
  • Washcloths or bandanas for wet cloths
  • Water containers, and electrolyte drink mix
  • Light-colored, loose clothing and a wide-brimmed hat
  • Sunscreen
  • List of nearby cooling centers and how to get there

Helpful Extras

  • Reflective window film or covers, or outdoor shade cloth
  • Window fan
  • Kiddie pool or large tub (also useful for cooling someone with heat stroke)
  • Cooling towels
