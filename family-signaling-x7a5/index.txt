FAMILY SIGNALING & CHECK-IN METHODS
===================================

Three blasts on a whistle, a sign on the door, a note in an
agreed place. When phones are dead, a few simple signals that
everyone in the family already knows will do more than any
clever code. This guide sets them up.


START HERE
----------

1. GIVE EVERY PERSON A WHISTLE, children included, and teach one
   rule: three blasts means "I need help."
2. MAKE AN OK / HELP SIGN for the front door (a sheet of paper
   with OK on one side and HELP on the other is enough).
3. PICK THE NOTE SPOT: one place at home and one at each meeting
   place where family notes are always left.
4. SET CHECK-IN TIMES for when phones are down, such as "at home
   on the even hours, at the meeting place at noon and 5 pm."
5. TEACH CHILDREN WHAT TO DO IF LOST: stop, stay in one place,
   blow the whistle.

Write all of it on one card (there is a template near the end)
and give a copy to your out-of-area contact.


GET EMERGENCY HELP IF
---------------------

Signals reach family and neighbors. They do not reach an
ambulance. Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if someone
is badly hurt, trapped, missing in dangerous conditions, or in
immediate danger.

- A CHILD IS MISSING: call 911 right away. Do not spend an hour
  searching first.
- CALLS WILL NOT CONNECT: try a text to 911, or send someone to
  the nearest fire station. See the Message Relay When Phones
  Are Down guide.
- YOU HEAR OR SEE A DISTRESS SIGNAL (three of anything, a HELP
  sign, someone calling from a damaged building): answer so they
  know they were heard, then get help. Do not go into a damaged
  building yourself.

Outside the US the number differs; 112 works across the European
Union.


WHY SIMPLE BEATS CLEVER
-----------------------

Under stress people forget. A family code with eight whistle
patterns, colored cloths and a chalk mark that changes monthly
will not be remembered by a frightened ten-year-old, or by you.

Good family signals are:

- FEW. Three or four in total.
- OBVIOUS. A stranger or a rescuer should understand the
  important ones too. "HELP" on a door needs no decoding.
- PRACTICED. Tried out at least twice a year.

Use the widely taught signals for emergencies, and keep private
family signals for small things like "I was here."


THE RULE OF THREE
-----------------

Three of anything, repeated, is the widely taught distress
signal. The US National Park Service puts it this way: any
signal done three times in a series is a universal distress
call. The US Forest Service calls three whistle blasts a
well-known emergency signal.

- WHISTLE: three blasts, pause, three blasts again.
- LIGHT: three flashes, pause, repeat.
- CAR HORN: three honks, pause, repeat.
- KNOCKING: three knocks on a wall or pipe, pause, repeat.

Why three? Nature and accident rarely make a neat group of
three over and over. The repeat is what tells a listener it is
a person, on purpose.

Keep going at intervals until someone reaches you. Searchers
use the sound to home in on you.

OUTSIDE NORTH AMERICA: in the UK, mountain rescue teams teach
six blasts or flashes in a minute, a minute's pause, then
repeat. Any steady, repeated pattern will be taken as a call
for help.

ANSWERING: there is no universal "I heard you" whistle code.
Agree on your own. A simple choice is ONE LONG BLAST for "I hear
you, stay where you are." Then go toward the sound, or get help.


WHISTLES
--------

A whistle carries much farther than a voice and takes far less
effort than shouting, which matters when you are hurt, cold or
have been calling for an hour.

- One per person. Put it on a key ring, a jacket zipper or a
  backpack strap, not in a drawer.
- Keep one by each bed, and one in each car.
- A cord around a small child's neck can catch and choke. Clip
  the whistle to clothing or a pack instead.
- Have children try it. A small child may need practice to blow
  hard enough.
- Teach that it is never a toy outdoors. Three blasts will bring
  strangers running.


DOOR AND WINDOW SIGNS
---------------------

The OK / HELP sign is used in neighborhood programs such as
Washington State's. After a disaster each household hangs one
on the front door or a street-facing window:

  OK   = everyone here is all right, check others first
  HELP = we need help

No printed card? Write the word on paper with a marker.

How families use it:

- Someone coming home sees at a glance whether to hurry.
- Neighbors walking the street go to HELP doors first, then to
  doors with no sign.
- NO SIGN does not mean "fine." It may mean nobody is home, or
  nobody is able to put one up.

Limits to remember:

- A HELP sign is not a 911 call. Keep trying to reach emergency
  services.
- Do not post details on the outside of the house. "Gone to
  Grandma's until Friday" tells everyone the house is empty. Put
  that in the note inside.
- If your community issues its own door tags for evacuation or
  search teams, use those as directed.
- Take signs down when the emergency is over.


FAMILY NOTES
------------

A note is the one message that waits. Agree on exactly where
notes go, so nobody has to search:

- AT HOME: inside the front door, or on the refrigerator.
- AT EACH MEETING PLACE: one agreed spot, such as taped inside
  the lid of a certain mailbox, or under a marked stone by the
  gate.
- BACKUP: a second spot at home in case the first cannot be
  reached.

Every note says:

  Date and time:
  Who wrote it:
  Who is with me:
  How we are:
  Where we are going, and by what route:
  When I will check back here:

Write in pencil or permanent marker, which survive getting wet,
and put the note in a plastic bag. Keep paper, a pencil and
bags at the note spot now.

Leave the old notes in place and add new ones. The trail of
times tells whoever comes next what happened.


CHECK-IN TIMES AND MEETING PLACES
---------------------------------

Fixed times need no technology. If the family is scattered and
nothing works:

- Everyone tries the out-of-area contact from any working
  phone. This is still the best tool you have. See the Emergency
  Communications Plan guide.
- Go to the nearest agreed meeting place at the agreed times.
- Wait the agreed time (for example, 30 minutes), leave a note,
  and move to the next place only if that was the plan.
- Do not roam looking for each other. Two people searching for
  each other can miss for hours. One person waiting at a known
  place gets found.

The Evacuation Routes & Rally Points guide covers choosing the
places.


LIGHT SIGNALS AT NIGHT
----------------------

- THREE FLASHES, pause, repeat = "I need help."
- SOS = three quick flashes, three slow, three quick. Use it if
  you know it; three flashes is enough if you do not.
- A STEADY LIGHT in an agreed window can be your family's "we
  are home and fine." Decide which window.
- A phone flashlight works but drains the battery you may need
  for a call. Use a real flashlight if you have one.
- Never point a laser at an aircraft. It can blind the pilot and
  is a federal crime in the US.


IF A CHILD IS LOST
------------------

Teach this before any outing, the way the National Park Service
teaches it:

1. STOP as soon as you think you are lost.
2. HUG A TREE. Pick a tree and stay with it. No tree? A bench or
   a big rock is your "tree." The point is to stay in one place.
3. BLOW YOUR WHISTLE three times. Wait. Do it again.
4. ANSWER when you hear people calling your name. Nobody is
   angry with you. They are worried and looking for you.

For adults: go back as a group to where the child was last
seen and wait there. Do not fan out off the trail, or more
people get lost. Call 911 early. A child who stays put is far
easier to find than one who keeps walking.

Give children a simple rule for crowds and stores too: "If we
get separated, stay where you are and I will come back to you."


IF YOU ARE TRAPPED
------------------

Ready.gov's advice for someone trapped after an earthquake or
collapse:

- Send a text if you can.
- Bang on a pipe or wall. Use groups of three.
- Use a whistle instead of shouting.
- Cover your mouth with your shirt to keep dust out.

Shouting tires you quickly and makes you breathe in dust. Save
your voice for when you hear rescuers close by.


IN A VEHICLE
------------

If you are stranded, stay with the vehicle. It is far easier to
spot than a person on foot.

- Three honks, pause, repeat.
- Tie a bright cloth to the antenna or a door handle. Raise the
  hood if it is not snowing or raining hard.
- At night, flash the headlights in threes, or use the dome
  light. Watch the battery.
- If you must leave, put a note on the dashboard: time, which
  way you went, and where you are heading.


PEOPLE WHO NEED EXTRA THOUGHT
-----------------------------

- DEAF OR HARD OF HEARING: sound signals will be missed. Use
  light signals, door signs, written notes and a tap on the
  shoulder. Agree on a light pattern for "come here."
- BLIND OR LOW VISION: use sound signals and a fixed, easy to
  reach note spot. Read notes aloud at check-ins.
- OLDER ADULTS AND PEOPLE WHO MAY FALL: a whistle by the bed, in
  the bathroom and on a walker makes it possible to call for
  help from the floor.
- YOUNG CHILDREN: one rule only. "Stay put and blow three
  times."
- PEOPLE WHO CANNOT BLOW A WHISTLE: a hand bell, a pan and
  spoon, or a battery personal alarm does the same job.


WHAT TO LEAVE OUT
-----------------

- LONG CODES. Nobody remembers them.
- SECRET MARKS THAT KEEP CHANGING. They confuse your own family
  first.
- RADIO "PRIVACY CODES" AS A SECURITY MEASURE. On FRS and GMRS
  walkie-talkies they do not make a conversation private. See
  the Radio Use for Emergency Updates guide.
- FLAG COLOR CODES. No color scheme is universally understood. A
  written word is.


FAMILY SIGNAL CARD
------------------

Copy this, fill it in, and give one to every family member.

  HELP SIGNAL: three of anything (whistle, light, horn, knocks)
  OUR ANSWER: _________________________________________

  DOOR SIGN: OK = all fine    HELP = need help
  Sign is kept: _______________________________________

  NOTE SPOT at home: __________________________________
  Backup: _____________________________________________
  NOTE SPOT at meeting place 1: _______________________
  NOTE SPOT at meeting place 2: _______________________

  CHECK-IN TIMES: home at ________  meeting place at ________
  We wait: ______ minutes

  OUT-OF-AREA CONTACT: ________________________________
  Phone: ______________________________________________

Family checklist:

[ ] Whistle for every person, attached to something they carry
[ ] OK / HELP sign stored by the front door
[ ] Paper, pencil and plastic bags at each note spot
[ ] Flashlight by every bed
[ ] Signal card filled in and handed out
[ ] Children can say what to do if lost
[ ] Signals practiced in the last six months


PRACTICE
--------

10-MINUTE DRILL:
Play whistle hide-and-seek in daylight. Tell the neighbors it is
a drill, because three blasts is a real distress signal. One
person goes somewhere out of sight in the house, yard or a
nearby park, stays put, and blows three blasts every 30
seconds. Everyone else finds them by sound and answers with
your family's reply signal. Finish by walking to the note spot
together, where a practice note is waiting. Have the youngest
child read it out.

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

1. On a family walk in a forest park, your nine-year-old stops
   to look at something and then cannot see the group. The path
   forks. She is fairly sure the parking lot is downhill.

   ANSWER: She should not pick a path. The rule is stop, hug a
   tree, three whistle blasts, wait, repeat. Every step she
   takes on a guess widens the area you have to search. For the
   adults: go back together to where she was last seen, stay
   on the trail, answer her whistle with your reply signal,
   and call 911 early if she is not found within minutes.

2. After an earthquake you walk home. Nobody is there, there is
   no note, and your phone is dead. It is 3:40 pm and the family
   meeting time is 5 pm at the school gate. You want to drive
   around the places your partner might be.

   ANSWER: Do not roam. Put up the right door sign, leave a
   note inside the door with the time and your plan, borrow any
   working phone to text your out-of-area contact, and be at
   the school gate before 5. Driving around puts you on damaged
   roads, makes you the person nobody can find, and breaks the
   one plan you both know.

3. At night after a storm you hear three whistle blasts, a
   pause, then three more, coming from a neighbor's badly
   damaged house.

   ANSWER: Answer first, with a whistle or a shout, so they
   know someone heard and can save their strength. Then get
   help: call or text 911, or send someone to the fire station,
   and tell them the address and that a person is signaling
   from inside. Do not go into the damaged building. Stay where
   they can hear you, and keep talking to them until responders
   arrive.


QUICK REFERENCE
---------------

NEED HELP = THREE OF ANYTHING, REPEATED
Whistle, light, horn, knocks. Pause between groups. Keep going.
(UK: six in a minute, pause a minute, repeat.)

DOOR SIGN
OK = fine, check others first.   HELP = come.
No sign = unknown. Check.

NOTE (at the agreed spot, in a plastic bag)
Date/time - who - who is with me - how we are - where we are
going - when I will check back.

SEPARATED
Out-of-area contact first. Then meeting place at the agreed
time. Wait, leave a note. Do not roam.

LOST CHILD
Stop. Hug a tree. Three blasts. Wait. Repeat.

TRAPPED
Text. Bang on a pipe in threes. Whistle, do not shout. Cover
your mouth.

A signal is not a 911 call. Life in danger: call 911.
