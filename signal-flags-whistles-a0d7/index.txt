SIGNAL FLAGS, WHISTLES & VISUAL SIGNALS
=======================================

Being found is mostly being noticed. Searchers look and listen
for things nature does not make: three sharp sounds in a row, a
flash of light, a straight line, a patch of bright color that
moves. This guide covers the few signals that rescuers actually
recognize, and how to make them big enough to work.


DO THIS NOW
-----------

If you are lost, stranded or hurt and need to be found:

1. STOP AND STAY PUT. Moving makes you harder to find. Move only
   if staying where you are is a danger to life.
2. TRY THE PHONE. Call 911 (or the local emergency number). If
   the call fails, text 911, or use satellite SOS if your phone
   has it. A weak signal can still carry a text.
3. BLOW A WHISTLE: three blasts, pause, three blasts. Keep
   repeating at intervals.
4. GET VISIBLE. Go to the nearest open ground you can reach
   safely. Spread out anything bright.
5. BY DAY, FLASH A MIRROR or anything shiny toward aircraft,
   roads, trails and far hillsides. AT NIGHT, FLASH A LIGHT in
   groups of three.
6. AIRCRAFT OVERHEAD? Stand in the open and hold BOTH arms
   straight up. Lay out a big V (need help) or X (need medical
   help) on the ground.


GET EMERGENCY HELP IF
---------------------

Signals are for when you cannot call. If you have any way to
reach 911 (or your local emergency number), do that first, and
do it early: before dark, before the battery dies, before a
minor injury becomes a serious one.

Tell them where you are, what is wrong, how many people, what
you are wearing and what equipment you have. Then stay where
you said you would be.

Outside the US: 112 works across the European Union and from
mobile phones in many other countries. In the UK, dial 999 or
112 and ask for Police, then Mountain Rescue.


WHAT MAKES A SIGNAL WORK
------------------------

- THREES. Any signal done three times in a series is widely
  taught as a distress call: three whistle blasts, three
  flashes, three fires.
- CONTRAST. Dark on snow, bright on forest floor, shiny against
  dull.
- SIZE. From the air a person is a dot. Make marks far bigger
  than feels necessary.
- STRAIGHT LINES AND SHARP CORNERS. They do not occur in nature
  and catch the eye.
- MOVEMENT. A waved cloth is seen long before a still one.
- REPETITION. One flash is a glint. A pattern is a person.


WHISTLE
-------

A whistle carries much farther than a voice and costs far less
energy than shouting.

- THREE BLASTS, a pause, then three more. Make them long and
  loud.
- Blow at regular intervals, and again whenever you hear
  voices, engines or an answering whistle.
- Do not stop when you hear a reply. Searchers use your whistle
  to steer toward you.
- In the UK, mountain rescue teams teach six blasts in a
  minute, a minute's pause, then repeat. Any steady, repeated
  pattern will be taken as a call for help.

No whistle? Bang two rocks or a pot in threes, or use a car
horn: three honks, pause, repeat.


SIGNAL MIRROR
-------------

On a sunny day a mirror is the longest-reaching signal you can
carry. Reflected sunlight can be seen for miles, and a mirror
never runs out of battery. Any shiny surface will do: a compact
mirror, a phone screen, a food tin lid, foil smoothed over
something flat.

How to aim it (the two-finger method):

1. Hold the mirror beside your eye with one hand, facing
   roughly toward the sun and the target.
2. Stretch out your other arm and make a V with two fingers.
   Put the target (aircraft, vehicle, searchers) in the V.
3. Tilt the mirror until the bright spot of reflected sunlight
   lands on your two fingers.
4. Now the light is passing through the V onto the target. Rock
   the mirror slightly so the spot sweeps across the V and back.
   That makes the flash.
5. Flash in groups of three if you can. Even an untidy flicker
   gets attention.

Never look at the sun to line it up. No aircraft in sight? Flash
toward roads, trails and ridgelines at intervals. Someone may be
looking your way.

IMPORTANT: unless you are in a real emergency, do not flash a
mirror at aircraft or drivers. Practice on a tree or a fence
post.


LIGHT AT NIGHT
--------------

- THREE FLASHES, pause, repeat.
- SOS if you know it: three quick, three slow, three quick.
- Point the beam at what you want to notice you: an aircraft, a
  road, lights across a valley.
- Use it wisely. A flashlight left on all night is dead by
  morning. Signal when there is someone to see it, and at
  intervals.
- A phone torch or screen works, but that battery is also your
  call for help.
- A strobe mode, if your light has one, helps. At sea, a bright
  white light flashing about once a second is itself a listed
  distress signal.
- Never point a laser at an aircraft. It can blind the pilot,
  and in the US it is a federal crime.


SOS
---

SOS is the most widely recognized distress signal there is. In
Morse code it is three short, three long, three short:

  . . .   - - -   . . .

Send it with anything: flashes of light, whistle blasts, taps,
or letters on the ground. US navigation rules list SOS "made by
any method" as a distress signal, and the FAA's advice for
survivors is: if in doubt, use the international symbol SOS.


GROUND-TO-AIR SIGNALS
---------------------

This is the international ground-air code for survivors, as
published by the FAA. There are only five symbols:

  V      Require assistance
  X      Require medical assistance
  N      No
  Y      Yes
  Arrow  Proceeding in this direction (point the arrow the way
         you are going)

If in doubt: SOS.

How to lay them out:

- BIG. At least 10 feet (3 m) tall, larger if you can.
- EXACT. Make the shape clean, with straight strokes.
- CONTRAST. Use strips of fabric, logs, stones, branches, gear,
  anything that differs in color from the ground.
- ON SNOW. Drag, shovel or tramp the shape into the snow. The
  trodden lines look dark from the air.
- IN THE OPEN. A clearing, a beach, a ridge. Not under trees.
- KEEP SIGNALING. A ground symbol works together with mirror,
  light, smoke and radio, not instead of them.

An aircraft that has seen and understood your signal rocks its
wings from side to side.

Older survival books list many more symbols (for food, maps,
fuel and so on). They are not in the current FAA table, and
searchers may not know them. Stick to the five.


BODY SIGNALS TO AIRCRAFT
------------------------

Stand in the open, against a plain background. Make each signal
slowly and keep repeating it until the pilot responds. These
are the FAA's published signals:

- BOTH ARMS STRAIGHT UP, held there: pick us up.
- LYING FLAT ON YOUR BACK, arms stretched out past your head:
  need medical help, urgent. Use it only when a life is at
  stake.
- ONE ARM WAVING OVERHEAD: all OK, do not wait.

Read that last one again. A single-arm wave, the thing most
people do when they see a helicopter, tells the pilot you are
fine. If you need help, use two arms.

What the aircraft answers:

- Rocks its wings: message received and understood.
- Dips its nose several times: yes.
- Swings its tail from side to side (fishtails): no.

On the water, the recognized distress signal is slowly and
repeatedly raising and lowering both arms outstretched to each
side.


BRIGHT CLOTH AND FLAGS
----------------------

You do not need a flag code. Ships have a full signal-flag
alphabet, and boaters who need it learn it. On land, colors
carry no agreed meaning: a red rag is not "danger" and green
is not "safe" to a stranger. What works is simple:

- Tie the brightest cloth you have (jacket, tarp, bandana,
  towel) to a long stick and wave it at aircraft and vehicles.
- Orange and red stand out against most natural backgrounds.
  At sea, orange is the color of the recognized distress smoke
  and the marker panel used for identification from the air.
- Spread bright things flat in the open where they can be seen
  from above, and weigh them down.
- A stranded car: tie bright cloth to the antenna or a door
  handle.

If you need to say more than "here I am," write it. Words on a
big sheet (HELP, OK, INJURED, 2 PEOPLE) need no code. See the
Family Signaling & Check-In Methods guide for door signs.


FIRE AND SMOKE
--------------

Fire is a last-resort signal because it can become the
emergency.

- DO NOT LIGHT A FIRE when fire danger is high or during
  wildfire season. The National Park Service says so plainly:
  it could start a wildfire and put you and others at great
  risk. Use a light, mirror and whistle instead.
- If conditions are safe, build it in the open, on bare ground
  cleared well around, in a clearing or on a ridge, not among
  trees.
- BY DAY, SMOKE SHOWS. Add green vegetation or damp leaves to
  an established fire to make thick pale smoke.
- AT NIGHT, FLAME SHOWS. Three fires in a triangle is the
  traditional distress pattern. One good fire you can keep
  going is worth more than three you cannot.
- Do not burn plastic, rubber or tires close to where you are
  sheltering. The smoke is toxic.
- Never leave it unattended, and put it out completely.


FLARES
------

Flares belong mainly in boat kits. If you carry them:

- A red hand flare or red parachute flare is a recognized
  distress signal. Using one for anything except real distress
  is prohibited under US navigation rules.
- Read the instructions printed on the flare now, not in the
  dark. Check the expiry date.
- They burn extremely hot. Hold as directed, point away from
  people, and keep away from dry vegetation and fuel.
- You have only a few. Fire one when you can see or hear
  someone who could see it.


HELPING SEARCHERS FIND YOU
--------------------------

- Stay where you are once you have signaled.
- Make camp near your signals, not hidden in the trees behind
  them.
- Wear your brightest layer on the outside.
- If a group member is missing, the rest of the group goes to
  the last place the person was seen and waits there. Do not
  scatter to search off the trail.
- Tell someone your route and return time before every trip.
  It is the signal you send before anything goes wrong.


BE-FOUND KIT
------------

Small enough for a pocket, car door or day pack:

[ ] Whistle (one per person, attached to clothing or pack)
[ ] Small mirror
[ ] Flashlight or headlamp with fresh batteries
[ ] Bright bandana or cloth, orange or red
[ ] Charged phone and a small power bank
[ ] Marker and paper
[ ] Lighter or matches in a waterproof container


PEOPLE WHO NEED EXTRA THOUGHT
-----------------------------

- CHILDREN: teach "stay put, hug a tree, blow three times" and
  clip a whistle to their jacket. See the Family Signaling &
  Check-In Methods guide.
- DEAF OR HARD OF HEARING: you may not hear an answering
  whistle or a helicopter. Rely on light and mirror, keep
  watching the sky, and signal on a schedule.
- PEOPLE WHO CANNOT BLOW A WHISTLE OR RAISE BOTH ARMS: a
  battery alarm, a pot and spoon, and a large written HELP or
  ground X do the same job.


PRACTICE
--------

10-MINUTE DRILL:
On a sunny day, pick a tree, fence post or wall about 50 steps
away. Never aim at people, cars, windows or aircraft. Using a
small mirror and the two-finger method, put the bright spot on
the target and make three flashes. Give each family member a
turn. Then try it with a phone screen or a tin lid. Finish with
whistles: three blasts, pause, three blasts, once each (warn
the neighbors first that it is a drill).

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

1. Your hiking partner has a broken ankle. After two hours a
   helicopter crosses the valley. You jump up and wave one arm
   as hard as you can.

   ANSWER: One arm waved overhead is the published signal for
   "all OK, do not wait." Use both arms held straight up for
   "pick us up," and get into the open against a plain
   background. Flash the mirror too. If it circles, a big X on
   the ground says "need medical help." Wings rocking means
   they understood.

2. You are lost at dusk in late summer. The hillside is dry
   grass and pine, and there is a burn ban. You are cold, and a
   big signal fire seems like the obvious way to be seen.

   ANSWER: Do not light it. On a dry slope a signal fire can
   outrun you, and no rescue is quick enough for that. Put on
   every layer, get out of the wind, and signal with what
   cannot spread: whistle in threes, flashlight in threes when
   you hear or see anyone, and something bright laid out for
   morning. Text 911 if there is any signal at all.

3. Stranded by deep snow, you stamp the word HELP in letters
   about two feet high beside the trees where you are camped.

   ANSWER: Too small and in the wrong place. From an aircraft,
   two-foot letters at the tree line vanish. Move to open snow
   and make one symbol at least 10 feet tall. A V or an X is
   two strokes and HELP is more than ten, so you can make it
   much bigger with the same effort. Tramp the lines deep so they
   look dark, and lay branches in them for contrast.


QUICK REFERENCE
---------------

LOST OR STRANDED
Stop. Stay put. Call or text 911. Then signal.

THREE OF ANYTHING = HELP
Whistle: 3 blasts, pause, repeat
Light:   3 flashes, pause, repeat
SOS:     . . .  - - -  . . .  (short, long, short)

MIRROR
Mirror by eye. Other arm out, two-finger V on the target.
Put the bright spot on your fingers. Rock it. Not at aircraft
unless it is a real emergency.

GROUND SYMBOLS (10 ft / 3 m or bigger, open ground, contrast)
V = need help
X = need medical help
Arrow = going this way
Y = yes     N = no
In doubt: SOS

BODY SIGNALS TO AIRCRAFT
Both arms up        = pick us up
Flat on back        = urgent medical help
ONE arm waving      = all OK, do NOT wait
Aircraft rocks wings = understood

FIRE
Not in high fire danger. Open bare ground only.
Day: smoke (green leaves). Night: flame.
