# Finding Safe Water Sources

> **Water is life.** Most people can survive only a few days without water, and less in heat. When the taps stop or the water is unsafe, knowing where to find water, and which sources to refuse, keeps your household going until help or service returns.

## Do This Now

1. **Use your stored and bottled water first.** Drink what you need today. Don't ration drinking water unless officials tell you to; look for more instead.
2. **Protect the water already in your house.** If officials report broken water or sewer lines, close your home's main water valve so contaminated water can't flow in.
3. **Tap the hidden water at home** (details below): the water heater tank, the pipes, ice cubes, the toilet tank (not the bowl) if no cleaner has been added, and the liquid in canned fruit and vegetables.
4. **Never drink floodwater**, water from radiators or heating boilers, waterbed water, or water that smells of fuel or chemicals. Pool and hot-tub water is for washing and cleaning only.
5. **Treat every outdoor source before drinking:** rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 ft), or disinfect it. See the Water Purification guide.
6. **Follow local officials.** They will announce boil-water notices, do-not-drink orders, and water distribution points.

## How Much Water You Need

- A normally active person needs about **3/4 gallon (about 3 liters) of fluid a day**, from water and other drinks.
- **Children, nursing mothers, and people who are sick** may need more. Needs can **double in very hot weather**.
- For planning, count **1 gallon (about 4 liters) per person per day** for drinking and basic sanitation, plus extra for pets.
- Water weighs about 8.3 lb per gallon (1 kg per liter). If someone in your household can't carry water, arrange help now.

## Water Source Priority

Not all sources are equal. Use them in this order.

### Tier 1: Safest

**Stored and commercially bottled water.** Use it first.

**Hidden water in your home.** The water heater tank, pipes, ice cubes made from safe water, the toilet tank (not the bowl) if the water is clear and no cleaner has been added, and liquid from canned fruit and vegetables. This water is safe if it was in your plumbing before any main break or contamination notice. Close the main valve to keep bad water from flowing in.

### Tier 2: Usable After Treatment

**Rainwater.** Rain picks up germs and chemicals from the air, roofs, gutters, and bird droppings. Collect from a clean surface, let the first rain wash the surface off before collecting, and treat it before drinking.

**Springs and wells.** Usually cleaner than surface water, but a well that was flooded can be contaminated and needs testing and disinfecting before use. Treat spring and well water in an emergency.

**Flowing streams and rivers.** Collect upstream of towns, camps, farms, and roads. Moving water is not automatically safe: always treat it.

**Large lakes.** Collect away from the shore and away from outflows or pollution sources. Always treat.

### Tier 3: Last Resort (treat thoroughly)

**Small ponds and slow-moving water.** Higher germ load. Settle, pre-filter, and treat.

**Snow and ice.** Melt it before drinking (eating snow lowers your body temperature), then treat it. Avoid colored snow.

### Never Drink

- **Floodwater.** Assume it carries sewage, fuel, and chemicals.
- **Water from radiators or boilers** (part of your heating system).
- **Waterbed water.** Fungicides or chemicals in the vinyl can make it unsafe.
- **Swimming pool and hot tub water** (for washing and cleaning only).
- **Water with a fuel or chemical smell, oily sheen, or bright green scum** (possible toxic algae; boiling won't help).
- **Salt water**, unless you distill it.
- **Urine, alcohol, or blood.** They don't hydrate you and can make things worse.

## Finding Water in Different Settings

### At home and in town

- **Your own house:** see "Emergency Water Sources at Home" below.
- **Official distribution points:** listen to local radio, emergency alerts, or officials for locations.
- **Rain collection** from clean roofs, tarps, or gutters (treat it).
- **Swimming pools:** for flushing toilets, washing, and cleaning only.
- **Don't open fire hydrants.** Firefighters need the water and the pressure.

**Avoid:** toilet bowls, radiators, water near industrial sites, and standing water in damaged buildings.

### Suburban and rural

- Your well (a hand pump or generator can run it when the power is out; a flooded well needs testing and disinfecting first)
- Cisterns and rain barrels (clean and disinfect a cistern that took in floodwater before using it)
- Streams, ponds, lakes, and springs
- Livestock troughs and stock ponds (treat thoroughly)

### Wilderness

**Signs of water nearby:**
- Green, lush vegetation in otherwise dry country
- Valley bottoms and low ground, where water collects
- The outside bends of dry stream beds (dig there)
- The base of cliffs and rock outcrops, and shaded crevices
- Animal trails that converge, and birds or insects gathering

Animals drinking at a source does not mean it's safe. Wildlife spreads Giardia and Cryptosporidium. Treat everything.

## Collecting Water

### Rainwater

1. Use a clean surface: a tarp, a clean roof, or plastic sheeting sloped toward a container.
2. Let the first few minutes of rain rinse the surface, and divert that water away.
3. Collect into clean, covered containers.
4. Treat before drinking.

### Digging for ground water

1. Dig at the outside bend of a dry stream bed, at the base of a slope, or where vegetation is green.
2. Dig down 1 to 3 feet and wait for water to seep in.
3. Let the sediment settle, then scoop from the top.
4. Treat before drinking.

### Transpiration bag

1. Tie a clear plastic bag over a leafy branch of a plant you know is not poisonous, in full sun.
2. Weight one corner so water collects at a low point.
3. Collect the condensed water after several hours.
4. Yields are small. Treat the water if you can.

### Solar still

1. Dig a hole about 2 to 3 feet wide and 2 feet deep.
2. Put a container in the center and green plants around it.
3. Cover the hole with clear plastic, seal the edges with soil, and put a small rock in the center over the container.
4. Water condenses and drips into the container.

Yields are small and unreliable, and digging costs sweat. Treat a still as a supplement, not a main supply.

### Morning dew

Drag an absorbent cloth through wet grass early in the morning, before the sun dries it, and wring it into a container. Avoid grass that may have been sprayed with chemicals. Treat before drinking.

### Plants

- Bamboo segments and banana or plantain stems can hold drinkable water.
- Birch and maple sap run in late winter and early spring.
- **Many plants are poisonous.** Only use plants you can identify with certainty.

## Judging a Source

You can't see germs. These clues help you avoid the worst water, but **every source still needs treatment**.

### What you see

| Observation | Possible meaning |
|-------------|------------------|
| Clear water | Fewer particles; germs may still be present |
| Cloudy water | Sediment, possible sewage or runoff; settle and filter before treating |
| Oily sheen or film | Fuel or chemicals. Don't drink |
| Persistent foam | Detergent or pollution |
| Green or blue-green scum, "spilled paint" look | Possible toxic algae. Don't drink, and keep pets out |
| Strong unusual color | Possible chemical contamination |

### What's around it

**Warning signs:** dead fish or animals in or near the water, dead vegetation at the edge, nearby factories, mines, farms, or sewage outlets, a road or town upstream, and recent flooding.

### What you smell

Don't drink water that smells of fuel, chemicals, sewage, or rotten eggs. No smell doesn't mean safe: many dangerous contaminants have none.

## Emergency Water Sources at Home

### Water heater tank

A tank typically holds 30 to 80 gallons.

1. **Turn off the electricity or gas** to the heater. If you turn off the gas, you may need a professional to relight it.
2. **Turn off the water intake valve** at the tank.
3. **Turn on a hot-water faucet** somewhere in the house so air can get in.
4. **Open the drain valve** at the bottom of the tank and collect the water in clean containers.
5. The water may be hot and may contain sediment. Let it cool and settle.
6. **Refill the tank before turning the power or gas back on.**

### Pipes

1. Turn on the **highest** faucet in the house to let air in.
2. Collect water from the **lowest** faucet.
3. Expect a few gallons at most.

### Toilet tank (not the bowl)

Usable if the water is clear and has not been treated with tank cleaners or color-changing tablets. Treat it before drinking if you can.

### Ice cubes and canned food

Melted ice made from safe water and the liquid packed in canned fruit and vegetables are safe to drink.

## Special Cases

- **Babies:** use ready-to-feed formula if possible. If you must mix formula, use bottled water or water that was boiled and cooled.
- **Older adults and people who are ill** dehydrate faster and may not feel thirsty. Offer water regularly.
- **People with disabilities or limited mobility:** plan now for who will carry water, and keep a supply where they can reach it.
- **Pets:** count their water in your supply and give them treated or bottled water.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Use stored water first** and don't ration what you need to drink.
2. **Tap your home's hidden water** before going outside.
3. **Never drink floodwater** or water that may contain fuel or chemicals.
4. **Treat every outdoor source**, no matter how clean it looks.
5. **Follow local officials** for boil-water notices and distribution points.
