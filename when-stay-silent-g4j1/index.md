# When to Stay Silent, and When to Speak Up

> **Be private, not secret.** A few quiet habits keep an empty house, your supplies and your family's whereabouts out of the wrong hands. But the people who can help you (911, rescuers, shelter staff, neighbors you trust) need more information from you, not less.

## Start here

1. **Tell people, not the public.** When you evacuate, tell your family, your out-of-town contact and one trusted neighbor directly where you are going. Don't announce it on a public feed.
2. **Post after you are home.** Photos of the packed car, the boarded-up windows and the hotel can wait until you are back.
3. **Check your settings today.** On each social app, look at who can see your posts and whether your location is attached to them.
4. **Slow down for anyone who asks for money or personal details.** Ask for identification and check it yourself. FEMA never charges a fee.
5. **Agree as a household on what you never keep quiet.** The list is in the next section. Make sure children know it too.

## Never stay silent about these

Privacy stops where safety starts. Speak up, loudly and early, about all of these:

- **A life in danger: call 911** (or your local emergency number). Give your location first. If speaking would put you in danger, text 911 where the service exists, and call if you can.
- **People still inside.** Tell firefighters and rescuers at once who is missing, where in the building they are likely to be, and anything that affects reaching them, such as a wheelchair, oxygen equipment or a locked room. If you can't reach someone during a fire, get out and tell the 911 operator where the person is. Tell them about trapped pets too.
- **Where you are, if you are trapped.** Send a text, or bang on a pipe or wall. Use a whistle instead of shouting, and cover your mouth with your shirt against the dust. Three blasts is the distress signal.
- **Where you have gone.** When you evacuate, tell your out-of-town contact and let family and friends know when you leave and when you arrive, so that nobody goes looking for you in a danger area.
- **Medical needs.** Tell responders and shelter staff about medicines, conditions, allergies and equipment that needs power. Nobody can help with a need they don't know about.
- **Hazards and threats.** Report downed power lines, gas smells, suspicious packages and threats of violence to the authorities, even if it feels like someone else's business.
- **How you are coping.** Staying silent about despair helps no one. In the US, call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) at any hour. Other countries have their own crisis lines.

## What to keep quiet, and from whom

"Stay silent" almost never means saying nothing to anyone. It means choosing the audience.

| Information | Keep it away from | Do share it with |
|-------------|-------------------|------------------|
| That your home is empty, and for how long | Public posts, strangers, notes on the outside of the door | Family, your out-of-town contact, one trusted neighbor |
| Your exact location right now | Public posts | Family, 911, rescuers |
| What you have stored: generator, fuel, food, cash, medicines | Public posts, loud talk in supply lines, strangers at the door | Your household; neighbors you have chosen to team up with |
| Bank, card and Social Security numbers, FEMA registration number | Anyone who contacted you first | An agency or company you contacted yourself, on a number you looked up |
| Where your children are and who collects them | Public posts | The school and the people you authorized |
| Your daily routine: when you fetch water, when the house is empty | Public posts, casual questions from strangers | Household |

None of this is special to disasters. It is the same advice police give before a vacation, applied to a week when more homes than usual are empty and more strangers than usual are knocking on doors.

## Social media during an evacuation

The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) gives three rules that fit an evacuation exactly: don't post your schedule or routine, use the privacy settings, and remember that once you post something you can't take it back. Copies survive on other people's devices even after you delete it.

**Before and during:**

- **Use direct messages.** A group text or a private family chat tells the people who need to know. A public post tells everyone.
- **If you post publicly, post only that you are safe.** Leave out the destination, the return date and the photo of the house. Some platforms have a "mark yourself safe" feature for disasters, which does exactly this.
- **Turn off location tags on public posts.** Sharing your live location privately with family is a good idea. Attaching it to public posts is not.
- **Look at the background of your photos.** A house number, a street sign, an open garage or a generator on the porch says more than the caption.
- **Ask the family to follow the same rule.** One relative's public "praying for the Garcias, their whole street has cleared out" undoes everyone's care. Teenagers especially need this said plainly.

**Afterward:** tell the whole story once you are home.

## An empty house that doesn't advertise

If the order is to leave now, lock the doors and windows and go. Never delay an evacuation to make the house look lived in. When you do have time:

- **Close and lock all doors and windows,** including sliding doors and the garage.
- **Leave your note inside.** Ready.gov advises leaving a note saying when you left and where you are going. Put it where family or responders who enter will find it, such as the kitchen table. A note on the outside of the door tells everyone who walks past.
- **Don't hide a spare key** under the mat, a flowerpot or in the mailbox. Give it to someone you trust instead.
- **Ask a trusted neighbor who is staying, or returning first,** to collect mail, packages and flyers from the step.
- **If you have two vehicles, leave one in the driveway.**

When you return, look before you enter. If a door or window has been forced, don't go in. Call the police from somewhere safe.

## Supplies and plans: quiet, not secret

You don't owe anyone a list of what is in your garage. You also survive disasters with your neighbors, not despite them. Both are true.

**Be quiet with strangers and the public:**

- Don't post photos of your stockpile, your generator or your full fuel cans.
- In supply lines and at distribution points, be friendly without giving an inventory or an address.
- Carry small amounts of cash and don't count it in view.

**Be open with a chosen circle:**

- Ready.gov's advice for people with disabilities suits every household: build a support network, tell those people where you keep your emergency supplies, and consider giving one trusted person a key. That is the opposite of secrecy, and it is right.
- Check on neighbors who may need a ride when you evacuate.
- **Share needs and offers, not totals.** "We can charge two phones this afternoon" or "we could use ice" builds trust without a list.

**Speak up early if you depend on power or help.** Ask your power company to put you on its list for priority restoration if you use electric medical equipment. Many local emergency management offices keep a voluntary registry for people with disabilities. Tell neighbors who could help you get out of the building.

See the Community Mutual Aid Basics guide for organizing with neighbors.

## At the door and on the phone

After a disaster, the FTC and FEMA warn, scammers pose as FEMA staff, utility workers and contractors. They are fishing for money and for information.

- **You don't have to answer a stranger's questions** about who lives there, when you will be away, or what you own.
- **Ask for identification, then verify it yourself.** Call the agency or utility on a number from your bill or its official site, not one the visitor gives you.
- **Real FEMA inspectors** wear an official badge and show ID when asked. They never charge, and they don't ask for bank details.
- **Never pay on the spot** by cash, wire transfer, gift card, payment app or cryptocurrency.
- **A caller who says a relative is in trouble and needs money now?** Hang up and call the relative yourself. A family password settles it in seconds. See the Emergency Communications Plan (Offline) guide.

The Verifying Information During Emergencies guide covers disaster scams in more detail.

## Radios are not private

Two-way radios (FRS, GMRS, CB) are open to anyone on the channel, and "privacy codes" do not make a conversation private. On the air:

- Don't read out which houses are empty, what supplies you hold, or where cash is kept.
- **In an emergency, drop all of that.** Use plain language and say exactly where you are and what you need.

See the Radio Use for Emergency Updates guide.

## The one time literal silence protects you

If there is an attacker in a building and you can't get away, hiding quietly is the official guidance. Getting away is the first choice whenever it is possible.

1. **Hide out of the attacker's view.** Lock and block the door, close the blinds and turn off the lights.
2. **Silence your phone,** and make sure it won't buzz. Keep silent.
3. **Call or text 911 if you can.** If talking would give you away, text 911 where the service exists: put the address first, then what is happening.
4. **Stay where you are until law enforcement comes in.** Keep your hands empty and visible, follow instructions, and don't point, scream or grab hold of officers.

This is a short summary. See the Lockdown Procedures guide.

### Texting 911

"Call if you can, text if you can't." Texting 911 is not available everywhere, it is slower than a call, and it may not work while roaming. Where it does work:

- Put "911" in the "To" field, with no one else on the message. Group texts fail.
- First message: the exact location, including the city, and whether you need police, fire or medical help.
- Use plain words. No photos, video, emoji or abbreviations.

Find out now whether your county supports it. Your county or state 911 website will say.

## The quiet mistakes

Saying too much is the smaller risk. These are the common ways people say too little, and they are usually worse:

- Not calling 911 because they "don't want to make a fuss" or draw attention
- Not telling rescuers that a neighbor is still inside, out of respect for the neighbor's privacy
- Hiding a medical condition or a missing medicine from shelter staff
- Leaving without telling anyone, so family and neighbors don't know whether to search
- Staying away from neighbors altogether, and missing both the help and the warnings they would have passed on

## People who need extra thought

- **Children:** teach two lists. Things we don't tell strangers or put online: that we are away, where we are staying, what we have at home. Things we always tell a firefighter, police officer or teacher: our name, our address, who is hurt, and who is still inside.
- **Older adults and people living alone:** agree on a rule that no payment or personal detail goes to a caller or visitor until a family member has been phoned.
- **People with disabilities or medical equipment:** silence costs you most. Register, tell your support network, and wear a medical alert tag.
- **Pets:** if a pet is trapped, tell firefighters. Don't go back in.

## Privacy and speak-up checklist

- [ ] Out-of-town contact chosen, and everyone has the number
- [ ] One or two trusted neighbors have your number, and you have theirs
- [ ] A trusted person holds a spare key
- [ ] Social accounts checked: who can see posts, location tags off for public posts
- [ ] Household rule agreed: post after we are home
- [ ] Family password agreed
- [ ] Everyone knows how to call 911, and whether texting 911 works here
- [ ] Medical details on a card in each wallet or on each phone's emergency screen
- [ ] Power company told about electric medical equipment, if you have any
- [ ] Whistle in every go-bag

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Two halves, five minutes each. First, open the social app you use most and look at your last ten posts as a stranger would. Could they tell where you live, when the house is empty, or what is in it? Fix the audience setting and switch off location tagging for public posts. Second, sit the household down and have each person, children included, say out loud what they would tell a 911 call-taker about a made-up emergency: the address first, then what is happening, then how many people are involved. Don't dial. Saying your own address under pressure is harder than it sounds.

**What would you do?**

1. *A hurricane evacuation has been ordered for tomorrow morning. You have boarded the windows and loaded the car, and you want to post a photo of the house with "Off to my sister's in Atlanta until Sunday, pray for us" so that everyone knows you are OK.*

   **Answer:** Tell people, not the public. Send that message to your family group chat, your out-of-town contact and the neighbor you trust. If you want to post publicly, say only that you are safe and out of the area: no destination, no return date, no picture of the house. Put the full story and the photos up when you are home. And leave on time. No post is worth a later start.

2. *After an earthquake, firefighters are working down your street asking whether everyone is accounted for. Nobody has seen the man next door. He is fiercely private, would hate a fuss, and you happen to know he sleeps in the back room and uses oxygen.*

   **Answer:** Tell them all of it, immediately: that he hasn't been seen, his name, the back room, the oxygen. This is exactly the information rescuers need, and they can't get it from anyone else. His privacy matters on an ordinary day. Today it could cost him his life, and the oxygen is something the crew needs to know about for their own safety as well.

3. *Day three of a power outage. In the line for bottled water, a friendly stranger asks how you are managing. You have a generator, 20 gallons (76 liters) of fuel and a full freezer, and you are rather proud of it. Then he asks which house is yours.*

   **Answer:** Stay friendly and stay vague. "We're getting by, thanks. How about you?" is a complete answer. You don't need to describe your supplies or give your address to someone you met two minutes ago, and you don't need to be rude or suspicious either. If he needs help, point him to the distribution point or offer something specific that you choose, such as charging a phone. Save the full picture for the neighbors you know and have agreed to work with.
