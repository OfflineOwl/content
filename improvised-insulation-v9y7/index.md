# Improvised Insulation Techniques

> **Warm the people, not the house.** When the heat fails you cannot keep a whole home warm with blankets and tape, and you do not need to. Shrink the space you live in, stop the drafts, and wrap the people. Still, dry, trapped air is what keeps you warm, and nearly everything in this guide is a way to trap it.

## Do this now

1. **Check the people first.** Babies and older adults lose heat fastest and may not complain. If anyone is confused, drowsy, slurring words or fumbling, go to "Get emergency help if" below.
2. **Put layers on now, before you feel cold:** loose layers, a hat, and dry socks. It is far easier to stay warm than to get warm again.
3. **Pick one small room and move everyone into it,** pets included. Close every other door in the house.
4. **Shut the cold out:** rolled towels under the doors, curtains closed, and blankets or plastic over the windows.
5. **Get off the floor.** Put rugs, cardboard or cushions under anyone sitting or sleeping.
6. **Never heat with an oven, stove, grill, camp stove or generator indoors.** They make deadly carbon monoxide (CO). If you cannot stay warm safely, leave for a warming center, shelter, or a home with heat.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **Anyone shows signs of hypothermia:** shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech or drowsiness. In a baby: bright red, cold skin and very low energy. A body temperature below 95°F (35°C) is a medical emergency.
- **A cold person stops shivering but is not getting better.** That is a sign of getting worse.
- **A CO alarm sounds, or people in a closed-up room get a headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion.** Get everyone into fresh air first, then call.
- **Anything catches fire** and is not out in seconds. Get out and call from outside.

While you wait for help with hypothermia: move the person to the warmest room, handle them gently, take off wet clothing, and warm the chest, neck, head and groin first with dry blankets or skin-to-skin contact. Give warm, non-alcoholic drinks only if they are fully awake. See the Extreme Cold guide for full first aid.

## How heat escapes

| Heat leaves by | You notice it as | What stops it |
|----------------|------------------|---------------|
| **Moving air** (drafts) | Cold air at door bottoms, window edges, outlets on outside walls | Block the gap: towels, tape, plastic sheeting |
| **Contact** (conduction) | A cold floor, wall or window pulling the warmth out of whatever touches it | Thickness: rugs, cardboard, foam, cushions, blankets |
| **Radiation** | A chill on the side of you that faces bare window glass | Cover the glass; put something between you and it |
| **Damp** | Clammy clothes that never feel warm | Stay dry; change wet layers at once |

Fix drafts first. The US Department of Energy calls air leaks among the greatest sources of energy loss in a home, so start with every gap you can feel with your hand.

**The one rule for materials:** insulation is trapped air. Anything thick, dry and fluffy works. Anything wet or squashed flat does not.

## Set up one warm room

**Choose it:**

- Small, with a door that closes and as few windows as possible.
- A sun-facing room is worth having in daytime, as long as you cover the glass at night.
- If you have a fireplace or wood stove that is safe to use, choose that room. See the Emergency Heating guide.
- Close to a bathroom, so nobody has to cross a freezing house.

**Close it up:**

1. Close off the rooms you are not using. Shut every interior door.
2. Stuff rolled towels or rags along the bottom of each door to the room.
3. Hang a blanket over any doorway that has no door, fixed at the top and overlapping the frame.
4. Cover the windows (next section).
5. Lay rugs, blankets, cardboard or foam mats over bare floor.
6. Pull chairs and beds away from windows and outside walls.
7. Bring everyone in. Each person gives off steady body heat, and in a small closed room it adds up.

Keep a way out. Do not tape or nail shut the door or the window you would use to escape a fire.

## Windows

Glass is the thinnest part of the wall, so it gets the most attention.

- **By day, use the sun.** Open curtains on sun-facing windows and let the light in. Clear plastic over the glass lets you keep the sun and lose the draft.
- **At dusk, close everything:** blinds first, then curtains, then a blanket or quilt over the lot.
- **Fit matters more than thickness.** A blanket that hangs loose lets cold air pour out from behind it. Pin or tape it to the frame or wall on all four sides, or tuck it tight behind the curtain rod and onto the sill.
- **Plastic sheeting:** tape clear plastic (painter's sheeting, a shower curtain liner, opened-out bags) to the inside of the frame, sealed all the way round. The layer of still air between plastic and glass is the insulation. Bubble wrap taped over the pane does the same job and still lets light through.
- **Cardboard** cut to fit the frame works well on windows that get no sun, and for every window at night.
- **Seal the leaks:** press tape, rope caulk or strips of rag into gaps where you feel air moving.

## A room within the room

A tent, or a den made of blankets, is the best trick in this guide. The smaller the space, the more your own body heat warms it. Utah's emergency management agency recommends exactly this: set up a tent in your warm room, or build one from blankets and couch cushions.

- **Pitch a camping tent** on top of rugs or sleeping pads. No stakes needed.
- **No tent?** Drape blankets over a table so they reach the floor, or over chair backs and a sofa. Push mattresses together underneath.
- **Insulate underneath first.** A den on a bare floor is a cold den.
- **Everyone in together,** with sleeping bags and blankets, and the pets if they will stay.

Three rules:

- **No flame and no heater inside it or against it, ever.** No candles, lanterns, or stoves, including ones sold as tent-safe. Use battery lights.
- **Fabric, not plastic.** Blankets and tent fabric let air through. Do not wrap a sleeping space in plastic sheeting.
- **Babies sleep beside it, not in it.** A baby needs their own firm, flat sleep surface with no pillows or loose blankets, in the warm room.

## Insulate the people

### Layers

| Layer | Job | Use |
|-------|-----|-----|
| **Base** | Moves sweat off the skin | Wool, silk, or synthetic. Not cotton, which stays wet and cold |
| **Middle** | Traps air | Fleece, wool, down, a second sweater |
| **Outer** | Stops drafts | A windbreaker or rain shell, even indoors |

- **Loose beats tight.** Tight clothes squeeze out the air and slow the blood to hands and feet.
- **Cover the ends:** hat, scarf, mittens (warmer than gloves), two pairs of socks if your shoes still fit loosely over them.
- **Your head is not special.** It loses heat like any other bare skin; it is just the part people leave uncovered. Cover it.
- **Take a layer off before you sweat,** and put it back when you stop moving. Damp clothes chill you later.
- **Short of clothes?** Crumpled newspaper stuffed between two layers traps air. So does a blanket worn as a shawl under a coat.

### Fuel and movement

- **Eat and drink.** Food is the fuel your body burns to make heat. Warm, sweet drinks and regular meals help.
- **Skip alcohol and go easy on caffeine.** Alcohol makes you feel warm while you lose heat faster.
- **Move a little every hour,** but stop short of sweating.

## Sleeping warm

- **What is under you matters as much as what is over you.** A mattress is good insulation. On the floor, build up pads, cushions, folded blankets or flattened cardboard first.
- **Sleeping bag plus blankets,** with a hat and dry socks. Change out of the clothes you wore all day if they are at all damp.
- **An emergency foil blanket** reflects body heat and blocks drafts but has no thickness of its own. Put it over your other blankets, not next to your skin, and never rely on it alone.
- **A hot water bottle helps** if you can heat water safely. Use hot, not boiling, water, close it tightly, and wrap it in a towel. Not for babies, or for anyone who cannot feel heat or move away from it.
- **Share warmth.** Adults and older children sleeping close together stay warmer than each alone.

**Babies under 1 year should never sleep in a cold room.** Dress them in layers or a wearable blanket, about one layer more than an adult would need, and feel the chest or back of the neck to check they are neither cold nor sweaty. If you cannot keep the room warm, take the baby somewhere that is.

**Older adults** can become hypothermic in a home that is only mildly cool. The US National Institute on Aging puts the danger zone as low as 60 to 65°F (16 to 18°C). Check on them often; they may not feel cold.

## What not to do

- **Do not heat with an oven, stove, grill, camp stove, patio heater or generator.** A room you have just sealed against drafts fills with CO even faster.
- **Do not block the vent, flue or chimney of anything that burns fuel:** furnace, water heater, fireplace, wood stove. Close a fireplace damper only when the fire is completely out and the ashes are cold.
- **Do not seal a room tight around an unvented heater.** If you use a kerosene or propane heater made for indoor use, it needs fresh air: keep a door to the rest of the house open, or a window open about an inch, and never sleep with it running.
- **Do not rely on candles for heat.** A flowerpot over tea lights gives off no more heat than the candles alone, and you have just filled the room with hanging blankets.
- **Do not let coverings near heat.** Keep blankets, plastic and cardboard at least 3 feet (1 m) from any heater or fireplace, and 12 inches (30 cm) from a candle.
- **Do not drink to warm up.**

## When to leave

Go to a warming center, shelter, or a home with heat if:

- People are still shivering with all their layers on, in the room you have set up.
- Anyone shows signs of hypothermia (and call 911).
- There is a baby, an older adult, or someone ill in the house, and the room keeps getting colder.
- The only heat you have left is something on the "what not to do" list.

A room thermometer helps you decide with facts instead of feelings. Before leaving in freezing weather, protect your pipes if you know how. See the Emergency Heating guide.

## Supplies to keep

- [ ] Clear plastic sheeting and a roll of tape for each window in your warm room
- [ ] Extra blankets and a sleeping bag per person
- [ ] Hats, wool socks, and a base layer for everyone
- [ ] Foam pads, rugs, or flattened cardboard boxes for the floor
- [ ] A tent, or clips and cord for hanging blankets
- [ ] Old towels for door gaps
- [ ] Room thermometer and a body thermometer
- [ ] Flashlights and lanterns, not candles
- [ ] Battery or battery-backup CO alarm and smoke alarms

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Go on a draft hunt. On a cold or windy day, walk the room you would use as your warm room and hold the back of a damp hand, or a strip of tissue, near the window edges, the bottom of the door, and the outlets on outside walls. Wherever the tissue moves, cold air is getting in. Then roll a towel to fit the door gap, measure the windows, and write the sizes on a card with your plastic sheeting. With children, finish by building the blanket den and timing it.

**What would you do?**

1. *It is the second night without heat and the room is down to 52°F (11°C). You have just taped plastic over the windows. Your partner suggests running the gas oven with its door open, "only for an hour, to take the edge off."*

   **Answer:** No. A gas oven is not vented to the outside, so it puts carbon monoxide straight into the room, and you have just sealed the room. Add a layer and a hat, get everyone into a tent or blanket den with sleeping bags, eat something, and have a warm drink if you can heat one safely outdoors. If people are still shivering after that, it is time to leave for somewhere with heat.

2. *You check on an older neighbor. She says she is fine, but her house is cold, she is wearing a thin cardigan, she is slow to answer you, and she fumbles her cup.*

   **Answer:** Believe the signs, not the words. Confusion and fumbling hands are hypothermia warnings, and older adults often do not feel cold. Take her temperature if you can: below 95°F (35°C), or signs like these when you cannot measure, means call 911. Meanwhile wrap her in dry blankets, cover her head, and give a warm drink if she is fully alert. Do not leave her alone in the cold house.

3. *The blanket den in the living room is a success, but it is dark inside. Your child asks for the candle lantern, and you remember a small propane heater in the garage that says it is safe for tents.*

   **Answer:** Neither goes in. A den is a small space full of fabric, and flame or a fuel heater inside it means fire and carbon monoxide where people are sleeping. Give the child a flashlight or glow stick, and add warmth with more insulation underneath, another person, or a wrapped hot water bottle. An indoor-rated heater, if you use one at all, stays out in the room with 3 feet of clear space, fresh air, a CO alarm, and goes off before anyone sleeps.

## Quick Reference

```
NO HEAT? IN ORDER:

1. CHECK PEOPLE. Confused, drowsy,
   slurring, fumbling, or a baby
   with red cold skin = call 911.
2. LAYERS ON: loose layers, hat,
   dry socks. No cotton on skin.
3. ONE SMALL ROOM, everyone in.
4. DOORS: shut, towels underneath.
5. WINDOWS: sun in by day; blinds,
   curtains + blanket at night,
   edges sealed.
6. FLOOR: rugs, cardboard, pads.
7. DEN: tent or blankets over a
   table. No flame inside.
8. EAT, DRINK, MOVE. No alcohol.

NEVER FOR HEAT: oven, stove, grill,
camp stove, generator. Don't block
flues or appliance vents.

COVERINGS: 3 ft from heaters,
12 in from candles. Keep exits clear.

BABIES: never sleep in a cold room.
OLDER ADULTS: at risk at 60-65 F.

STILL SHIVERING? LEAVE for a
warming center or heated home.
```
