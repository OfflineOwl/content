# Battery Management & Rotation

> **The battery you forgot is the one you'll need.** Light, news, and your phone all run on stored energy. A small stock of the right cells, checked twice a year, covers most outages. One battery type, lithium-ion, can also burn your house down, so learn its warning signs before you learn anything else.

## Start here

1. **List what you'd rely on in an outage** (flashlights, radio, phone, medical devices) and the battery each one takes. Many households can get by on AA, AAA, and USB charging.
2. **Put lithium AA or AAA cells in the flashlight and radio you'd grab in the dark.** They keep for 20 years or more, work in deep cold, and resist leaking. Keep spare alkaline or lithium cells in their packaging beside them.
3. **Charge every power bank to full and write today's date on it.** Recheck every three months, and again whenever a storm is forecast.
4. **Inspect every rechargeable pack you own.** Swollen, cracked, leaking, smelly, or ever under water: it leaves the house today (see Lithium-Ion Fire Safety).
5. **Move coin and button cells, and anything powered by them, out of reach of small children.**

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **A battery or device is smoking, on fire, popping, or hissing.** Get everyone out and call from outside. Do not try to put out a lithium-ion battery fire yourself.
- **Anyone may have swallowed a button or coin battery, or put one in their nose or ear.** Do not wait for symptoms. A battery stuck in the throat can cause serious burns in about 2 hours. In the US, call the National Battery Ingestion Hotline at 800-498-8666 or Poison Control at 800-222-1222 and go to an emergency room now. Do not make the person vomit, and give nothing to eat or drink, with one exception: for a child 12 months or older who swallowed a lithium coin cell within the last 12 hours and can swallow, give 2 teaspoons (10 mL) of honey every 10 minutes, up to 6 doses, on the way. Never delay the trip to find honey.
- **Someone feels dizzy, sick, or gets a headache while a generator or car engine is running** to charge things. Get to fresh air first. See the Generator Safety guide.

Outside the US, use your country's emergency and poison center numbers.

## Four Chemistries, Four Jobs

| Type | Volts per cell | Keeps in storage | Best for | Watch out for |
|------|----------------|------------------|----------|---------------|
| **Alkaline** (single-use) | 1.5 | 5 to 10 years; loses about 3% of capacity a year at 68°F (20°C) | Cheap bulk spares, low-drain devices: radios, clocks, small LED lights | Leaks when old, drained, or mixed. Weak in cold and in high-drain devices |
| **Lithium AA/AAA** (single-use, sold as "lithium" or "ultimate lithium") | 1.5 | 20 years or more, keeping about 95% of capacity | Stored flashlights and radios, cold weather, anything you can't afford to find dead | Costs more. **Not rechargeable** |
| **NiMH** (rechargeable AA/AAA) | 1.2 | Slowly drains itself; top up every few months | Devices you use every week; long outages if you have a way to recharge | Buy the "pre-charged" or "low self-discharge" kind, which holds its charge far longer. Needs a NiMH charger |
| **Lithium-ion** (phones, power banks, power stations, tool packs) | 3.6 to 3.7 | Ages even on the shelf; faster when hot or kept full | The most energy for the weight; anything USB | Fire risk if damaged, wet, overheated, or badly charged |

Numbers are from manufacturer technical handbooks. The date printed on the package is the one that counts for your cells.

**Standardize.** When you buy a flashlight, lantern, or radio, choose one that takes AA or AAA, or charges by USB. One spare pack then fits everything, and you can move cells from a toy to the radio at 2 a.m.

## Store Them So They Work

- **Cool, dry, room temperature.** Heat speeds up self-discharge in every chemistry. A hot attic, car, or garage quietly empties your stock.
- **Keep cells in the original package** or a plastic battery case. Loose batteries in a drawer or pocket can short against coins, keys, or each other and get very hot.
- **Take batteries out of devices you're storing for months.** Leaks ruin the device, not just the cell. The exception is the light you need instantly: load it with lithium cells and check it twice a year.
- **Never mix** old with new, one brand with another, or alkaline with rechargeable in the same device. Mixed sets can leak or rupture. Replace the whole set at once.
- **Never recharge a single-use battery.** It can leak or burst.
- **Cold does not destroy charge; it hides it.** A flashlight left in a freezing car will be dim, then recover fully once the cells warm up. Carry spares in an inside pocket in winter. Alkaline cells are rated to about 0°F (-18°C); lithium AA cells keep working down to -40°F (-40°C).
- **If a cell has leaked,** wear gloves and eye protection and keep the crust off your skin while you clean the compartment.

## Rotate Without Thinking

1. Write the purchase month and year on every package with a marker.
2. New stock goes to the back. Everyday devices (remotes, toys) get fed from the front, so the emergency stock is always the freshest.
3. Twice a year, run the Twice-a-Year Check below. Tie it to a date you already keep, such as the clock change.

How many to keep: enough to reload every emergency device at least twice, then adjust using the runtime math below. A household that standardizes on AA can meet this with one or two bulk packs.

## Estimate Runtime in Watt-Hours

Milliamp-hours (mAh) can't be compared across different voltages. Watt-hours (Wh) can. Convert everything to Wh and the planning becomes simple arithmetic.

```
Wh = volts x amp-hours
     (mAh / 1,000 = amp-hours)

Hours of runtime = Wh / device watts
```

**Worked example: a 20,000 mAh power bank.** The cells inside are 3.7 V, so it holds 3.7 x 20 = 74 Wh. A phone with a 4,500 mAh, 3.85 V battery holds about 17 Wh. On paper that is 74 / 17 = a little over 4 charges. In practice you get fewer, because stepping the voltage up for USB and back down inside the phone turns some of the energy into heat. Count how many charges your own pack really delivers once (see Practice) and write that number on it.

**Worked example: AA cells.** One alkaline AA at a light load gives about 2,500 mAh at an average of roughly 1.2 V, or about 3 Wh. Four of them hold about 12 Wh, enough to run a 1-watt LED lantern for around 12 hours. The same four cells in a 5-watt device will fall well short of the 2.4 hours the arithmetic suggests, because alkaline capacity drops at high drain. That is the case for lithium or NiMH cells in bright lights.

**Plan a day.** Add up the day's needs, then compare with what you have stored:

| Device (example) | Watts | Hours a day | Wh a day |
|------------------|-------|-------------|----------|
| Phone, one full charge | | | 17 |
| LED lantern | 3 | 5 | 15 |
| Radio | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| **Total** | | | **34** |

Your devices will differ: read the watts from the label or manual. At 34 Wh a day, a 74 Wh power bank is about two days, not the week its "20,000" suggests.

## Make a Phone Last

The same charge lasts far longer when you treat the phone as an emergency tool instead of a pastime. In rough order of payoff:

1. **Turn on Low Power Mode (iPhone) or Battery Saver (Android)** as soon as the power fails, not at 20%.
2. **Dim the screen** and shorten the screen timeout.
3. **Use airplane mode whenever you don't need the network,** and switch Bluetooth and location off. Check in at agreed times instead of leaving the phone on. See the Emergency Communications Plan guide.
4. **Text, don't call.** Texts need less network capacity and are more likely to get through when networks are overloaded.
5. **No video, games, hotspot, or continuous navigation.** These are heavy drains.
6. **Keep it out of heat and deep cold.** Phones are built for about 32 to 95°F (0 to 35°C). Heat permanently damages the battery; cold makes it look empty until it warms.
7. **In a household, run one phone at a time** and keep the others switched off as reserves.

## Recharging Without the Grid

Charge everything to full before any forecast storm; a power bank then covers the first days. A car's 12 V USB adapter charges a phone well, but never run the engine in a garage, even with the door open: exhaust contains carbon monoxide. For what solar panels and hand cranks can honestly deliver, see the Solar Power for Emergencies and Hand-Powered & Solar Devices guides. For generators, see the Generator Safety guide: outdoors only, at least 20 feet (6 m) from the house.

## Lithium-Ion Fire Safety

Lithium-ion packs store a lot of energy in a small space. If a cell is damaged, overheated, or overcharged, it can go into thermal runaway: a chain reaction of uncontrolled heating that ends in fire or explosion, and that can restart after it seems to be out. US fire agencies give the same short list of rules.

**Buying**

- Choose devices and chargers listed by a nationally recognized testing laboratory, such as UL.
- Use only the battery and charger designed for the device. A battery that was not made for that use can be dangerous.

**Charging**

- Charge on a hard, flat surface. **Never under a pillow, on a bed, or on a couch.**
- **Unplug when it's full.** Don't leave things charging indefinitely.
- Charge only between 32°F and 105°F (0°C and 40°C). Not in a freezing garage, not in a hot car.
- For e-bike and scooter packs, the Consumer Product Safety Commission says to stay present while they charge and never charge while you sleep. Treat power stations and other big packs the same way, and never charge them where they block your way out.
- Unplug anything that gets noticeably hot while charging.

**Storing**

- Room temperature, out of direct sun, away from anything that can burn. Never in a hot car.
- For a device you won't use for months, store it around half charged and top it up every six months.

**Stop using a battery at once if you notice**

- A smell
- A change in color
- Too much heat
- A change in shape (swelling, bulging, a case that has split or no longer sits flat)
- Leaking
- Odd noises (popping, hissing)

If it is safe, move the device away from anything that can burn, preferably outside. Then call 911. If it is already smoking or burning, do not carry it: get out and call.

**Damaged or swollen packs.** Don't charge them, don't squeeze or puncture them, and don't put them in the trash. Keep them outside in a metal or other non-combustible container, away from the building, until you can take them to a household hazardous waste site.

**Water-damaged packs.** A lithium-ion battery that has been in floodwater can be damaged inside and catch fire after the water is gone, even when it has dried and appears to work. Don't use it or charge it. Unplug it from any charger, get it out of the building, and place it in a metal or non-combustible tub at least 6 feet (2 m) from structures and debris. For flooded e-bikes, golf carts, and vehicles, keep them 50 feet (15 m) from buildings and tell your fire department. Never put these batteries in the trash, recycling, or storm debris piles.

## Disposal

| Battery | What to do (US EPA guidance) |
|---------|------------------------------|
| Alkaline, zinc-carbon | Household trash is allowed in most communities; recycling is better. Check local rules |
| Button and coin cells, single-use lithium | Never in the trash. Tape the terminals or bag each one; take to a battery recycler or household hazardous waste site |
| NiMH, NiCd, lithium-ion | Never in trash or curbside recycling. Tape terminals or bag separately; use retailer take-back or hazardous waste collection |
| Car and other lead-acid | Return to a battery retailer or hazardous waste program |

Rules differ outside the US; ask your local waste authority.

## People Who Need Extra Planning

- **Medical devices.** If anyone depends on a powered device (oxygen concentrator, CPAP, pump, powered wheelchair), ask the supplier or care team now: how long does the internal battery last, can it run on another power source, and is it harmful if it stops for a while? Keep the answers on paper with phone numbers for the supplier, your power company, and the fire department, and ask the power company whether it keeps a list of customers with medical equipment. The FDA publishes a fill-in booklet for this. See the Special Needs Preparedness guide.
- **Hearing aids and glucose meters.** Stock their specific cells and store them high and locked if children visit.
- **Small children.** Coin cells are the dangerous ones. Tape shut any battery door a child can open. Tape or bag used cells and keep them out of reach until you can recycle them.
- **Cold climates.** Use single-use lithium cells outdoors and in the car kit.

## Twice-a-Year Check

- [ ] Switch on every flashlight and radio
- [ ] Open each battery compartment; look for crust, rust, or swelling
- [ ] Check package dates; move the oldest to everyday use
- [ ] Top up power banks and NiMH cells; redate them
- [ ] Inspect every lithium-ion pack for swelling, cracks, heat, or smell
- [ ] Recycle dead and suspect batteries

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Set a timer for 10 minutes. Collect every flashlight, lantern, and radio in the home, switch each one on, then open its battery compartment and look inside. Next, lay every power bank and spare phone on a flat table: a case that bulges, rocks, or has split at the seam is finished. Note what was dead, corroded, or missing, and fix that list this week. Bonus for later: run your phone down, recharge it from a full power bank until the bank is empty, and write the real number of charges on the bank.

**What would you do?**

1. *The power has been out for two days. It comes back at 11 p.m. Both phones, three power banks, and the power station are empty, and you're exhausted. You want to plug everything in and go to bed.*

   **Answer:** Charge the phones first, on a hard surface such as a counter, not on the bed or couch. Leave the power station and other big packs until morning, when you're awake and in the room. Unplug things as they fill. A tired household with every outlet loaded is exactly when battery fires start.

2. *Floodwater reached a low shelf in the garage. Two tool batteries and a power bank were under water for a few hours. A week later they're dry, and the drill runs fine.*

   **Answer:** Stop using them and never charge them again. Water can leave damage inside that starts a fire later, and a pack that "still works" proves nothing. Move them outdoors into a metal or other non-combustible tub at least 6 feet (2 m) from the building, keep them out of the trash and the storm debris pile, and ask your fire department or household hazardous waste program where to take them.

3. *Your two-year-old was playing with a remote. The battery door is open and the coin cell is gone. She seems completely normal.*

   **Answer:** Treat it as swallowed and go now. A coin cell lodged in the throat can burn through tissue in about 2 hours, often with no symptoms at first. Call the Battery Ingestion Hotline (800-498-8666) or Poison Control (800-222-1222) on the way to the emergency room. Because she is over 12 months, give 2 teaspoons of honey every 10 minutes, up to 6 doses, if you have it within reach. Don't make her vomit and don't give food or drink.
