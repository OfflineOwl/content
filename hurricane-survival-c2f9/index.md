# Hurricane Survival

> **Run from water, hide from wind.** Most hurricane deaths are caused by water, not wind: storm surge on the coast and flooding from heavy rain inland. Nearly as many people die after the storm passes, from carbon monoxide, heat, electrocution, crashes, and cleanup injuries.

## Do this now

If a hurricane is approaching:

1. **Know your evacuation zone, and leave immediately if officials tell you to.** If you live in a storm surge zone or a mobile home, plan to leave.
2. **Finish preparing before tropical-storm-force winds arrive.** Once the wind picks up, it's too dangerous to be outside boarding windows or driving.
3. **If you stay, shelter in a small interior room on the lowest floor that won't flood**, away from windows. If water rises, move to a higher floor, but never into a closed attic.
4. **Never walk or drive through floodwater.** Turn Around, Don't Drown.
5. **After the storm, run generators outdoors only**, at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents, and **stay away from downed power lines**.

## Know the Alerts (US National Hurricane Center)

| Alert | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| **Hurricane Watch** | Hurricane conditions are **possible**. Issued about 48 hours before tropical-storm-force winds are expected. |
| **Hurricane Warning** | Hurricane conditions are **expected**. Issued about 36 hours before tropical-storm-force winds are expected. Finish preparations and leave if told to. |
| **Storm Surge Watch** | Life-threatening flooding from rising ocean water is **possible** within 48 hours. |
| **Storm Surge Warning** | Life-threatening flooding from rising ocean water is **expected** within 36 hours. Leave if told to. |
| **Tropical Storm Watch / Warning** | Tropical-storm-force winds (39 to 73 mph) are possible within 48 hours / expected within 36 hours. |
| **Extreme Wind Warning** | Winds of 115 mph or more are expected within the hour. Go to your interior shelter room immediately. |

Watches and warnings are timed to the arrival of tropical-storm-force winds, because outdoor preparation becomes dangerous once they arrive. Outside the US, follow your national weather service or regional cyclone warning center.

### Hurricane Categories (Saffir-Simpson Scale)

| Category | Sustained winds | Expected damage |
|----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 1 | 74 to 95 mph (119 to 153 km/h) | Very dangerous winds will produce some damage |
| 2 | 96 to 110 mph (154 to 177 km/h) | Extremely dangerous winds will cause extensive damage |
| 3 | 111 to 129 mph (178 to 208 km/h) | Devastating damage |
| 4 | 130 to 156 mph (209 to 251 km/h) | Catastrophic damage |
| 5 | 157 mph or higher (252 km/h or higher) | Catastrophic damage |

Categories 3 to 5 are "major hurricanes." **The category measures wind only.** It says nothing about storm surge, rainfall flooding, or tornadoes. A Category 1 hurricane or a tropical storm can still cause deadly flooding.

## Water Is the Biggest Killer

In the US, water causes most hurricane deaths.

**Storm surge** is ocean water pushed ashore by the storm's winds. It can rise many feet quickly, flood whole neighborhoods, and destroy buildings. From 1963 to 2012 it caused about half of all US deaths directly caused by hurricanes, more than any other hazard. Most evacuation orders are given because of water, especially storm surge.

**Inland flooding from heavy rain** has caused more deaths than surge in recent years: about 55% of direct US hurricane deaths from 2013 to 2023, compared with about 11% from storm surge. It can happen hundreds of miles from the coast, days after landfall, and doesn't depend on the storm's category. Slow-moving storms are especially dangerous. Rip currents and high surf also kill people, even when a storm is far offshore.

That's why the rule is **run from water, hide from wind**: if you're in a surge zone or flood-prone area, leave. If you're in a sturdy home outside those zones, you can often shelter in place. Wherever you are, never drive or walk into floodwater.

## Before the Storm

### Know Your Zone

- Find out if you live in a hurricane **evacuation zone** (often lettered or numbered) from your local emergency management office. Evacuation orders are given by zone.
- Know if your home is in a storm surge or flood zone. Evacuation zones are not the same as flood insurance zones.
- Plan where you'll go: friends or family inland, a hotel, or a public shelter. You often only need to go tens of miles, not hundreds.
- Plan more than one route.

### Who Should Evacuate

Leave if:

- Officials order an evacuation for your zone
- You live in a mobile or manufactured home, whatever the storm's strength
- You live in a storm surge zone or an area that floods easily
- You or someone in your household depends on electricity for medical equipment, or needs care you can't get without power and roads
- Your home isn't sturdy enough to withstand the expected winds
- You live high in a high-rise building, where winds are stronger and power loss can strand you without elevators

### Prepare Early (Days Out)

- [ ] Build or refresh your kit: water (at least 1 gallon per person per day for several days), food, medications, first aid, flashlights, batteries, radio
- [ ] Refill prescriptions and get extra medical supplies
- [ ] Fill vehicle gas tanks; get some cash
- [ ] Copy important documents into a waterproof bag
- [ ] Plan for pets (carriers, food, records). Check which shelters accept pets.
- [ ] Charge phones and power banks
- [ ] Bring in or tie down outdoor furniture, grills, and anything the wind can throw

### When a Watch or Warning Is Issued

- Install storm shutters or board windows with exterior-grade plywood. Taping windows doesn't prevent them from breaking.
- Set your refrigerator and freezer to their coldest settings, and freeze containers of water to keep food cold longer.
- Fill clean containers with drinking water. Fill the bathtub with water for flushing and cleaning, not drinking.
- If you're evacuating, leave as early as you can. Roads get crowded, and conditions worsen fast. Don't wait for a storm to strengthen before following an evacuation order.
- Before you leave, unplug appliances and turn off utilities if officials tell you to.

### People Who Need Extra Help

- Register for your area's special-needs or medical shelter program if you need one, well before hurricane season.
- If you rely on powered medical devices, plan for backup power or evacuate somewhere with power.
- Check on older neighbors and people who live alone.

## During the Storm

### If You're Sheltering in Place

- Stay inside, away from windows, skylights, and glass doors.
- Go to a small interior room, closet, or hallway on the lowest floor that won't flood. In a multi-story building away from the water, the first or second floor is usually best.
- **If water is rising inside, move to a higher floor.** Don't go into a closed attic, where you could be trapped. Go onto the roof only as a last resort, and call 911 or signal for help.
- Keep a weather radio or phone on for updates, including tornado warnings. Hurricanes can spawn tornadoes; if a tornado warning is issued, go to your interior room on the lowest floor that won't flood.
- Don't go outside during the storm.

### The Eye Is Not the End

If the eye passes over, the wind and rain may stop suddenly and the sky may clear. **Stay inside.** The storm is only half over: the winds will change direction and quickly return to hurricane force.

### Power Outages

- Use flashlights and battery lanterns, not candles.
- Text instead of calling, so phone lines stay open for emergencies.
- Keep the refrigerator and freezer closed. A closed refrigerator keeps food cold for about 4 hours; a full freezer for about 48 hours (24 if half full).
- **Never use a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors** or in a garage, even with the door open.
- Turn off or unplug electronics to protect them from surges when power returns.

## After the Storm

From 2013 to 2023, nearly as many people in the US died from causes after a hurricane as from the storm itself. Common causes include worsening medical conditions (including from heat without power), carbon monoxide poisoning, injuries during preparation and cleanup, car crashes, falls, and electrocution. Older adults are hit hardest.

### Carbon Monoxide (CO)

- **Run generators outdoors only**, at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents. Never in a home, garage, basement, carport, or shed, even with doors and windows open.
- Never use grills, camp stoves, or charcoal indoors.
- Use battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms.
- **If you feel dizzy, sick, weak, or get a headache while a generator or fuel-burning device is running, get to fresh air immediately and call 911.** People who are asleep or have been drinking can die before they notice symptoms.
- See the Generator Safety guide.

### Electrocution

- **Stay away from downed power lines**, and treat every line as live. Don't walk or drive through water that a line may be touching.
- If a power line falls on your car, stay inside.
- Don't enter flooded rooms if the electricity may be on, and never turn power on or off, or use electrical tools, while standing in water.
- Don't use electrical appliances or outlets that got wet until they've dried out and an electrician checks them.
- **Never plug a generator into a wall outlet** ("backfeeding"). It can electrocute utility workers and neighbors. Use a properly installed transfer switch or plug appliances directly into the generator with outdoor-rated cords.

### Floodwater

- Don't walk, swim, or drive through floodwater. It can be deep, fast, electrified, and full of sewage, chemicals, and debris. If you must be in or near it, wear a life jacket.
- Wash with soap and clean water after any contact. Keep wounds out of floodwater and cover them with waterproof bandages.
- See the Flood Response guide.

### Returning Home and Cleaning Up

- **Wait until officials say it's safe** to return.
- Stay out of damaged buildings until they're inspected. If you smell gas, leave and call the gas company from outside.
- Wear sturdy shoes, gloves, and long pants. Use chainsaws only if you know how, wear protective gear, and never work alone.
- **Heat:** power may be out for days in hot weather. Drink water, rest in the shade, and watch for heat illness, especially in older adults and children. See the Extreme Heat guide.
- Don't drink tap water until officials say it's safe. Follow boil-water notices: a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 ft). Boiling won't remove fuel or chemicals.
- Throw out food that touched floodwater, and perishable food left more than 4 hours without power. When in doubt, throw it out.
- Dry out your home within 24 to 48 hours if you can, to limit mold.
- Photograph damage for insurance. In the US, apply for FEMA assistance if a disaster is declared.
- Check on neighbors, especially older adults.
