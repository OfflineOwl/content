# Radio Use for Emergency Updates

> When the power is out and the internet is down, radio is often the most reliable way to get official emergency information. A battery or hand-crank radio, a NOAA Weather Radio set to your county, and phone alerts turned on give you three independent ways to be warned.

## Do This Now

1. **Turn on a battery or hand-crank radio.** Tune to a local AM/FM news station, or the NOAA Weather Radio (WX) band.
2. **Follow what officials say:** shelter, evacuate, or stay put. Don't leave a safe place just to look for information.
3. **Keep your phone on** (not in airplane mode) so Wireless Emergency Alerts can reach you.
4. **Save batteries:** set a NOAA Weather Radio to alert mode so it wakes up for warnings, and check broadcast radio on a schedule.
5. **Using two-way radios with family?** Agree on a channel, turn **privacy codes off** so you can hear anyone calling for help, and keep messages short.

**Call 911** (or your local emergency number) for emergencies. Radio is for information; it doesn't replace calling for help.

---

## Why Radio Matters

### Advantages

- Works without internet or cell service
- Runs on batteries, hand-crank, or solar
- One broadcast reaches everyone in a wide area
- Carries official warnings and instructions in real time

### When Radio Is Essential

- Power outages
- Hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, wildfires, earthquakes
- Chemical or radiological emergencies (when you're told to stay inside)
- Evacuations
- Any time cell networks are jammed or down

## How Official Alerts Reach You

### Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)

- Short alerts sent to phones in the affected area by authorized federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial officials
- **Free, no sign-up, no app**, and they don't track your location
- A unique tone and vibration, repeated twice
- **Not affected by network congestion**
- Types: National Alerts, Imminent Threat Alerts (extreme weather, disasters, active threats), Public Safety Alerts, AMBER Alerts, and opt-in test messages
- Up to 360 characters on newer phones; Spanish is supported
- **Phones that are off or in airplane mode don't get them.** Some phones need WEA turned on in settings (sometimes called "Government Alerts" or "Emergency Alerts")

### Emergency Alert System (EAS)

- The national public warning system on TV, AM/FM radio, satellite radio, and cable
- Lets the President address the nation within 10 minutes in a national emergency
- State and local officials and the National Weather Service also use it for weather warnings, AMBER Alerts, and local emergencies
- The NWS uses NOAA Weather Radio as its main way to trigger EAS alerts

### NOAA Weather Radio All Hazards (NWR)

- A nationwide network of more than 1,000 transmitters run by the National Weather Service, covering all 50 states, nearby coastal waters, and US territories
- Broadcasts warnings, watches, and forecasts 24/7 from your nearest NWS office
- Also carries **non-weather emergencies**: chemical releases, earthquakes, AMBER Alerts, 911 outages, and more
- Seven frequencies: 162.400, 162.425, 162.450, 162.475, 162.500, 162.525, and 162.550 MHz
- Needs a radio with the weather band; some areas, especially in mountains, have weak or no coverage

### Outdoor Sirens and Local Alerts

- Sirens are meant to warn people outdoors. Their signals vary by community, and you may not hear them inside.
- When you hear a siren, go inside and turn on the radio, TV, or check your phone
- Sign up for your city or county's text or email alerts
- The FEMA app can send National Weather Service alerts for up to five locations

## Types of Emergency Radio

### NOAA Weather Radio Receiver

- Dedicated receiver for NWS broadcasts
- Tone alert wakes the radio for warnings, even overnight
- SAME lets you choose your county (see below)
- Best for: warnings and official emergency information

### AM/FM Radio

- Local news and official instructions from emergency management
- AM signals travel much farther at night
- Ask your local emergency management office which stations carry emergency information in your area, and write them down
- Best for: local news, evacuation routes, shelter locations

### Shortwave Radio

- Long-distance and international broadcasts
- Needs a longer antenna and more practice
- Best for: large-scale events and outside perspectives

### Scanner (Police, Fire, EMS)

- Listen to public safety radio for situational awareness
- Many agencies now use digital or encrypted systems that basic scanners can't hear
- Laws on scanners vary by state, especially for use in vehicles
- Best for: situational awareness, not official instructions

## Choosing an Emergency Radio

### Features to Look For

- [ ] NOAA Weather Radio band
- [ ] AM/FM
- [ ] **Tone alert** (sounds an alarm for warnings even when the audio is off)
- [ ] **SAME** (alerts only for the counties you choose)
- [ ] The **Public Alert** or **NWR All Hazards** logo (meets NWS technical standards)
- [ ] Battery power, plus hand-crank or solar
- [ ] External antenna jack (helps in weak-signal areas)
- [ ] USB port to charge a phone
- [ ] Flashlight
- [ ] For people who are Deaf or hard of hearing: a jack for strobe lights or bed shakers

Keep a home weather radio on AC power with batteries as backup, so it still works when the power goes out.

## Operating Your Radio

### Finding Stations

**NOAA Weather Radio:**
1. Switch to the weather (WX) band
2. Choose the channel your county uses (look it up on the NWS station listing, or try each channel for the clearest local broadcast)
3. Program your county's SAME code
4. Set the radio to alert mode

**AM/FM:**
- Write down your local emergency information stations in advance
- Try AM at night for distant stations

### SAME (Specific Area Message Encoding)

- A **6-digit code** for your county (or part of a county)
  - First digit: 0 means the whole county; other digits mean part of a county
  - Next two digits: your state
  - Last three digits: your county
- Your radio then alerts only for the areas you program
- **Find your code:** the NWS county listing at weather.gov/nwr/counties, or call or text "Go" to **1-888-NWR-SAME (1-888-697-7263)**
- Near a county line? Program the neighboring counties too, if your radio allows
- Program the station that the NWS designates for your county, not just the loudest one

### Battery Conservation

- Leave a weather radio in alert mode instead of playing continuously
- Listen to broadcast radio on a schedule
- Lower the volume
- Use the hand crank or solar panel to stretch batteries
- Keep spare batteries

## Understanding Emergency Alerts

### Alert Terms (National Weather Service)

| Term | Meaning | What to do |
|------|---------|------------|
| **Warning** | Hazardous weather is happening, imminent, or likely, and threatens life or property | Take protective action now |
| **Watch** | Conditions are favorable for hazardous weather; it's possible but not yet certain | Get ready; review your plan |
| **Advisory** | Less serious conditions that could become dangerous if you don't take precautions | Be careful. (The NWS has been replacing many Advisory headlines with plain-language ones, so you may hear either.) |
| **Emergency** (for example, Tornado Emergency) | The NWS's highest alert level: a severe threat to life is happening now | Get to the safest shelter you can reach immediately |

### What Alerts Sound Like

- **NOAA Weather Radio:** a brief burst of digital static (the SAME code), then a **1050 Hz warning alarm tone**, then the voice message, then a short burst at the end
- **EAS on TV and radio:** digital data bursts, then a **two-tone attention signal** (853 and 960 Hz played together), then the message, then end-of-message bursts
- **Phones (WEA):** a loud, distinct tone and vibration, repeated twice

When you hear any of these, stop and listen.

### Who Sends Alerts

- National Weather Service
- State and local emergency management
- FEMA and the President (national alerts)
- Law enforcement (AMBER Alerts)

## Radio Check-In Schedule

During an emergency:
- Keep a weather radio in alert mode at all times
- Check broadcast radio at set times (for example, the top of each hour)
- Listen right away after any alert tone
- Listen before making big decisions, like leaving shelter

## Two-Way Radios (FRS and GMRS)

Two-way radios let family members or neighbors talk over short distances when phones are down.

### Family Radio Service (FRS)

- **No license needed** (US)
- **22 channels** (1-22)
- Power limited to 2 watts on channels 1-7 and 15-22, and 0.5 watt on channels 8-14
- Range is short: often under a mile or two among buildings, trees, or hills; farther across open ground or water

### General Mobile Radio Service (GMRS)

- **FCC license required** (US), but **no exam**
- **$35** application fee; the license lasts **10 years**
- You must be 18 or older; your **immediate family members** can use radios under your license
- Uses the same 22 channels as FRS, plus repeater channels
- Higher power (up to 50 watts on main channels for mobile and base stations) and repeaters can extend range to several miles or more
- Say your FCC call sign at the end of each conversation and at least every 15 minutes during long ones

### Privacy Codes Are Not Private

**FRS and GMRS "privacy codes" (CTCSS and DCS tones) do not make your conversation private.** They only stop your radio from playing other people's transmissions. Anyone on the same channel without a code, or with a scanner, hears everything you say.

**In an emergency, turn privacy codes off** (use "carrier squelch") so you can hear everyone on the channel, including someone calling for help.

### Using Two-Way Radios

- Agree on a primary and a backup channel
- Set check-in times
- Listen before you transmit
- Keep transmissions short, and pause so others can talk
- Use plain language, not codes
- Practice before an emergency
