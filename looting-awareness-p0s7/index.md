# Looting Awareness & Prevention

> **The rumor is bigger than the risk.** After almost every disaster, nearly everyone hears that looting has started. Decades of disaster research find that the real thing is rare after storms, floods and earthquakes. Do not let the story keep you in the path of the hazard. Lock up, document what you own, and go.

## Start here

1. **Decide now that you will leave when told to.** Worry about belongings is one of the reasons people stay behind in dangerous places. Settle the question on a calm day.
2. **Film every room today.** A 15-minute phone video is the single most useful thing you can do for your property. Send a copy somewhere outside the house.
3. **Read your insurance.** Check what is covered for theft, what the deductible is, and whether you need separate flood or earthquake cover.
4. **Write a grab list** of what cannot be replaced: documents, medications, photos, hard drives, small valuables.
5. **Swap phone numbers with two neighbors,** so whoever gets home first can report back.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **Someone is breaking into a home with people inside.** Get out if you can do it safely. If not, lock yourself in a room and call.
- **You return home and find a forced door, a broken window, or signs that someone is inside.** Do not go in. Go to a safe place and call.
- **Someone threatens you** over supplies, fuel or property. Hand it over, get away, then call.

Do not confront or follow anyone. Describe what you saw and let the police handle it.

## What the research says

Social scientists have studied how people behave in disasters since the end of World War II. A summary of that work from the University of Delaware's Disaster Research Center puts the findings this way:

- **Looting stories spread everywhere.** Surveys typically find that 70 to 90 percent of people in a disaster area have heard them.
- **Actual looting during the emergency is rare.** In a typical community disaster in a developed country it is not a significant problem, and often not a single case occurs. What does happen usually involves items of little value.
- **Ordinary crime tends to fall,** not rise, during the emergency period.
- **The dominant behavior is helping.** Neighbors dig each other out, share food and give rides. In the words of that summary, if disaster unleashes anything, it is less criminality than altruism.

| What people expect | What researchers usually find |
|--------------------|-------------------------------|
| Looting starts as soon as the power goes out | Stories far outnumber cases, and some of what is reported is people taking water, food or medicine to survive |
| Empty homes are stripped during evacuations | Cases are few. After Hurricane Katrina, a police official told researchers it was mainly businesses, not homes |
| Crime surges in the chaos | Reported crime and arrests usually drop below normal during the emergency |
| The danger is strangers at the door | The crimes that do grow after disasters are fraud and contractor scams during recovery |

### The honest exceptions

Rare does not mean never.

- **A few disasters have seen real looting.** The research names St. Croix after Hurricane Hugo in 1989 as a case where unusual local conditions produced widespread looting. After Hurricane Katrina in 2005, a research team in Louisiana heard consistent accounts of real theft in New Orleans, mainly from businesses, mixed in with many cases of people taking supplies to stay alive. Looting arrests there still appeared lower than the city's normal daily rate of theft and burglary, though record keeping was disrupted. One police official said incidents spiked when the city reopened to residents.
- **Civil unrest is different.** In riots, looting can be widespread and public. That is a different situation with different advice: see the Home Security During Civil Unrest guide.
- **Places with high everyday theft** will still have theft after a disaster.

### Why it matters

Researchers have documented people refusing to evacuate because of looting reports on the news. After Katrina, one man told the research team that police had to force him out of his flooded apartment for that reason. Storm surge, fire and floodwater kill people. A thief takes things that can be replaced.

## Before you evacuate: the ten-minute lockup

If officials tell you to leave, leave. If time allows:

- [ ] Take the grab list: documents, medications, small valuables, laptops and drives
- [ ] Take your house keys, spare keys and garage door remotes
- [ ] Close and lock every door and window, including the door from the garage into the house
- [ ] Move valuables out of sight of the windows
- [ ] Lock the garage, shed and gates, and put ladders and tools away
- [ ] Unplug small appliances. Leave the refrigerator and freezer plugged in unless flooding is likely
- [ ] Shut off water, gas or electricity only if the home is damaged and you are told to
- [ ] If you have no inventory, film each room on the way out
- [ ] Tell your out-of-town contact and a trusted neighbor where you are going

And three things not to do:

- **Do not announce online that the house is empty.** Post about the trip after you are back.
- **Do not leave a key hidden outside.**
- **Do not stay behind to guard the house.** No lock, light or inventory is worth being there when the hazard arrives.

The Home Hardening Basics guide covers the door, lock and lighting upgrades that make a home a poor target all year.

## If you are staying, or first back

- **Keep to normal security.** Lock doors, light the entries if you have power, and keep fuel, tools and equipment locked up and out of sight from the street.
- **Know who is on the street.** Trade numbers with the neighbors who stayed. Check on older people and anyone living alone.
- **Watch and report. Do not patrol.** City guidance for block groups is clear: share information and report suspicious activity, but do not display or use weapons, do not take the law into your own hands, and do not spread rumors.
- **Expect helpers.** Most strangers you see after a disaster are neighbors, utility crews, volunteers and people looking for relatives. Be courteous. Ask for identification before you let anyone inside.
- **Check a rumor before you pass it on.** Looting reports are among the most repeated and least confirmed stories in any disaster.

### If you see someone taking things

- From your yard, car or a neighbor's house: stay inside or stay back, call the police, and note descriptions, vehicles and direction of travel.
- Do not go out to stop them, block their car, or follow them.
- If someone tries to enter a home you are in: leave by another exit if it is safe. If it is not, lock yourself in a room, call 911 and stay on the line. See the Lockdown Procedures guide.
- If you are confronted: give up the property. See the Personal Safety and De-Escalation guide.

Laws about protecting property and using force differ from state to state and country to country, and emergency declarations can add curfews and other rules. Find out what applies where you live. This guide does not give legal advice.

## Coming home

1. **Wait for officials to reopen the area.** Returning before debris and downed lines are cleared is dangerous.
2. **Look before you enter.** If a door is forced, a window is broken from outside, or you hear someone inside, do not go in. Go to a safe place and call the police.
3. **If the home looks secure,** go through the hazard checks first: gas smell, downed lines, structural damage. See the guide for the hazard you evacuated from.
4. **If something was stolen or damaged:** leave things as they are until you have photographed and filmed everything. Report theft to the police, call your insurer promptly, list what is missing with serial numbers, and keep receipts for repairs and replacements.

## The crime that really does follow disasters

The same research that debunks the looting panic says fraud during the recovery period is real and can be large: false claims, repair work paid for and never done. FEMA publishes the warning signs.

- **Fake inspectors.** Real FEMA inspectors carry an official government badge and will show identification. A shirt or jacket with a logo proves nothing. They already have your registration number, they never ask for bank details, and FEMA never charges for an inspection.
- **Fake aid.** Federal agencies do not charge for disaster assistance or for help filling in forms. Anyone asking for a fee or a deposit to release a grant is a thief.
- **Contractors.** Use licensed local contractors with references you can check. Get the scope of work and guarantees in writing. Do not pay more than half up front, and do not let anyone rush you.
- **Identity theft.** If an inspector turns up or a FEMA letter arrives and you never applied, say so and call the FEMA Helpline at 800-621-3362.

Outside the US, check anyone who offers aid or repairs with your local authority before you pay or sign.

## Inventory and insurance

- **Film or photograph every room.** Open closets and drawers. Include the garage and shed.
- **Capture serial numbers** on electronics, appliances, tools and bikes.
- **Photograph things that cannot be marked,** such as jewelry and collections, one at a time.
- **Store a copy away from home:** cloud storage, your email, or a relative's house.
- **Update it yearly** and after big purchases.
- **Ask your insurer** what is limited or excluded. Jewelry, firearms, computers and collections often need added coverage. Homeowners policies do not cover flooding.
- **Renters:** the building owner's policy does not cover your belongings. You need your own.

If you have no inventory after a loss, rebuild one from memory, from photos on your phone and your family's, and from online order histories.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Make the video. Start at the front door with your phone and walk through every room, saying aloud what you see: "living room, television, bought last year." Open each closet and drawer for a second. Pause on serial number plates. Finish in the garage. Then email the file to yourself or upload it, so a copy exists outside the house. Set a reminder to redo it in a year.

**What would you do?**

1. *A mandatory evacuation is ordered for your coastal street ahead of a hurricane. Your neighbor says he is staying "to keep the looters out" and that you would be foolish to leave your house empty.*

   **Answer:** Leave. The evacuation order is about water and wind that can kill you. The looting he fears is, according to decades of research, far rarer than the stories about it, and anything taken can be claimed on insurance if you have documented it. Do the ten-minute lockup, film the rooms on the way out, and tell him where you are going in case he changes his mind.

2. *The area reopens three days after the storm. You pull up to your house and the side door is standing open. You think you hear something move inside.*

   **Answer:** Do not go in to find out. It may be a thief, a person sheltering, an animal or the wind, and you cannot tell from the driveway. Go back to the car or to a neighbor's house, call 911, and wait for the police to check the house. Take photographs and count losses once they say it is clear.

3. *A man in a FEMA jacket with a clipboard says he can speed up your claim. He needs a $150 inspection fee and your bank account number for the deposit.*

   **Answer:** It is a scam. FEMA never charges for an inspection, and real inspectors do not ask for bank information. Ask to see his official badge, give him nothing, and close the door. Then call the FEMA Helpline at 800-621-3362 to check the status of your own application and report him.
