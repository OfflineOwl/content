# Family Roles & Responsibilities

> **In the first five minutes of an emergency, the question that wastes the most time is "who is doing what?** " Three people grab the dog and nobody grabs the medicine. Decide the jobs now, give every job a backup, and practice once, and those five minutes go to getting everyone safe.

## Start Here

1. LIST THE PEOPLE AND ANIMALS who cannot get themselves out: babies and young children, anyone who needs help walking, waking or understanding, and pets. Give each one a named helper and a named backup helper.
2. HAND OUT THE SEVEN CORE JOBS below. One primary and one backup for each. In a small household one person holds several jobs; that is fine as long as it is written down.
3. GIVE EVERY CHILD A JOB THAT FITS THEIR AGE. Even a three-year-old has one: "stay with Grandma and hold my flashlight."
4. WRITE IT ON ONE PAGE and put it on the refrigerator. Make a card for each person's wallet or backpack.
5. WALK THROUGH IT ONCE this week (see PRACTICE). Ten minutes of walking finds the gaps that an hour of talking misses.

This guide goes with the Family Emergency Action Plan guide, which covers the rest of the plan: contacts, meeting places, alerts and what to do in each kind of emergency.

---

## Life Safety Comes Before Every Role

- If someone's life is in danger, call 911 (or your local emergency number) first. Anyone can make that call, whatever their role, including children who have been taught how.
- In a fire, every person has the same job: get out, stay out, and go to the outside meeting place. Nobody goes back or slows down for supplies, documents, pets or anything else.
- A role is a default, not an order. If your job would put you in danger, drop it and get to safety.

---

## Why Roles Work

Stress narrows attention. People under pressure repeat what they have practiced and forget what they only talked about. A role turns a vague duty ("someone should get the documents") into a practiced habit that belongs to one person.

Roles also protect the people most at risk. The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) warns that children, older adults and people with disabilities may need help to wake up and get out, and says to make sure someone will help them. "Someone" has to be a name.

Children do better with a job as well. A small, real task gives a frightened child something to do with their hands and a reason to feel useful, during the emergency and in the days after it.

## The Seven Core Jobs

These build on the role table in the Family Emergency Action Plan guide. Its "Children" and "Helping others" roles are combined here as People, and Health is added. Fill in a primary and a backup for each.

```
1. PEOPLE (children and anyone who needs help)
   Counts heads. Stays with the children. Helps the person who
   needs a hand to move, wake up or understand what is happening.
   This is the first job to assign and the last to give up.
   Primary: ______________   Backup: ______________
```

```
2. COMMUNICATIONS
   Texts the out-of-town contact and sends the family group text
   saying who is safe and where to meet. Listens to the radio and
   alerts and passes on what officials say. Text first; a text
   often gets through when calls are jammed.
   Primary: ______________   Backup: ______________
```

```
3. SUPPLIES
   Knows where the go-bags and the home kit are. Grabs them when
   you leave, if there is time. Keeps them stocked the rest of
   the year.
   Primary: ______________   Backup: ______________
```

```
4. HEALTH
   Brings the medicines, glasses, hearing aids and medical
   devices, with chargers and batteries. Knows each person's
   conditions and allergies. Has taken a first aid and CPR class,
   or is the one who signs up for it.
   Primary: ______________   Backup: ______________
```

```
5. PETS
   Leashes or crates the animals and brings food, medicine and
   records. If you are told to evacuate, pets go too.
   Primary: ______________   Backup: ______________
```

```
6. UTILITIES AND HOUSE
   Knows where the water, electricity and gas shutoffs are and
   when to use them. Locks up on the way out.
   Primary: ______________   Backup: ______________
```

```
7. DOCUMENTS
   Grabs the documents kit (IDs, insurance, medical lists, cash).
   Afterward, photographs damage and keeps receipts.
   Primary: ______________   Backup: ______________
```

UTILITY RULES FOR WHOEVER HOLDS JOB 6:

- Water: shut it at the house valve for any leak or burst, or if street pipes may be broken. See the Household Water Shutoff & Safety guide.
- Electricity: at the breaker panel, switch off the individual breakers first, then the main. Never touch the panel while standing in water.
- Gas: shut it off ONLY if you smell gas, hear hissing or blowing, or officials tell you to. Get everyone out first. Once it is off, only the gas company or a qualified professional turns it back on. Practice finding the valve, but do not turn it during practice.
- Fire extinguisher: for adults who know how to use one, on a small fire, with a clear way out behind them, while everyone else leaves. NFPA does not recommend training children to use extinguishers.

## Match the Job to the Person

Assign by ability and by where people actually are, not by who is "in charge" of the family.

ASK FOR EACH JOB:

- Who is usually home at 2 a.m.? At 2 p.m. on a weekday? The primary should be the person most likely to be there.
- Who can physically do it? Lifting a toddler, carrying a 30-pound (14 kg) bag, kneeling at a valve and reading small labels are different abilities.
- Who already knows how? The person who fills the pill organizer each week is the natural Health lead.
- Who stays steady? Communications suits a calm person more than a strong one.

A grandparent who cannot carry a go-bag can run Communications from a chair. A teenager who is home alone after school most days may be the best primary for Pets. A person who uses a wheelchair may be the household's best planner and record keeper.

KEEP THE NUMBER OF JOBS PER PERSON REALISTIC. Plan as if each adult can do one thing well in the first two minutes. Rank the jobs: people first, then health items, then everything else.

## Jobs for Children by Age

Ages are a guide. Go by the child in front of you.

BABIES AND TODDLERS (under 3):

- No job. Each one is assigned to a specific adult, with a backup, and stays within arm's reach.
- The adult brings the diaper bag and a carrier or sling so their hands stay free.

AGES 3 TO 5:

- Stay with my assigned grown-up and hold their hand.
- Know my full name, a parent's full name, and my address.
- Carry my comfort item. Hold a flashlight.
- In a fire drill: go to the meeting place with my grown-up. Children under about six usually cannot get out on their own, so the fire plan must name who helps each one.

AGES 6 TO 10:

- Know how and when to call 911 and say my name and address.
- Know the out-of-town contact, a parent's phone number by heart, the meeting places and the family password.
- When the smoke alarm sounds, get out and go to the outside meeting place, even if no adult is nearby. Never go back in.
- Carry my own small go-bag.
- Helper jobs when things are calm: count heads at the meeting place, fill the pet's water bowl, hand out flashlights.

AGES 11 TO 13:

- Everything above, plus a real backup job: Pets, Supplies or Communications (sending the "we are OK" text).
- Help a younger brother or sister get shoes and a coat on and walk with them to the meeting place.
- Know where the water shutoff and breaker panel are, and watch an adult use them.

TEENS (14 TO 17):

- Can hold a primary job and act as backup for an adult's job.
- Can look after younger children for a short time and run the plan if no adult is home. Make sure they have the contacts, the password and permission to act.
- Can learn the water shutoff and the breaker panel hands-on, and where the gas valve is and the rule for using it.
- Can take a first aid and CPR class. Many communities also run Teen CERT (Community Emergency Response Team) training.

NOT CHILDREN'S JOBS, AT ANY AGE:

- Fighting a fire or using an extinguisher
- Going back for anything or anyone
- Deciding about gas
- Being responsible for getting a baby or an adult out alone

Make practice a game for young children. The American Academy of Pediatrics notes they will be less scared and more willing to practice.

## Backups: The Two-Deep Rule

Every job needs two names, because the primary may be at work, asleep, hurt or out of town.

- THE BACKUP MUST BE ABLE TO DO THE JOB ALONE. That means they have done it once: found the valve, opened the documents box, lifted the pet carrier.
- DO NOT MAKE THE SAME TWO PEOPLE primary and backup for everything. If both commute to the same office, a weekday emergency leaves every job empty.
- NAME A BACKUP OUTSIDE THE HOUSE for the jobs that need one: a neighbor with a key who can get your pets or check on a relative, and who knows where the supplies are. Ready.gov calls this a buddy system for pets and a support network for people. Offer to be theirs.
- NO BACKUP PRESENT? The job falls to whoever is there, in this order: people, health items, then the rest.

## People Who Need a Helper

OLDER ADULTS AND PEOPLE WITH DISABILITIES:

- Let the person choose their own helpers and say what help they want. They are a member of the team with a role of their own, not a task.
- At least one helper should have a spare key, know where the supplies are, and know how to use any lifesaving equipment or give medicine.
- Show helpers how to move, fold and operate a wheelchair or other equipment before it is needed.
- Deaf or hard of hearing: someone is assigned to wake and alert them. Blind or low vision: someone guides them on the practiced route.
- See the Special Needs Preparedness guide.

BABIES AND YOUNG CHILDREN:

- One adult per baby or toddler if you can. If adults are outnumbered, the plan says which child each adult takes, and older children walk holding on to that adult.

PETS AND SERVICE ANIMALS:

- A service animal stays with its handler. Pets get one named person. See the Pet Preparedness & Care guide.

## Different Households

ONE ADULT, OR ONE ADULT WITH CHILDREN: You hold every adult job, so cut the list to what one person can carry: people, then one grab-bag that already holds the health items and document copies. Recruit a neighbor or nearby relative as your outside backup, and give older children real jobs.

SEVERAL GENERATIONS UNDER ONE ROOF: Assign by ability, not age. Decide who helps whom on the stairs. An elder who knows everyone's numbers may be the right Communications lead.

CHILDREN WHO LIVE IN TWO HOMES: Each home needs its own role sheet, and the children should know both. Both homes use the same out-of-town contact and the same family password. Agree in advance who collects the children from school in an emergency, and make sure the school has that list.

ROOMMATES AND SHARED HOUSES: Same jobs. Agree who checks on whom, swap emergency contact numbers, and make sure everyone knows where the shutoffs are.

SOMEONE WORKS NIGHTS OR TRAVELS OFTEN: Treat that person as a backup, not a primary, for everything.

## Role Card

Copy one for each person. Keep it in a wallet, backpack or phone case.

```
  NAME: ________________________________
  MY JOB: ______________________________
  1. ___________________________________
  2. ___________________________________
  3. ___________________________________
  I AM BACKUP FOR: _____________________
  MY HELPER / WHO I HELP: ______________
  MEET OUTSIDE AT: _____________________
  MEET AWAY FROM HOME AT: ______________
  OUT-OF-TOWN CONTACT: _________________
  PHONE: _______________________________
  FAMILY PASSWORD: (memorize, don't write)
```

Keep each job to three short lines. If it does not fit on the card, it is too complicated to remember under stress.

---

## Keep It Current

Review the roles at least once a year, and whenever something changes:

- A new baby, a new pet, or someone moving in or out
- A child old enough for the next step up
- A new job, shift or school
- An illness, injury or new medical equipment
- After any drill or real event: what was slow, and who was overloaded?

## Roles Checklist

- [ ] Every person who needs help has a named helper and a backup
- [ ] All seven jobs have a primary and a backup
- [ ] Each child has a job that fits their age
- [ ] Every backup has done the job once
- [ ] An outside backup (neighbor or relative) has a key and knows the plan
- [ ] One-page role sheet posted at home
- [ ] Role card made for every person
- [ ] School and childcare have the current pick-up list
- [ ] Walk-through done, and a date set for the next one
- [ ] Home fire drill twice a year, once at night

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a role walk-through. Gather everyone, say "this is a drill, we have to leave in five minutes," and start a timer. Each person walks to the thing their job needs and touches it: the go-bags, the medicine, the leash and carrier, the documents kit, the water valve, the phone (send the out-of-town contact a real text that starts with "DRILL"). Meet at the outside meeting place and count heads. Then run it again with one adult sitting out as "not home," so the backups do the jobs. Do not turn any utility off, and do not run on stairs. Afterward ask each person, children included, what was hard to find.

### What Would You Do?

1. A strong earthquake has just stopped. Your job is Utilities, and you are already heading for the gas meter with the wrench. Nothing smells of gas. Your partner is shouting that they cannot find your seven-year-old.

   **Answer:** Drop the wrench and find the child. People come before every other job. Gas is shut off only if you smell it, hear it hissing, or are told to, and shutting it off without cause can leave you without heat or hot water for days until a professional restores it. Count heads, check for injuries, then sniff around the meter and appliances.

2. The smoke alarm goes off at 3 a.m. Your nine-year-old's job is Pets, and from the hallway you can hear her calling the cat, who is hiding under her bed.

   **Answer:** Get her out now, without the cat. In a fire every person's only job is to get out and stay out; the role sheet does not apply. Tell the firefighters a pet is inside and where. Afterward, fix the plan: a child's pet job is for evacuations with warning time, never for fires, and children need to hear that said out loud.

3. A chemical spill closes roads near your daughter's school on a weekday. You hold the People job, you are 20 minutes away, and the school has texted that students are sheltering in place. Other parents are driving there.

   **Answer:** Stay where you are and follow the school's instructions. Driving into a shelter-in-place area puts you in the hazard and the school cannot release children until it is lifted. Your job right now is Communications instead: text the out-of-town contact and your backup, watch for the school's reunification message, and make sure the person who goes is on the school's pick-up list and carries ID.
