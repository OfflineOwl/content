# Hygiene With Limited Water

> **Spend your wash water on your hands first.** When water is short, most of the illness you can prevent travels on hands: from the toilet and diapers to food, faces, and wounds. A little soap and water at the right moments protects you more than a full bath.

## Do this now

1. **Set up a handwashing spot** beside the toilet and another where food is prepared: a jug of safe water with a spigot, soap, a towel, and a bucket to catch the runoff.
2. **Wash with soap at the moments that matter:** after the toilet or a diaper, before touching food, before and after tending a wound or a sick person.
3. **Scrub with the water off.** Wet, stop the flow, soap, scrub 20 seconds, then rinse under a thin stream. The scrubbing uses no water at all.
4. **Keep sanitizer for when you truly cannot wash.** It fails on dirty or greasy hands and against several diarrhea germs.
5. **Wipe down four zones daily** with a damp cloth: face, armpits, groin, feet. Dry them well.
6. **Never cut drinking water to wash.** Drink what you need, then wash hands, then everything else.

## Get medical help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) for confusion, trouble breathing, a racing heart with fever and shivering, or anyone who cannot be woken: these can be signs of sepsis or severe dehydration. Get medical care promptly for:

- **A wound that turns red, swollen, or oozing,** has red streaks spreading from it, or comes with fever
- **Diarrhea or vomiting with dehydration:** very little urine, dry mouth, dizziness on standing, a child crying without tears or unusually sleepy
- **Bloody diarrhea,** or a baby or older adult who cannot keep fluids down
- **Feet that are numb, swollen, painful, or blistered** after days of being wet
- **A child who swallowed hand sanitizer:** call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 in the US)

## Hands First

A speck of stool too small to see can carry millions of germs. Hands move it to food, cups, and faces, which is how diarrheal disease runs through a household or shelter.

### When to wash

- After using the toilet, changing a diaper, or cleaning up a child
- Before, during, and after preparing food, and before eating
- Before and after treating a cut or caring for someone who is sick
- After blowing your nose, coughing, or sneezing
- After touching garbage, animals, or animal waste

### How to wash with very little water

1. **Wet** your hands with a small pour of safe water, then stop the flow.
2. **Soap and lather:** backs of hands, between fingers, thumbs, under nails.
3. **Scrub for 20 seconds.** This is the step that removes germs, and it needs no water.
4. **Rinse** under a thin stream: a spigot barely open, or a helper pouring from a cup.
5. **Dry completely** with a clean towel or in the air. Wet hands spread germs more easily.

What does not matter: water temperature (cold removes as many germs as warm), bar or liquid soap, and "antibacterial" on the label. Plain soap is enough.

What does matter: **flowing water, not a shared basin.** Hands dipped into standing water that others have used pick germs back up. Keep nails short and scrub under them.

### Build a handwashing station

- A jug or cooler with an on/off spigot, set at the edge of a table, with a bucket underneath
- Bar soap tied to the handle in a sock or stocking so it cannot walk away
- A clean towel for each person, changed daily, or paper towels
- Wipe the spigot with disinfectant regularly, and turn it off with a paper towel or the back of your wrist

The water caught in the bucket is not clean enough to wash with again. Use it to dilute urine before pouring that out (see the Emergency Sanitation and Waste Disposal guide).

### Hand sanitizer: what it can and cannot do

Use one with **at least 60% alcohol**. Cover every surface of both hands and rub until dry, about 20 seconds. Do not wipe it off.

Sanitizer is a backup, and it has hard limits:

| Sanitizer does not work well | So |
|---|---|
| On visibly dirty or greasy hands | Wash, or wipe the dirt off first |
| Against norovirus, *Cryptosporidium*, and *C. difficile* (all causes of diarrhea) | After the toilet, diapers, vomit, or diarrhea, use soap and water |
| On chemicals such as pesticides and heavy metals like lead | Wash with soap and water |

Other facts worth knowing:

- **Baby wipes are not hand cleaners.** They are not designed to remove germs, and the CDC does not recommend them for hands. Use a wipe to take off visible dirt, then sanitizer, then wash when you can.
- **Water but no soap?** Rub your hands hard under the stream and dry them.
- **Do not make your own sanitizer.** Home mixes can be too weak to work, or harmful.
- Supervise young children so they do not swallow it.

## Which Water Is Safe for Washing

Water that is not safe to drink is sometimes fine on skin, and sometimes not.

| Water | Hands | Body | Teeth | Wounds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bottled, boiled, or disinfected | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Tap water under a boil-water notice | Often yes, with soap; follow the notice | Often yes; follow the notice and keep it out of your mouth and eyes | No: use boiled or bottled | No: use boiled and cooled, or bottled |
| Floodwater, or water with sewage, fuel, or chemicals | No | No | No | No |
| Rivers, lakes, and streams after flooding or near sewage or animal waste | No | No | No | No |

To make clear water safe from germs, bring it to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 ft / 1,500 m) and let it cool. Boiling does not remove fuel or chemicals. The Water Purification guide covers bleach and filters.

**No water at all:** wipe off visible dirt, use sanitizer, and treat soap and water as the first thing to restore. Keep your hands away from your face and from other people's food until then.

## Washing Your Body With a Basin

Skin problems start where sweat and dirt collect. You do not need a shower. You need a cloth, soap, and about a quart (1 liter) of safe water.

**Work from cleanest to dirtiest:**

1. Face and neck
2. Armpits
3. Hands and arms
4. Chest, back, and skin folds
5. Feet, between the toes
6. Groin and bottom last, with a fresh part of the cloth

Rinse the cloth, wipe off the soap, and dry every fold. For the vulva, use water only and wipe front to back.

- **Each person has their own cloth and towel,** hung to dry fully between uses.
- **Body wipes** are fine for skin when water is very short.
- **Hair can wait.** Brush it and tie it back.
- **Underwear and socks** come first when there is laundry water. Air everything else.

Do not bathe in floodwater or in rivers and lakes that may hold sewage or animal waste, least of all with a cut or rash.

### Feet

Feet that stay wet for days are injured by the wetness itself. Trench foot (immersion foot) can develop at temperatures as high as 60°F (16°C), and wet feet lose heat about 25 times faster than dry ones. They hurt, swell, feel heavy or numb, and can blister.

- Clean and dry your feet every day, and put on clean, dry socks
- Sleep with bare feet raised
- If you suspect trench foot: take off wet boots and socks, dry the feet, stay off them, and get medical attention right away

## Teeth

Brush twice a day for 2 minutes with fluoride toothpaste, using only water you would drink. Then **spit, and do not rinse.** Rinsing washes away the fluoride left on your teeth, so the water-saving method is also the better one. A sip to wet the brush is all you need.

See the Dental Emergencies guide for toothache and injuries.

## Periods, Diapers, and Incontinence

**Periods:**

- Wash your hands before and after changing any product.
- Change pads every few hours. Change tampons every 4 to 8 hours and never wear one longer than 8.
- A menstrual cup must be cleaned after each use and handled with clean hands. If you cannot manage both with safe water, switch to pads. When your period ends, rinse the cup and boil it for 1 to 2 minutes.
- Wrap used products and put them in the trash. Never flush them.

**Diapers:**

- Change them away from where food is prepared.
- Bag the diaper and put it in the garbage, not in an emergency toilet bucket.
- Clean and then disinfect the changing surface (see the bleach table below).
- Wash your hands and the child's hands with soap and water every time.

**Incontinence:** clean and dry the skin at every change, and stock pads, wipes, and gloves ahead of time.

## Wounds and Skin

- Wash cuts with soap and clean water (drinkable, or boiled and cooled), and cover them. Use a waterproof bandage if the wound might get wet.
- Keep open wounds out of floodwater, seawater, and brackish water.
- Look at every wound daily. Redness, swelling, or oozing means get medical care.

The Infection Prevention and Basic First Aid guides cover wound care in detail.

## Bleach for Surfaces: The CDC Dilutions

Bleach is for surfaces, never for hands or skin. Every ratio below assumes **regular, unscented household bleach labeled 5% to 9% sodium hypochlorite**. If the label gives no percentage, or the product is scented or "splashless", do not rely on it to disinfect.

| Job | Bleach | In water | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disinfect hard surfaces: toilet seat, changing table, sick room, door handles | 5 tablespoons (1/3 cup), or 4 teaspoons | 1 gallon, or 1 quart (about 20 mL per liter) | Clean first. Keep wet at least 1 minute |
| After vomit or diarrhea (norovirus) | 5 to 25 tablespoons | 1 gallon | Leave on at least 5 minutes. The CDC states this one for 5% to 8% bleach |
| Sanitize dishes and food surfaces touched by floodwater | 1 tablespoon | 1 gallon (about 4 mL per liter) | Wash, rinse, sanitize, air dry |

- **Clean first.** Disinfectant does not work through dirt.
- **Mix fresh each day** with room-temperature water. Diluted bleach weakens within 24 hours.
- **Never mix bleach with ammonia or any other cleaner.** The fumes can be dangerous.
- Open windows, wear gloves, and protect your eyes.

## How Far a Gallon Goes

Ready.gov's planning figure is at least 1 gallon (3.8 liters) per person per day, for drinking and sanitation together. Drinking comes first and should not be rationed. What is left covers handwashing, teeth, and a cloth wash, not showers or laundry. Store more if you can; see the Water Storage & Rotation and Water Rationing guides.

- [ ] Basin, washcloths, and a towel for each person
- [ ] Body wipes
- [ ] Toothbrushes, fluoride toothpaste, floss
- [ ] Pads or tampons; diapers and wipes if needed
- [ ] Nail clippers and waterproof bandages
- [ ] Spare socks and underwear
- [ ] Regular unscented bleach (5% to 9%) with measuring spoons
- [ ] Disposable gloves and trash bags

## Common Mistakes

- **Sanitizer as the default.** It is the fallback. Soap and water is the method.
- **One basin for everyone.** Rinse under flowing water.
- **Rinsing off in the flooded creek.** Floodwater carries sewage.
- **"Clean" hands from a baby wipe.** Wipes move dirt; they do not remove germs.
- **Skipping drinks to save water for washing.** Dehydration is the bigger danger.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** The one-cup handwash. Measure 1 cup (240 mL) of water into a bottle. Rub a little cooking oil and cinnamon or flour on your hands as stand-in dirt. Have someone pour while you wash properly: wet, stop, soap, scrub for 20 seconds, rinse. See how much water is left and whether any "dirt" remains around your nails and thumbs. Then swap. Now you know how much water a wash really takes in your household, and who rushes the scrub.

**What would you do?**

1. *You have just changed the diaper of a toddler with diarrhea. The water jug is across the camp, dinner needs stirring, and there is sanitizer in your pocket.*

   **Answer:** Walk to the jug and wash with soap. Sanitizer works poorly on soiled hands and against several of the germs that cause diarrhea, including norovirus. If there were truly no water, you would wipe off anything visible, use sanitizer, stay away from the food, and wash at the first chance. Someone else stirs dinner.

2. *Day three without a shower. The creek behind the house is high and brown after the storm, and you have a scraped shin. A quick dip would feel wonderful.*

   **Answer:** Stay out. Floodwater and swollen creeks carry sewage and animal waste, and an open scrape is a way in for infection. Do a basin wash with a quart of safe water instead, clean the scrape with soap and clean water, and cover it with a waterproof bandage.

3. *Someone vomited on the kitchen floor. The only bleach in the house says "splashless, fresh scent" and gives no percentage.*

   **Answer:** Do not count on that bottle to disinfect. The CDC ratios assume regular unscented bleach labeled 5% to 9%. Put on gloves, pick up the mess with paper towels, and wash the area thoroughly with detergent and water, which physically removes much of the contamination. Bag the waste, then wash your hands with soap and water. Do not mix products to make something "stronger". When you can get proper bleach, disinfect with the stronger vomit-and-diarrhea mix.
