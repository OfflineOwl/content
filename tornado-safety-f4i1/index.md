# Tornado Safety

> **Minutes matter.** Tornadoes can form quickly and are often hidden by rain or darkness. Decide now where you will shelter, and go there the moment a warning is issued for your area.

## Do this now

If a **Tornado Warning** is issued for your area, or you see or hear a tornado coming:

1. **Go to shelter immediately.** Don't wait to see the tornado or to hear a siren.
2. **Get as low and as far inside as you can:** a basement, storm cellar, or safe room is best. Otherwise, a small windowless interior room on the lowest floor (bathroom, closet, inner hallway).
3. **Get down and cover your head and neck** with your arms. Pull a mattress, blankets, sleeping bags, or a coat over your body. Put on a helmet if one is within reach.
4. **In a mobile home, vehicle, or outdoors?** Get to the nearest sturdy building or storm shelter if you can reach it before the tornado does. Never shelter under an overpass.
5. **Stay in shelter until the warning expires** or officials say the danger has passed. Keep monitoring a weather radio, phone alerts, or local media.

## Know the Alerts (US National Weather Service)

| Alert | What it means | What to do |
|-------|---------------|------------|
| **Tornado Watch** | Conditions are favorable for tornadoes in and near the area. | Review your plan, charge phones, check your kit, know where you'll shelter. Be ready to move fast. |
| **Tornado Warning** | A tornado has been sighted or indicated by weather radar. | Take shelter **now**. |
| **Tornado Emergency** | The NWS's highest alert. A tornado is causing, or is about to cause, catastrophic damage and a severe threat to life. | You should already be in shelter. Stay there. |

A watch that says **"Particularly Dangerous Situation" (PDS)** means long-lived, strong, and violent tornadoes are possible. Treat it seriously and make sure you can reach your shelter quickly.

**Don't rely on outdoor sirens.** They are meant to warn people who are outdoors, and you may not hear them inside, in a storm, or while asleep. Have at least two other ways to get warnings: a NOAA Weather Radio with battery backup, Wireless Emergency Alerts on your phone, or local TV and radio.

Outside the US, follow your national weather service's warnings. The sheltering advice in this guide applies anywhere.

### Natural Warning Signs

Take shelter right away if you notice:

- A loud, continuous roar or rumble that doesn't fade after a few seconds like thunder
- Strong, persistent rotation in the base of a cloud
- Whirling dust or debris on the ground under a cloud, even if there's no visible funnel
- Hail or heavy rain followed by a dead calm or a fast, intense wind shift
- At night: small, bright, blue-green to white flashes at ground level near a storm (power lines being snapped)

Many tornadoes are wrapped in rain or occur at night and can't be seen, and some strike before a warning is issued. Don't wait for visual confirmation when a warning is issued, and don't wait for a warning if you see these signs. A green sky is not a reliable tornado sign.

## Before Tornado Season

- **Pick your shelter spot** at home, work, and school, and practice getting there. Know where the nearest public storm shelter is.
- **Consider a safe room.** The best protection is a safe room built to FEMA criteria or a storm shelter built to the ICC 500 standard.
- **Set up warnings:** a NOAA Weather Radio with fresh batteries, and emergency alerts turned on on every phone.
- **If you live in a mobile home,** find the nearest sturdy building or community shelter now and time how long it takes to get there. Plan to go there **when tornadoes are forecast, before warnings are issued** and the storms arrive. Staying with friends or family in a sturdy building that day is a good plan.
- **Plan for people who need help:** older adults, young children, people with disabilities or limited mobility, and anyone who uses medical equipment. Decide who will help them and how.
- **Plan for pets:** keep carriers and leashes near your shelter spot.
- **Stock your shelter area** with the kit below.

## Where to Shelter

### House With a Basement

- Go to the basement. Get under a sturdy workbench or heavy table, or cover yourself with a mattress or sleeping bag.
- Know where heavy objects (pianos, refrigerators, waterbeds) sit on the floor above, and don't shelter directly beneath them.
- The interior part of the basement, away from windows, is safest.

### House Without a Basement

- Go to a small interior room on the lowest floor: a bathroom, closet, inner hallway, or under the stairs.
- Put as many walls as possible between you and the outside. Stay away from windows, doors, and outside walls.
- Crouch low, face down, and cover your head with your arms and thick padding.
- A metal bathtub can give partial protection if you cover yourself with padding. Plastic and fiberglass tubs don't.

### Apartment, Condo, or High-Rise

- Go to the lowest floor you can reach safely, to a small windowless room or hallway in the center of the building.
- An interior stairwell can work if it isn't crowded. Stay away from windows and glass.
- Don't use elevators: the power can fail and trap you.

### School, Office, Hospital, Store, or Mall

- Follow the building's tornado plan and go to the designated shelter area.
- Interior hallways, bathrooms, and storage rooms on the lowest floor, away from glass, are safest.
- **Avoid large open rooms** such as gyms, auditoriums, cafeterias, and open store floors. Wide-span roofs can collapse.

### Church, Theater, or Arena

- Go to an interior bathroom, hallway, or storage room.
- If there's no time, get down under the seats or pews and cover your head.

### Mobile or Manufactured Home

- **Get out.** Even a tied-down mobile home is not as safe as a sturdy building or underground shelter, and mobile homes can't withstand the force of tornado winds.
- Go to the nearest sturdy building or storm shelter. Leave early, before warnings are issued and storms arrive.
- If a warning catches you there, go to the nearest sturdy building right away if you can reach it in time. If you can't, follow the advice for being outdoors.

### In a Vehicle

There's no safe option in a vehicle, only less dangerous ones. If a warning is issued while you're driving:

1. **Drive to the closest sturdy shelter if you can safely reach it.** Don't try to outrun a tornado.
2. **If the tornado is visible, far away, and traffic is light,** you may be able to drive out of its path by moving at right angles to its track. Don't try this in heavy traffic or in a city.
3. **If you're caught by flying debris or extreme wind,** pull over and park out of traffic lanes. Then choose:
   - Stay in the car with your seat belt on. Put your head down below the windows and cover it with your arms and a coat, blanket, or cushion.
   - Or, if you can safely get to an area **noticeably lower than the roadway**, such as a deep ditch, get out and lie flat there, covering your head with your arms.
4. **Never shelter under a highway overpass or bridge.** Overpasses offer no extra protection, have proved deadly in tornadoes, and stopped cars create dangerous traffic jams.

### Outdoors With No Shelter

There's no perfect option outdoors. Choose based on what's closest:

- Get into a vehicle, buckle up, and try to drive to the nearest sturdy shelter.
- If that's impossible, lie flat and face down in a low spot (a ditch or ravine) noticeably lower than the ground around you, and cover your head and neck with your arms.
- Get as far as you can from trees and cars, which can be blown onto you. Watch for flowing water in low areas.
- Sheds, storage buildings, and tents are not safe shelters.

## During the Tornado

- **Get low:** kneel or crouch face down on the floor.
- **Cover your head and neck** with your arms to protect against flying and falling debris.
- **Add padding:** a mattress, sleeping bags, thick blankets, or couch cushions over your body, and sturdy furniture around you.
- **Helmets:** a bike, sports, or hard-hat helmet may add protection for your head. There's no research proving they work in tornadoes, so never delay getting to shelter to find one. Keep helmets in your shelter spot so they're ready.
- **Put on sturdy shoes** if they're within reach, so you can walk safely through debris afterward.
- **Keep children with you** under the padding.

## After the Tornado

### Stay Safe Right Away

- **Stay in shelter until the warning has expired.** More storms or tornadoes may follow.
- **Check yourself and others for injuries.** Call 911 (or your local emergency number) for serious injuries. Give first aid if you're trained; stop bleeding with firm, direct pressure. Don't move someone who is seriously hurt unless they're in immediate danger, such as from fire or collapse.
- **Watch where you step.** Wear sturdy shoes or boots, long pants, and work gloves. Nails, broken glass, and sharp metal cause many post-storm injuries.

### Hazards to Watch For

- **Downed power lines:** treat every fallen line as live. Stay far away, keep others away, and report it to the utility or 911. Don't touch anything a line is touching, including cars, fences, and puddles.
- **Gas leaks:** if you smell gas or hear hissing, leave the building right away. Don't use light switches, appliances, matches, lighters, or anything that could spark. Shut off the main gas valve from outside if you know how, and call the gas company or 911 from a safe distance.
- **Damaged buildings:** stay out until officials say they're safe. Walls and roofs can collapse later. If you're inside and hear shifting or unusual noises, get out.
- **Fire:** use flashlights or battery lanterns, never candles, in a damaged building.
- **Carbon monoxide:** if you use a generator, run it outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents. Never use generators, grills, or camp stoves indoors or in a garage. See the Generator Safety guide.
- **Cleanup injuries:** chainsaws, ladders, and heavy debris injure many people after storms. Learn how to use a saw safely before you start, wear protective clothing and eye protection, keep bystanders back, check for nails and power lines before cutting, and watch for bent, trapped branches that can spring back. Work with a partner and take breaks.

### Next Steps

- Let family know you're safe. Text instead of calling so phone lines stay open for emergencies.
- Listen to local officials for information about shelters, water safety, and road closures.
- Photograph damage before cleanup for insurance, and watch out for scammers posing as contractors or insurance agents.
- Check on neighbors, especially older adults and people living alone.
- Children and adults can be frightened for days or weeks afterward. Keep routines where you can, and talk about what happened.

## Tornado Kit

Keep these in or near your shelter spot:

- [ ] Helmets for everyone (bike, sports, or hard hat)
- [ ] Sturdy shoes for everyone
- [ ] Flashlight and spare batteries
- [ ] NOAA Weather Radio (battery or hand-crank)
- [ ] Whistle or air horn, to signal for help if trapped
- [ ] First aid kit and a few days of essential medications
- [ ] Water and snacks
- [ ] Phone power bank
- [ ] Thick blankets, sleeping bags, or a mattress for cover
- [ ] Pet carrier and leash
- [ ] Copies of ID and insurance documents
