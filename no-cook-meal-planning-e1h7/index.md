# No-Cook Meal Planning

> **The stove is optional. Calories, water, and a can opener are not.** Commercially canned food is already cooked and can be eaten straight from the can. A few days of planned no-cook meals means no fuel, no flame indoors, and far less water spent on cooking and washing up.

## Start here

1. **Do the count.** People × days × about 2,000 calories for each adult. That is your target before you buy anything.
2. **Build every meal from three parts:** a protein, a starch, and a fruit or vegetable. The table below gives the building blocks.
3. **Buy single-meal sizes.** With no refrigerator, an opened can of meat, fish, or beans has to be eaten within 2 hours.
4. **Choose low-salt foods and foods packed in liquid.** Salt makes you thirsty, and the juice in a can of peaches counts as fluid.
5. **Box it together** with a manual can opener, spoons, and paper plates, and label the box.
6. **Store the water to go with it:** at least 1 gallon (3.8 liters) per person per day.

## When No-Cook Is the Right Plan

- **The power or gas is out** and you have no safe way to cook. Camp stoves, grills, and generators belong outdoors only (see the Cooking Without Power guide).
- **Water is short.** Rice, pasta, and dried beans take water and fuel. Canned and ready-to-eat foods take neither.
- **It is too hot or too smoky** to add heat or flame, or a fire ban is in force.
- **You are leaving.** A go-bag has no kitchen.
- **The first days of any disaster,** when you have better things to do than cook.

FEMA's advice for an emergency food supply is the same: foods that need no refrigeration, water, special preparation, or cooking are best.

## The Building Blocks

Calories and protein below are from USDA food data for typical products. Brands vary, so check your own labels.

| Food | Portion | Calories | Protein | Notes |
|------|---------|----------|---------|-------|
| Peanut butter | 2 tablespoons (32 g) | 190 | 7 g | About 2,700 calories in a 16 oz jar |
| Mixed nuts | 1 oz (28 g), a small handful | 170 | 5 g | Buy unsalted if water may be short |
| Canned light tuna in water, drained | 3.5 oz (100 g), most of a 5 oz can | 115 | 25 g | Among the most protein per calorie |
| Canned chicken, with broth | 3.5 oz (100 g) | 165 | 22 g | Small cans avoid leftovers |
| Canned kidney beans, drained | 3.5 oz (100 g), about half a cup | 125 | 8 g | Also 5 to 6 g of fiber |
| Rolled oats, dry | 1/2 cup (40 g) | 150 | 5 g | Soften in cold milk or water |
| Saltine crackers | 10 crackers (30 g) | 125 | 3 g | Salty: about 280 mg sodium |
| Raisins | 1.5 oz box (43 g) | 130 | 1 g | Dense, light, keeps well |

**For anything else, do the label math:** calories per serving × servings per container. Write the total on the lid with a marker. Ready-to-eat cereal, granola bars, shelf-stable (UHT) milk, canned fruit, canned vegetables, applesauce, tortillas, and jelly all fit a no-cook plan.

**Protein.** The recommended daily amount is 46 g for adult women and 56 g for adult men (71 g in pregnancy and while breastfeeding). One can of tuna and four tablespoons of peanut butter cover most of that.

## A Worked Example: One Adult, One Day

| Meal | Food | Calories | Protein |
|------|------|----------|---------|
| Breakfast | 1/2 cup oats soaked in water or shelf-stable milk, a box of raisins, 2 tablespoons peanut butter | 470 | 13 g |
| Lunch | Tuna (100 g) on 10 saltines | 240 | 28 g |
| Snack | 2 oz (57 g) mixed nuts | 340 | 10 g |
| Dinner | Kidney beans (200 g drained) with canned chicken (100 g) and 10 saltines | 540 | 41 g |
| Evening | 2 tablespoons peanut butter | 190 | 7 g |
| **Total** | | **1,780** | **99 g** |

Add a can of fruit, a can of vegetables, and a cup of shelf-stable milk and the day reaches about 2,000 calories or a little more, with fluid and fiber the dry foods lack. Notice what the table shows: protein is easy on canned food. Calories are the hard part, and the nuts and peanut butter carry them.

## A Three-Day Rotation

Eating the same thing every day gets hard fast, and people who are bored with their food stop eating enough.

| | Breakfast | Lunch | Dinner | Snacks |
|---|-----------|-------|--------|--------|
| **Day 1** | Cereal with shelf-stable milk, dried fruit | Tuna on crackers, canned peaches | Peanut butter and jelly tortillas, canned green beans | Nuts, granola bar |
| **Day 2** | Soaked oats with raisins and peanut butter | Three-bean salad (canned beans, canned corn, a dressing packet), crackers | Canned chicken in tortillas with salsa, applesauce | Trail mix, canned fruit |
| **Day 3** | Granola bars, canned fruit, shelf-stable milk | Canned chili eaten from the can, crackers | Canned salmon, canned potatoes, canned carrots | Peanut butter on crackers |

Single-serve packets of mustard, mayonnaise, hot sauce, and salad dressing keep without refrigeration until opened and make canned food much easier to eat. Coffee, tea, cocoa, and hard candy cost little and matter a lot. FEMA's point about familiar foods applies here: they lift morale and give a feeling of security under stress.

## Food Safety With No Stove and No Fridge

**Commercially canned food is cooked in the can.** It is safe to eat cold. Home-canned vegetables and meats are different: CDC advises boiling them for at least 10 minutes before eating, so they are not no-cook foods.

**Check every can first.** Do not eat from cans that are swollen, dented, or corroded, and do not eat food that looks or smells abnormal even if the can looked fine. Never taste food to test it.

**The 2-hour rule.** Once opened, canned meat, fish, beans, milk, and anything you have mixed (tuna salad, bean salad, soaked oats) is perishable. With no refrigeration, eat it within 2 hours, or within 1 hour if it is hotter than 90°F (32°C), and throw away what is left. This is the rule that should shape your shopping: small cans, shared between people, opened one meal at a time.

**Shelf-stable milk** is only shelf-stable until you open it. Pour what you will drink. Mix powdered milk a glass at a time, with safe water.

**Clean hands, clean utensils.** Use hand sanitizer or soap and water before eating, wipe can tops before opening them, and use disposable plates and utensils when water is short.

## Salt, Thirst, and Water

FEMA's guidance is direct: if your water supply is limited, do not eat salty foods, because they make you thirsty. Choose salt-free crackers, whole-grain cereals, and canned foods with a high liquid content instead.

| If water is short, go easy on | Choose instead |
|-------------------------------|----------------|
| Jerky and cured meat sticks | Canned tuna, chicken, or beans |
| Salted nuts, chips, pretzels | Unsalted nuts, unsalted peanut butter |
| Salted crackers | Unsalted-top or salt-free crackers |
| Dry foods that need water added | Canned fruit, vegetables, and soups with their liquid |

A normally active adult needs about 3/4 gallon (about 3 liters) of fluid a day, and more in heat. **Never ration drinking water to make it last.** Drink what you need today and look for more tomorrow (see the Water Rationing Guidelines guide).

## People Who Need a Different Plan

**Babies.** Breastfeeding needs no supplies. Otherwise store ready-to-feed formula, which needs no water. Never water formula down. Use prepared formula within 2 hours of preparing it and within 1 hour of the start of a feeding, then throw the rest away. No honey before a child's first birthday.

**Young children.** Several classic no-cook foods are choking hazards for small children: whole nuts, spoonfuls of peanut butter, raisins and other dried fruit, whole grapes, hard candy, granola bars, popcorn, chips, pretzels, and whole beans (CDC). Spread peanut butter thin, mash beans, cut soft canned fruit small, and keep children seated and watched while they eat.

**Diabetes.** Keep meals regular. Skipped or delayed meals can cause low blood sugar in people who take insulin or certain diabetes pills. Stock fast sugar: half a cup (4 oz) of regular juice or soda, or a tablespoon of sugar or honey, treats a low.

**Older adults and anyone who has trouble chewing.** Soft foods: canned fruit, applesauce, soups, and nutrition drinks. Choose pull-tab cans or pouches if hand strength is limited.

**Allergies and medical diets.** Read every label now, not during the emergency, and keep that person's food in its own marked box.

**Pets.** Store their food and their water too.

## Storing and Rotating

- Keep the box somewhere cool, dry, and dark, off the floor.
- FEMA's rule of thumb: replace boxed crackers, dried fruit, and boxed powdered milk within about 6 months, and canned foods, peanut butter, cereal, and canned nuts within a year or by the date on the label. See the Emergency Food Storage Basics guide for longer-term storage.
- Date everything with a marker. New items go to the back.
- Eat from the box and replace what you eat. Food you have never tasted is a gamble in an emergency.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Tonight, lay out one no-cook dinner for your household using only the cupboard and a manual can opener. Open one can with the manual opener to prove it works. Add up the calories from the labels and compare the total with roughly 600 to 700 per adult, a third of a 2,000-calorie day. If you could not find the opener or the meal came up short, you have your shopping list.

**What would you do?**

1. *It is a hot afternoon, the power is out, and it is 95°F (35°C) in the house. You open a large can of chicken for lunch, eat half, and cover the rest to finish at dinner in five hours.*

   **Answer:** Throw the rest out now. Opened canned meat is perishable, and above 90°F the limit is 1 hour, not 2. Covering it does nothing. It will look and smell fine at dinner, which is what makes this mistake common. Next time open a small can, or share a large one so it is finished at one sitting.

2. *Your household is down to a day and a half of water. Dinner can be jerky, salted peanuts, and crackers, or canned peaches, canned green beans, and unsalted peanut butter on tortillas.*

   **Answer:** The second menu. Salty food raises thirst, and the liquid in the peaches and beans adds fluid. Do not respond by drinking less: drink what you need and put your effort into finding more water.

3. *Your two-year-old is hungry and upset. The fastest things within reach are a handful of trail mix and a spoonful of peanut butter.*

   **Answer:** Both are choking hazards at that age: whole nuts, raisins, and peanut butter by the spoonful. Take one more minute. Spread the peanut butter thinly on a cracker or tortilla, or offer applesauce, mashed beans, or soft canned fruit cut small. Sit the child down and stay close while they eat.
