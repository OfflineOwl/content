# When to Self-Treat vs. Seek Help

> In an emergency you may have to handle medical problems yourself. Knowing which problems can't wait, and which you can safely manage, can save a life.

## Do this now

1. **Check the "Call 911 now" list below.** If anything on it matches, call 911 (or your local emergency number, such as 112 or 999) right away.
2. **If phones are down,** send someone for help (fire station, hospital, shelter, neighbor with a radio) while you give first aid. Write down what you see and when.
3. **Start first aid while you wait:** CPR for someone who's unresponsive and not breathing normally, firm pressure or a tourniquet for severe bleeding, an epinephrine auto-injector for anaphylaxis.
4. **Not on the list?** Use the sections below to decide whether you can manage it, and keep watching for signs that it's getting worse.

## Call 911 Now

These need emergency care. Don't wait to see if they get better.

### Stroke: think BE FAST

| Letter | Check |
|--------|-------|
| **B**alance | Do they suddenly feel off-balance or dizzy? |
| **E**yes | Do they suddenly have trouble seeing normally? |
| **F**ace | Ask them to smile. Does one side of the face droop? |
| **A**rms | Ask them to raise both arms. Does one arm drift down? |
| **S**peech | Ask them to repeat a simple phrase. Is it slurred or strange? |
| **T**ime | If you see any of these, call 911 right away. |

Also call for sudden numbness or weakness of the face, arm, or leg (especially on one side), sudden confusion or trouble understanding, or a sudden severe headache with no known cause.

**Write down the time the symptoms started**; doctors use it to choose treatment. Call an ambulance rather than driving; treatment can start on the way.

### Heart attack

- Chest discomfort (pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain) that lasts more than a few minutes, or goes away and comes back
- Discomfort in one or both arms, the back, neck, jaw, or stomach
- Shortness of breath, with or without chest discomfort
- Cold sweat, nausea or vomiting, lightheadedness, or unusual tiredness

Women, older adults, and people with diabetes are more likely to have the less typical symptoms (shortness of breath, nausea, unusual tiredness, back or jaw pain).

**While waiting:** keep them resting. If they're an alert adult with chest pain, aren't allergic to aspirin, and haven't been told by a clinician not to take it, they can chew one regular 325 mg aspirin or 2 to 4 low-dose (81 mg) aspirin. If you're unsure, skip the aspirin and wait for help. If they become unresponsive and aren't breathing normally, start CPR and send for an AED.

### Other "call now" emergencies

- **Not breathing, or only gasping:** start CPR.
- **Choking:** can't breathe, cough, or speak.
- **Unresponsive**, or can't be woken.
- **Severe bleeding:** spurting, pooling, soaking through bandages, or an amputation.
- **Anaphylaxis (severe allergic reaction):** trouble breathing, wheezing, swelling of the tongue, lips, or throat, a hoarse voice, faintness, or widespread hives with vomiting after a sting, food, or medicine. Help them use their epinephrine auto-injector right away, then call.
- **Trouble breathing** of any kind: can't speak in full sentences, lips or face turning blue or gray, ribs sucking in.
- **Seizure** that lasts more than 5 minutes, repeated seizures without waking in between, a first-ever seizure, a seizure in water, in a pregnant person, or in a baby under 6 months, a seizure with injury or trouble breathing, or not back to normal 5–10 minutes after it stops.
- **Poisoning or overdose:** collapsed, seizing, trouble breathing, or can't be woken. For a suspected opioid overdose, give naloxone if you have it, but don't delay CPR or the call. If the person is awake and well, call Poison Help (US: 1-800-222-1222) for advice.
- **Major trauma:** a fall from height, a vehicle crash, a deep wound to the head, neck, chest, belly, or groin, or a suspected spine injury.
- **Head injury with danger signs:** passing out, growing drowsiness or can't be woken, a worsening headache, repeated vomiting, a seizure, slurred speech, weakness or numbness, confusion or unusual behavior, or one pupil larger than the other.
- **Serious burns:** deep burns, burns larger than the person's palm, burns on the face, hands, feet, genitals, or across joints, burns that go around a limb, electrical or chemical burns, or signs of smoke inhalation (burns on the face, singed nose hair, soot around the mouth or nose, trouble breathing).
- **Signs of shock:** pale, cold, clammy skin, a fast weak pulse, fast breathing, confusion, after injury, bleeding, or illness.
- **Possible sepsis:** an infection plus confusion, shortness of breath, a high heart rate or weak pulse, fever with shivering or feeling very cold, extreme pain, or clammy, sweaty skin.
- **Possible meningitis:** sudden fever with a headache and stiff neck, especially with sensitivity to light or confusion. Babies may instead feed poorly, be very irritable, or have a bulging soft spot.
- **Any fever in a baby under 3 months:** 100.4°F (38°C) or higher.
- **Heat stroke:** body temperature 103°F (39.4°C) or higher, hot, red, dry or damp skin, a fast strong pulse, confusion, or passing out. Move them somewhere cooler and cool them with cool cloths or a cool bath while you wait. Don't give them anything to drink.
- **Hypothermia:** confusion, slurred speech, fumbling hands, memory loss, or drowsiness in the cold (in babies: bright red, cold skin and very low energy). A body temperature below 95°F (35°C) is an emergency.
- **Suspected carbon monoxide poisoning:** headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, or confusion, especially in several people sharing a space with a generator, grill, heater, or car running. Get everyone outside into fresh air first, then call.
- **Pregnancy or recent birth (up to a year after):** heavy bleeding (soaking a pad in an hour or less), a severe headache that won't go away, vision changes, chest pain or a racing heart, trouble breathing, fainting, severe belly pain, a seizure, or thoughts of harming herself or the baby.
- **Thoughts of suicide:** in the US, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, 24/7). Call 911 if someone is in immediate danger.

**These can't be safely managed at home.** Do what first aid you can and keep trying to reach help.

## The Decision Framework

For anything not on the list above, ask:

1. **Is it life-threatening, or could it become so?**
2. **Is it getting worse?**
3. **Do I have the skills and supplies to treat it safely?**
4. **What happens if I wait?**
5. **What are the risks of treating it myself?**

**When in doubt, seek help.** A problem that's getting worse needs help sooner, even if it looked minor at first.

## Usually Safe to Manage Yourself

### Minor cuts and scrapes

**Self-treat if:**
- Bleeding stops with firm, steady pressure (call for help if it hasn't stopped after 10 minutes)
- The wound is shallow (no fat, muscle, or bone visible)
- Edges come together on their own
- No embedded object
- Not a bite or a puncture from a dirty object
- Tetanus vaccination is up to date

**Treat:** rinse with clean running water, wash around it with soap, cover it with a clean bandage, and change it daily.

**Get care if:** it won't stop bleeding, it's deep or gaping, it's on the face or over a joint, it's a bite, it has something embedded, or it shows signs of infection (spreading redness, warmth, pus, red streaks, fever).

### Minor burns

**Self-treat if:**
- Red skin with no blisters, or small blisters on a small area
- Not on the face, hands, feet, genitals, or across a major joint
- Not electrical or chemical, and no smoke inhalation

**Treat:** cool under clean, cool running water right away for 5 to 20 minutes (aim for 20). Then cover loosely with a clean, dry, non-stick dressing or plastic wrap. Don't put ice directly on it, don't use butter or home remedies, and don't pop blisters.

**Get care if:** the burn is deep (white, leathery, or charred), larger than the person's palm, on the face, hands, feet, genitals, or a joint, goes around a limb, is electrical or chemical, or the person is a young child or older adult.

### Sprains and strains

**Self-treat if:** they can bear some weight, there's no deformity, numbness, or tingling, and the pain is manageable.

**Treat:** avoid painful activity, and use a cold pack or ice wrapped in a damp cloth (up to 20 minutes at a time, never directly on skin). A snug (not tight) elastic wrap can help an ankle.

**Get care if:** they can't bear weight at all, there's deformity or numbness, swelling is severe right away, or it isn't improving after a few days.

### Mild dehydration

**Self-treat if:** the person is alert, can drink, and has mild signs (thirst, dark urine, dry mouth).

**Treat:** small, frequent sips of oral rehydration solution or water; rest somewhere cool.

**Get care if:** they can't keep fluids down, they're confused or very drowsy, they've barely urinated, or they're a baby, a frail older adult, or show signs of shock.

### Fever

**Self-treat if:** the person is alert, drinking fluids, and has no warning signs, and isn't a baby under 3 months.

**Treat:** fluids, rest, light clothing. Fever reducers (acetaminophen or ibuprofen) can help comfort; follow the label dose for age and weight. **Never give aspirin to children or teenagers.**

**Get care if:** any fever in a baby under 3 months; a temperature that rises above 104°F (40°C) repeatedly; fever with a stiff neck, an unexplained rash, a severe headache, confusion, trouble breathing, a seizure, or signs of dehydration; a child's fever lasting more than 24 hours under age 2, or more than 3 days at age 2 or older; or the person looks very ill.

### Diarrhea and vomiting

**Self-treat if:** mild symptoms, they can keep some fluids down, there's no blood, and no severe belly pain.

**Treat:** oral rehydration solution in small, frequent sips; bland food as they can manage; careful handwashing so it doesn't spread.

**Get care if:** blood in stool or vomit, can't keep fluids down, signs of dehydration, severe belly pain, high fever, symptoms lasting more than a couple of days, or the person is a baby, older adult, or has a weak immune system.

### Colds and mild flu

**Self-treat if:** typical symptoms (congestion, sore throat, mild fever), able to eat and drink, no trouble breathing.

**Treat:** rest, fluids, and over-the-counter relievers as labeled. Honey can ease a cough in anyone over 1 year old. **Never give honey to a baby under 1.**

**Get care if:** trouble breathing, chest pain, confusion, persistent vomiting, symptoms that improve and then get worse, or the person is high risk (older adult, pregnant, very young, or chronically ill).

## Gray-Zone Problems

These need careful judgment and professional care as soon as it's available.

### Possible fractures

**You can stabilize temporarily if:** the skin is intact, the hand or foot beyond the injury is warm with normal color and feeling, and you can immobilize it.

**Do:** splint it in the position you found it, immobilizing the joints above and below; apply cold wrapped in cloth; check circulation often.

**Get care urgently if:** bone is visible (open fracture), the limb beyond the injury is cold, pale, blue, or numb, there's a severe deformity, or the injury is to the hip, thigh, pelvis, spine, or head.

### Wound infections

**You may manage early on if:** redness is limited to the wound edges, there are no red streaks, and there's no fever.

**Do:** clean the wound 2–3 times a day, use warm moist compresses, mark the edge of the redness, and check it often.

**Get care if:** redness spreads past your mark, red streaks appear, a fever develops, or the person feels ill. Any sign of sepsis is an emergency.

### Asthma flare-ups

**You may manage it if:** they have their quick-relief (rescue) inhaler, respond to it, and can speak in full sentences.

**Do:** sit them upright, help them use their quick-relief inhaler exactly as their asthma action plan or prescription says (with a spacer if they have one), move them away from smoke or other triggers, and keep them calm. If they're still struggling about 15 minutes later, get emergency help.

**Call 911 if:** the inhaler isn't helping, they can't speak in full sentences, lips or fingernails are turning blue or gray, the skin between the ribs sucks in with each breath, or they're getting worse.

### Allergic reactions

**You may manage it if:** symptoms are mild (hives, itching, a runny nose) with no breathing trouble, throat tightness, faintness, or vomiting.

**Do:** remove the trigger, give an oral antihistamine as labeled, and watch closely for at least an hour.

**Treat as anaphylaxis and call 911 if** any breathing difficulty, throat tightness, hoarse voice, swelling of the tongue or lips, dizziness or fainting, or vomiting develops. Use their epinephrine auto-injector right away; antihistamines are too slow and don't replace epinephrine.

## When Help Is Hours or Days Away

```
Is this immediately life-threatening?
├── YES → Give first aid, keep trying to reach help by any means
└── NO  → Continue ↓

Is it getting worse?
├── YES → Higher priority: send for help now
└── NO  → Continue ↓

Can I treat it safely with what I have?
├── YES → Treat, write down what you did, and recheck often
└── NO  → Continue ↓

What happens if I wait?
├── Likely to get much worse → Prioritize getting help
└── Stable or improving      → Keep treating and keep watching
```

**If you can't reach help:**

1. **Do what you can** with what you have.
2. **Write everything down:** times, symptoms, treatments, and how the person responded.
3. **Recheck regularly** and watch for the "Call 911 now" signs.
4. **Keep trying** to reach help (phone, text, radio, runners, signals). Call 911 if you can; text 911 if you can't. Where texting 911 isn't available, you should get a bounce-back message saying so.
5. **Keep them comfortable,** warm or cool as needed, and hydrated if they can swallow.
6. **Don't blame yourself** for what you can't fix.

## Know Your Limits

**Most people can learn:** CPR and AED use, bleeding control with pressure and a tourniquet, wound cleaning and bandaging, splinting, recognizing a stroke or heart attack, helping someone use their own inhaler or epinephrine auto-injector, oral rehydration.

**Needs professional training:** closing wounds with stitches, setting (reducing) fractures, giving injections other than an auto-injector, IV fluids, advanced airway care, choosing and dosing prescription medicines, diagnosing internal problems.

Take a first aid and CPR course from the American Red Cross, the American Heart Association, or a local equivalent. Practice beats reading.

## Quick Reference

| Problem | Manage yourself? | Get help when |
|---------|------------------|---------------|
| Small cut | Yes | Won't stop bleeding, gaping, bite, infected |
| Deep wound | Control bleeding | Always |
| Minor burn | Yes | Face, hands, feet, genitals, joints, large, electrical, chemical |
| Large or deep burn | Cool and cover | Always (call 911) |
| Sprain | Yes | Can't bear weight, deformed, numb |
| Possible fracture | Splint | As soon as possible; open fracture is an emergency |
| Mild fever | Yes | Baby under 3 months, stiff neck, rash, confusion, more than 3 days |
| Mild dehydration | Yes | Can't keep fluids down, confused, baby or frail adult |
| Mild allergy | Yes | Any breathing, throat, or fainting symptoms (call 911) |
| Chest pain | No | Always (call 911) |
| Stroke signs | No | Always (call 911) |
| Severe bleeding | Pressure, tourniquet | Always (call 911) |
| Unresponsive | CPR if not breathing normally | Always (call 911) |
| Trouble breathing | No | Always (call 911) |
