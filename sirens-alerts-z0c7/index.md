# Understanding Sirens & Alert Systems

> **A siren tells you that something is wrong.** It does not tell you what. Your phone's alert is the same until you read it. Get inside, find out what the warning is for, and do what it says. Minutes matter.

## Do This Now

If an outdoor siren sounds, or your phone, radio, or TV sounds an emergency alert:

1. TREAT IT AS REAL. The only exception is your community's known siren test time.
2. READ OR LISTEN TO THE WHOLE ALERT. If it names an action (take shelter, evacuate, move to higher ground, stay indoors), do that now. Don't go looking for a second opinion first.
3. SIREN ONLY, NO MESSAGE? Go indoors, then find out why: check your phone for an alert, turn on a NOAA Weather Radio, or tune to local radio or TV.
4. DON'T GO OUTSIDE TO LOOK. Many tornadoes are hidden by rain or darkness, and some hazards can't be seen at all.
5. DON'T CALL 911 TO ASK WHY THE SIRENS ARE SOUNDING. Call 911 (or your local emergency number) only for an actual emergency.
6. STAY PROTECTED UNTIL THE WARNING EXPIRES or officials say the danger has passed. Sirens do not sound an all-clear. Silence does not mean safe.

---

## Get Emergency Help If

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- Someone is hurt, trapped, or in immediate danger.
- You are in the warned area, in danger, and cannot get to safety on your own.
- You see a fire, a collapse, or another life-threatening emergency.

Do NOT call 911 to ask what a siren means, whether a test is real, or when the warning will end. Those calls tie up the lines that people in danger need. Use the radio, TV, or your phone's alert instead.

---

## No Single System Reaches Everyone

Every warning system has a gap. Sirens can't be relied on indoors. Phone alerts miss phones that are off. Radio and TV alerts only reach people who are tuned in. So build layers, and aim for three:

- ONE THAT WAKES YOU: a NOAA Weather Radio with tone alert.
- ONE THAT FOLLOWS YOU: Wireless Emergency Alerts on your phone.
- ONE THAT GIVES DETAIL: local radio or TV, and your city or county's own alert service.

The sections below cover each one. For radio hardware and setup, see the Radio Use for Emergency Updates guide.

## Outdoor Warning Sirens

WHO THEY ARE FOR: people who are outdoors. The National Weather Service (NWS) describes sirens as an outdoor warning system designed only to alert those who are outside. You may not hear one indoors, in a vehicle, over a storm, or while asleep. Never make a siren your only warning.

WHAT ONE MEANS: something life-threatening is happening, so go indoors and get more information. That is all a siren can say.

WHO SOUNDS THEM: city or county officials (police, fire, or emergency management), not the NWS. Each community sets its own rules, so practice varies widely:

- Some sound sirens only for tornado warnings.
- Others also sound them for severe thunderstorms with winds of 70 mph (113 km/h) or more, or hail the size of golf balls.
- Some use them for chemical spills, other local emergencies, or tsunamis on the coast.
- Some sound only the sirens in the threatened part of a county.
- Officials may sound them on their own judgment, even before the NWS issues a warning.

TONES: many communities use one tone for everything. Others use two. Illinois, for example, uses a steady "alert" tone for storms, tornado warnings, and chemical incidents, and a rising-and-falling "attack" tone for an attack on the country. Don't assume your town matches a pattern you read somewhere. Look yours up.

HOW LONG THEY SOUND: usually about 3 minutes, then they stop to save the motor and the backup battery. Some places sound them again every few minutes. A siren that has gone quiet tells you nothing about whether the danger is over.

NO ALL-CLEAR: sirens do not sound an all-clear. The end of the danger is announced on radio, TV, and phones, or the warning simply expires.

TESTS: each community picks its own schedule. Illinois tests on the first Tuesday of each month at 10 a.m.; your town may use another day, time, or season. Find your schedule on your city or county emergency management website and write it in the Quick Reference below.

## Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA) on Your Phone

A WEA is a short message that looks like a text and arrives with a special tone and vibration, both repeated twice. Authorized officials send it through cell towers to every capable phone in the threatened area.

- Free. No sign-up, no app.
- It is broadcast from towers, like radio, so it does not track you and it still works when the network is jammed.
- It follows you. Travel into a warned area and your phone gets the alert there.
- Up to 360 characters on current networks (90 on old ones). Spanish is supported when officials send it.
- It will not interrupt a call in progress.

KINDS OF ALERT:

- National Alerts, sent only in a national emergency. Older phones and pages call them Presidential Alerts. You cannot turn these off.
- Imminent threat alerts: dangerous weather and local emergencies that need evacuation or immediate action.
- AMBER Alerts for abducted children, and in some areas Blue Alerts.

WHICH WEATHER WARNINGS BUZZ YOUR PHONE: the NWS sends WEAs for tsunami, tornado, hurricane, typhoon, storm surge, extreme wind, dust storm, and snow squall warnings. It sends them for flash flood warnings only when the damage threat is "considerable" or "catastrophic," and for severe thunderstorm warnings only when it is "destructive." So a quiet phone does not mean no warning is in effect.

LIMITS TO KNOW:

- A phone that is off or in airplane mode is not listening to the cell towers and will not get the alert. In storm season, leave it on overnight and use low power mode to save battery instead.
- You can turn off imminent threat and AMBER alerts in your phone's settings. Don't. One 3 a.m. alert about a missing child is not a reason to switch off tornado warnings.
- On some phones alerts have to be switched on, and very old phones can't receive them. Look in your settings for "Emergency Alerts," "Government Alerts," or "Emergency Alert Messages."
- Newer phones can target an alert to within about a tenth of a mile (160 m). Older ones may get an alert meant for the next town, or miss one meant for them.
- The message is short on purpose. Read it, act, then get details from another source.

## Emergency Alert System (EAS) on Radio and Tv

EAS is the national public warning system. It interrupts broadcast radio and TV, cable, and satellite radio and TV with an alert tone and a message.

- It was built so the President can address the country within 10 minutes in a national emergency.
- In practice it is used most by the NWS for dangerous weather, and by state and local officials for AMBER Alerts and local emergencies.
- Its gap: you only get it if a radio or TV is on.

---

## NOAA Weather Radio (Nwr)

NWR is a network of more than 1,000 NWS transmitters covering all 50 states, coastal waters, and US territories. It broadcasts forecasts, watches, and warnings 24 hours a day, and it carries non-weather emergencies too: chemical releases, earthquakes, AMBER Alerts, even 911 outages.

WHY IT IS WORTH HAVING: a weather radio with a tone alarm sits silent until the NWS sends a warning, then sounds a loud alarm (a 1050 Hz tone) and reads the message. It will wake you at 3 a.m. A siren will not.

- You need a receiver with the weather band. Home models start at about $20.
- Seven channels: 162.400, 162.425, 162.450, 162.475, 162.500, 162.525, and 162.550 MHz.
- SAME lets you set the radio to alarm only for your county. Your SAME code is 6 digits. Find it at weather.gov/nwr/counties, or call or text "Go" to 1-888-697-7263.
- Buy one with battery backup, and keep it plugged in so the batteries are full when the power fails.
- The alarm is normally tested each Wednesday between 10 a.m. and noon local time, on fair-weather days.
- Reception varies with terrain and distance. Test the radio in the spot where you plan to keep it.

## City and County Alerts, and Apps

- Many cities and counties run their own text, call, or email alert service. Unlike WEA, you have to sign up. These carry the local detail: which streets to evacuate, where the shelter is, when a boil-water notice ends. Search for your county's name plus "emergency alerts."
- The FEMA app sends NWS alerts for up to five places you choose, such as your home, your work, and a relative's town.
- Local TV, radio, and official social media accounts fill in the picture. Use them for detail, not as your first warning.

## Watch, Warning, Advisory

These NWS words tell you how sure and how serious the threat is.

- WATCH = BE READY. The risk has gone up, but where and when is still uncertain. Review your plan, charge your phone, know where you will shelter.
- WARNING = ACT NOW. A threat to life or property is happening or about to happen.
- ADVISORY = BE CAREFUL. Less serious conditions that can still hurt you if you ignore them.
- EMERGENCY (as in Tornado Emergency) = the highest level. A severe threat to life is under way.

A memory aid: a watch means the ingredients are on the counter. A warning means it is in the oven.

## What to Do for Each Warning

TORNADO WARNING: go to an interior room on the lowest floor of a sturdy building, away from windows. Leave a mobile home or vehicle for the nearest sturdy shelter. See the Tornado Safety guide.

SEVERE THUNDERSTORM WARNING: get inside a substantial building. Leave a mobile home, which high winds can overturn.

FLASH FLOOD WARNING: if you are in a flood-prone spot, move to higher ground right away. Do not walk or drive into flood water. See the Flood Response & Water Safety guide.

TSUNAMI WARNING: move to high ground or inland, away from the water. Aim for 100 feet (30 m) or more above sea level, or at least 1 mile (1.6 km) inland, and follow local evacuation signs. A TSUNAMI ADVISORY means strong, dangerous currents: stay out of the water and off beaches and harbors. See the Tsunami Response guide.

NATURE'S OWN TSUNAMI WARNING: at the coast, a strong or long earthquake, a sudden rise or fall of the sea, or a loud roar from the ocean is your warning. Protect yourself during the shaking, then go at once. Do not wait for a siren or a phone alert.

CHEMICAL OR HAZARDOUS MATERIAL ALERT: do exactly what it says, which will be either EVACUATE or STAY INDOORS.

- If told to evacuate, leave immediately.
- If told to stay indoors: bring pets in, close and lock all outside doors and windows, turn off fans, heating, and air conditioning that pull in outside air, close vents and fireplace dampers, and go to an interior room. Seal gaps with plastic sheeting and duct tape, or wet towels, if you have them. Keep the radio on. See the Shelter-in-Place Procedures guide.

AMBER OR OTHER PUBLIC SAFETY ALERT: read it. If you see the person or vehicle described, call 911 or the number in the alert.

## Common Mistakes

- WAITING FOR THE SIREN. It may not sound for your hazard, and you may not hear it. Act on the first warning from any source.
- ASSUMING A QUIET PHONE MEANS NO WARNING. Many warnings never trigger a phone alert.
- TREATING SILENCE AS AN ALL-CLEAR when the sirens stop after 3 minutes.
- IGNORING A SIREN BECAUSE THE SKY IS CLEAR. Sirens are used for more than weather.
- SWITCHING ALERTS OFF after one that annoyed you.

---

## People Who Need Extra Thought

- DEAF OR HARD OF HEARING: some weather radios have a jack for a strobe light or bed shaker. Phone alerts vibrate as well as sound, and many phones can flash or read alerts aloud.
- SOUND SLEEPERS AND OLDER ADULTS: don't count on sirens at night. Put a tone-alert weather radio in or near the bedroom.
- CHILDREN: a child's phone gets WEAs too. Teach them what the sound means and what to do if they are alone: go inside, read it, do what it says, then call you.
- VISITORS AND NEW ARRIVALS: your phone's alerts follow you, but siren meanings and test days change from town to town. Ask.
- OUTSIDE THE US: alert systems and siren signals differ by country. Ask locally or check the national emergency agency's website when you arrive.

## Alert Setup Checklist

- [ ] Emergency alerts turned on, on every phone in the household
- [ ] NOAA Weather Radio with tone alarm, SAME, and battery backup
- [ ] County SAME code programmed and the right channel selected
- [ ] Signed up for city or county alerts
- [ ] FEMA app or a local news or weather app with notifications on
- [ ] Local siren test day and time written down
- [ ] What your community sounds its sirens for written down
- [ ] Battery or hand-crank AM/FM radio and spare batteries
- [ ] Everyone in the household knows: a siren means go in and find out

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Do an alert audit. (1) On every phone in the house, find the emergency alert settings and confirm nothing is switched off. Time how long the slowest person takes to find them. (2) Look up your city or county emergency management page and write down the siren test day and time and what the sirens are sounded for. (3) Look up your county's SAME code and NWR channel and write them on the Quick Reference card. If you own a weather radio, check that it plays the local broadcast.

### What Would You Do?

1. It is a clear, calm Thursday afternoon and the outdoor sirens start. It is not the test day. Your phone shows nothing. A neighbor shrugs and keeps mowing.

   **Answer:** Go inside and find out. A clear sky rules out very little: sirens are also used for chemical releases and other local emergencies, and many warnings never trigger a phone alert. Turn on local radio or TV or the weather radio. If it turns out to be a malfunction, you have lost five minutes.

2. A tornado warning sent you to the basement ten minutes ago. The sirens have stopped, the rain has eased, and the children want to go back up to their show.

   **Answer:** Stay put. Sirens usually run for about 3 minutes and then shut off whether or not the danger has passed, and there is no all-clear siren. Stay sheltered until the warning's expiry time has passed or the radio or your phone says it was cancelled. A lull in the rain means nothing: many tornadoes are hidden in rain, and more storms may follow.

3. You are on a beach on holiday. The ground shakes for what feels like a full minute. Afterward everything looks normal: no siren, nothing on your phone, people going back to their towels.

   **Answer:** Leave now. At the coast a strong or long earthquake is the warning, and the first wave can arrive before any official alert. Walk fast to high ground or inland, aiming for 100 feet (30 m) up or a mile (1.6 km) in, and bring whoever will come. Stay there until officials say it is safe. Flooding and dangerous currents can go on for hours or days.
