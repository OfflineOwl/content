Earthquake Response & Aftermath

Drop, Cover, and Hold On. Earthquakes strike without warning. Most earthquake injuries come from falling and flying objects, and from people trying to move while the ground is shaking.

Do this now

When the shaking starts:

  1. DROP where you are, onto your hands and knees. This keeps the shaking from knocking you down and lets you crawl. Don't try to move more than a few steps (5 to 7 feet) before getting down.
  2. COVER your head and neck with one arm and hand. If a sturdy table or desk is close, crawl under it. If not, crawl next to an interior wall, away from windows, and stay low, bent over to protect your vital organs.
  3. HOLD ON until the shaking stops. Under a table, hold on to it with one hand and be ready to move with it. With no shelter, hold your head and neck with both arms and hands.
  4. Stay put until the shaking stops. Don't run outside or to another room.
  5. When it stops: check for injuries, expect aftershocks, and get out if the building is damaged. If you're near the coast and the shaking was strong or long, walk to high ground or inland as soon as you can stand (tsunami risk).

Two Dangerous Myths

Don't stand in a doorway. This is out-of-date advice. Doorways are no stronger than any other part of a house, they don't protect you from falling or flying objects, and they won't be a safe space in the rare case of a collapse. You're safer under a sturdy table.

The "triangle of life" is wrong. This claim says you should curl up next to a large object, not under a table, so you end up in a "void" if the building collapses. The US Geological Survey calls it a misguided idea, and the Earthquake Country Alliance (which runs the ShakeOut drills) says not to follow it:

  • It was based on observations of collapsed buildings in one earthquake in Turkey, and doesn't apply to buildings in the US and many other countries, where most buildings don't collapse.
  • Most earthquake injuries come from falling and flying objects, which Drop, Cover, and Hold On protects you from.
  • No one can predict where voids will form, and trying to move to a "better" spot during strong shaking is how many people get hurt.

Don't run outside during shaking. Glass and exterior building materials can fall on you as you go out the door.

Where You Are When It Starts

In Bed

Stay there. Lie face down and cover your head and neck with a pillow. Getting up in the dark, over broken glass, causes more injuries than staying in bed.

Wheelchair, Walker, or Cane Users: Lock, Cover, Hold On

  • Wheelchair: lock your wheels (or set the brake) and stay seated. Bend over and cover your head and neck with your arms, a pillow, or a book. Hold on until the shaking stops.
  • Walker: carefully get as low as you can, or sit in a chair or on a bed. Bend over and cover your head and neck.
  • Cane: keep it near you. If you can't get down, sit and bend over, covering your head and neck.

High-Rise Building

Drop, Cover, and Hold On. Stay away from windows. Don't use elevators. Don't be surprised if fire alarms or sprinklers go off. Don't rush for the stairs during shaking.

Theater, Stadium, or Arena

Drop in front of your seat, or lean over as far as you can. Cover your head with your arms and hold your neck. When the shaking stops, walk out slowly, watching for objects that could fall in aftershocks.

Outdoors

Move to a clear area if you can do so safely, away from buildings, trees, streetlights, signs, and power lines. Then Drop, Cover, and Hold On.

Driving

  1. Pull over to a clear spot, away from bridges, overpasses, signs, trees, and power lines. Stop and set the parking brake.
  2. Stay inside until the shaking stops.
  3. Then drive slowly. Watch for cracked roads, fallen debris, and damaged bridges and ramps.
  4. If a power line falls on your car, stay inside until a trained person removes it.

Near the Coast

Drop, Cover, and Hold On. If the shaking is strong (hard to stand) or long (some coastal agencies use 20 seconds or more as a guide), a tsunami may arrive within minutes. If you're on the beach or near the water, treat any earthquake as a warning. As soon as you can stand, walk quickly to high ground or inland. Walk rather than drive, and don't wait for an official warning. Remember: "Long or strong, get gone." See the Tsunami Response guide.

Earthquake Early Warning

Some regions send phone alerts that can give a few seconds' warning, such as ShakeAlert in California, Oregon, and Washington. Alerts come through Wireless Emergency Alerts, the earthquake alerts built into Android and iPhone, or the MyShake app. Depending on how close you are, an alert may arrive before, during, or after the shaking. If you get an alert or feel shaking, Drop, Cover, and Hold On right away.

After the Shaking Stops

Immediate Actions

  1. Check yourself, then others, for injuries. Give first aid. Call 911 (or your local emergency number) for serious injuries. Use the phone only for emergencies, and text instead of calling when you can.
  2. Put on sturdy shoes before walking anywhere. Broken glass is everywhere after a strong quake.
  3. Look for fire. Put out small fires right away if it's safe; otherwise get out and call for help.
  4. Get out of a damaged building and don't go back in until it's been inspected. Use stairs, never elevators.
  5. Expect aftershocks. Drop, Cover, and Hold On every time the ground shakes.

If You Smell Gas

If you smell gas (a rotten-egg smell) or hear hissing:

  1. Don't use matches, lighters, camp stoves, barbecues, light switches, appliances, or anything that could make a spark until you're sure there's no leak.
  2. Open a window if you can do it quickly, then get everyone out.
  3. Turn off the gas at the outside main valve if you know how and can do it safely. Keep a wrench by the meter for this.
  4. Call the gas company from outside, such as from a neighbor's home.
  5. Never turn the gas back on yourself. Only the gas company or a qualified professional should do that.

Only shut off the gas if you smell gas, hear hissing, or suspect a leak from broken pipes. After a big quake, it can take a long time for the gas company to get to everyone.

Electricity and Water

  • If you see sparks or broken wires, or smell hot insulation, turn off power at the main breaker if you can reach it without standing in water.
  • If water or sewer pipes are damaged, turn off the main water valve and don't flush toilets until you know sewer lines are intact.

Signs a Building Is Unsafe

Get out and stay out if you see:

  • Large cracks in walls, ceilings, or the foundation
  • A house shifted on its foundation, or doors and windows that suddenly jam
  • Partial collapse or a leaning structure
  • Gas smell, broken pipes, or electrical damage

Approach a damaged chimney with caution, and don't use it until it's inspected.

If You're Trapped

  • Cover your mouth and nose with cloth to keep out dust.
  • Don't light a match or lighter.
  • Tap on a pipe or wall or use a whistle so rescuers can find you. Shout only as a last resort, because shouting makes you inhale dust.
  • Send a text if you have a signal.

Aftershocks

  • Aftershocks are earthquakes that follow the main shock in the same area. Most are smaller than the main shock, but they can still be damaging or deadly, especially to buildings already weakened.
  • They're most frequent right after the main shock and become less frequent over time: there are about 10 times as many on the first day as on the tenth. They can continue for days to years.
  • A large aftershock can still come later. Aftershocks become less frequent over time, but not smaller.
  • Sometimes an earthquake is followed by a larger one, and the first quake turns out to have been a foreshock. Worldwide, the chance of a larger quake nearby within a week is about 5%.
  • After significant US quakes, the US Geological Survey (USGS) publishes aftershock forecasts. Follow local officials' advice.

Respond to every aftershock the same way: Drop, Cover, and Hold On.

Stress and Sleep

Anxiety, jumpiness, and trouble sleeping are normal after an earthquake and during aftershocks. Children may need extra reassurance and routine. Practicing Drop, Cover, and Hold On together can help them feel more in control.

Recovery

First Hours

  • Account for everyone in your household
  • Treat injuries; get help for serious ones
  • Check for fire, gas leaks, and electrical damage
  • Put on shoes and gloves before moving through debris
  • Let an out-of-area contact know you're safe (text if calls fail)
  • Listen to a battery or hand-crank radio for official information

First Days

  • Stay out of damaged buildings until inspected
  • Photograph damage before cleanup, for insurance
  • Check on neighbors, especially older adults and people living alone
  • Keep pets leashed or confined; they may panic and run off
  • Ration supplies until services return

Water and Food Safety

  • Use stored water until officials say tap water is safe. Broken pipes can contaminate the supply. Follow any boil-water notice.
  • If you run out, the water in your water heater tank, ice cubes, and the liquid in canned vegetables can be used. Turn off the water heater's gas or power first. Don't drink pool or spa water.
  • Don't eat or drink anything from open containers near shattered glass.
  • See the Water Purification and Food Safety Without Refrigeration guides for details.

Before the Next Earthquake

Secure Your Space

  • Anchor tall furniture, bookcases, and TVs to wall studs.
  • Strap your water heater to the wall.
  • Put latches on cabinets. Keep heavy and breakable items on low shelves.
  • Don't hang heavy pictures or mirrors over beds or couches.
  • Learn how to shut off gas, water, and electricity, and keep a wrench by the gas meter.

Plan and Practice

  • Practice Drop, Cover, and Hold On with your household. Pick safe spots in each room (under sturdy tables, along interior walls).
  • Plan how you'll reconnect if phones are down, and choose an out-of-area contact.
  • Make a plan for anyone who needs help moving, and for pets.

Supplies

  • Keep shoes, a flashlight, and a whistle by each bed.
  • Keep water, food, medications, and supplies for as long as you can manage. The USGS recommends water for at least two weeks in earthquake country (at least 1 gallon per person per day). Services can be out for a long time after a major quake.
  • Keep a go bag near the exit in case you need to leave.
