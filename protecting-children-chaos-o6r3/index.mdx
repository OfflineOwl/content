# Protecting Children During Chaos

> **A child who gets separated can only be returned if someone can tell who they belong to.** Two things protect children when everything is moving at once: an adult whose only job is that child, and information that travels with the child. Both take minutes to set up, and both have to be done before the day you need them.

## Start here

1. **Assign every child to a named adult,** with a backup. In a crowd or an evacuation, that adult has no other job.
2. **Put a contact card on each child today.** Name, your names and phone numbers, an out-of-town contact, allergies and medicines. Backpack, pocket or shoe.
3. **Take a photo of each child's face now,** and another on the day of any evacuation or big event, showing what they are wearing. Keep one printed in your wallet.
4. **Choose a family password** and teach it: "Only go with a grown-up who knows our word."
5. **Teach the lost rule:** stop, stay where you are, and look for someone in charge, such as a police officer.
6. **Ask your child's school or childcare how they reunite children with families,** and check who is on the pick-up list.

This guide covers physical safety and staying together. For fear, sleep, behavior and routines afterward, see the Re-Establishing Normalcy for Kids guide.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) right away if:

- **A child is missing.** Do not search for an hour first. See the next section.
- **A child is not breathing normally, cannot be woken, is bleeding heavily, or has been pulled from water or smoke.** See the Pediatric Emergency Care guide.
- **A child has been near a running generator, grill or camp stove and has a headache, is dizzy, sick or sleepy.** This may be carbon monoxide. Get everyone into fresh air first.
- **You find a child alone.** Stay with them where they are, and call.

## If your child is missing

1. **Call 911 or local police immediately.** In a declared disaster, also tell shelter staff or any responder on site.
2. **Give the description:** name, age, height, weight, hair and eye color, what they are wearing, and anything they carry or need (glasses, medicine, a comfort toy). Show the photo.
3. **One adult stays at the last place the child was seen** or at the agreed meeting spot, so the child finds someone there if they come back.
4. **Send someone to the places a child would go:** the car, the bathroom, the play area, the pet.
5. **In the US, call the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children: 1-800-843-5678 (1-800-THE-LOST),** open 24 hours. In federally declared disasters it can run the National Emergency Child Locator Center and a registry of children found without their families.
6. **Tell your out-of-town contact,** so anyone who finds your child and calls a number on the card reaches a person who knows what is happening.

Outside the US, call your local emergency number and ask shelter or relief staff about family tracing. Red Cross and Red Crescent societies run family tracing services in many countries.

## Why children are separated, and where

Reunification is the work of getting children back to their parents or guardians as quickly as possible after an emergency. It is needed most often for a simple reason: disasters do not wait for evenings and weekends. Children spend much of the week at school or childcare, and those are the places they are most often apart from their parents when something happens.

The second place is the crowd: an evacuation line, a shelter, a bus loading, a stadium parking lot. A small child lets go of a hand for ten seconds and is behind a wall of adults.

Babies, toddlers and children with some disabilities cannot say who they are. For them, written identification is the only voice they have.

## Identification that travels with the child

| What | How | Why |
|------|-----|-----|
| **Contact card** | Child's name, date of birth, your names and phones, an out-of-town contact, allergies, medicines, conditions. Laminate it or seal it in a zip bag. Backpack, coat pocket, or under a shoe insole. | Anyone who finds the child can reach you, and a medic knows what not to give. |
| **Your phone number on the child** | In a crowd or evacuation, write it on a clothing label, a wristband or a lanyard. | Cards get dropped with the backpack. |
| **Recent photo** | A clear photo of the face on your phone and printed in your wallet. Take a fresh one on the day, in that day's clothes. For a small child, add a photo of the child together with you. | Police and shelter staff can search with it, and a photo of you together helps show the child is yours. |
| **Medical ID** | A bracelet or tag for a child with a serious allergy, diabetes, seizures, autism or a child who does not speak. | It speaks when the child cannot. |
| **Copies of documents** | Photos of birth certificates, passports and custody papers on your phone, and paper copies kept outside the home. | Proving who you are speeds up release from a shelter or hospital. |

Update the card and the photo every six months. Young children change fast, and so do phone numbers.

**What children should carry in their heads,** as soon as they are able:

- Their full name, and their parents' or guardians' full names (not "Mommy")
- One phone number by heart. Make it a song.
- Their address, or at least the town and street
- The name of the out-of-town contact
- How and when to call 911, and to say their name and where they are
- The family password

## The family password and safe adults

**The password.** Pick a word that is easy to remember and hard to guess. Teach one rule: "If someone says they have come to get you, ask for our word. No word, no going, even if you know them." Give the word only to the adults who might really collect your child, and change it if it gets out. In an emergency, the person you send must have it.

**If you get lost.** Teach it in three steps and practice it out loud:

1. **Stop.** Do not go looking. If you can see our meeting spot, go there. If not, stay where you are.
2. **Find someone in charge:** a police officer, a firefighter, a teacher, shelter staff with a badge, or a worker behind a counter.
3. **Say:** "I am lost. My name is ___. My mom's number is ___." Show the card.

**Safe-adult rules worth saying plainly:**

- Do not leave the building, the shelter or the meeting spot with anyone, for any reason, unless they know the password.
- A safe adult does not ask a child to keep a secret or to come and help them look for something.
- If someone tries to take you, make noise: "This is not my dad."

**Pick a meeting spot every time you arrive somewhere busy:** the flagpole, the information desk, the green tent. Say it out loud and have the child say it back.

## Staying together when you have to move

- **One adult, one job.** The adult assigned to a child does not also carry the documents, load the car and answer the phone. See the Family Roles & Responsibilities guide.
- **Hands on.** Carry babies in a sling or carrier so your arms are free. Hold toddlers' hands or use a stroller. In thick crowds, older children hold on to your bag strap or belt.
- **Buddy pairs** for school-age children, each responsible for knowing where the other is.
- **Count heads** at every door, vehicle and stop. Say the number out loud.
- **Dress to be seen.** A bright hat or jacket is easy to spot, and you will remember it when asked "what is she wearing?"
- **Brief before you move:** "We are walking to the blue bus. You hold my hand. If we get split up, you stop and I come back to you."
- **Never send a child ahead,** or back for something.

**In a fire, the plan is different.** Children should know two ways out, crawl low under smoke, go straight to the outside meeting place even if no adult is with them, and never go back inside for a pet, a toy or a person. Children under about six usually cannot get out alone, so your plan must say who gets each one. Practice twice a year, once at night.

## School and childcare

- **Ask for the reunification plan:** where children would be taken if the building is evacuated, how parents are told, and what you must bring to collect your child (usually photo ID).
- **Keep the pick-up list current.** Add at least one person who lives or works near the school and could get there if you cannot. Give that person the password.
- **Keep your contact details current** with the office, including the out-of-town contact.
- **Do not rush to the school during a lockdown or shelter-in-place.** Roads fill, responders are delayed, and the school cannot release children until it is lifted. Wait for the official message and go where it tells you.
- **Children with phones:** make sure they know to follow the teacher first and text you second.

## Hazards children do not see

Disasters fill a child's world with new dangers at exactly the moment adults are busiest. Supervision is the protection: a named adult watching, not "everyone keeping an eye out."

- **Floodwater and standing water.** It can hide drops, debris and current, and it carries sewage and chemicals. No playing or wading. Wash hands after any contact.
- **Cleanup zones.** Keep children and pets out of damaged or flooded areas until cleanup is finished. That means children do not help with it. See the Post-Event Cleanup Safety guide.
- **Flood-soaked toys.** Wash and disinfect hard toys. Throw away stuffed animals and baby toys that cannot be cleaned.
- **Downed wires, damaged buildings, broken glass, nails.** Shoes on, always.
- **Generators, grills and camp stoves.** Outdoors only, with generators at least 20 feet (6 m) from doors, windows and vents, and children kept well back from hot surfaces and fuel.
- **Candles, matches, lighters, lamp oil, medicines, cleaning products.** In a shelter or a relative's house these are suddenly at child height. Put them up high.
- **Vehicles.** Never leave a child alone in a car, even for a minute, and watch for children behind vehicles in crowded evacuation and shelter lots.
- **Water for babies.** If tap water may be unsafe, keep breastfeeding if you already are. For formula, ready-to-feed is the safest kind in an emergency. See the Emergency Nutrition for Children guide.
- **Strangers and crowding in shelters.** Go with children to the bathroom, keep them in sight, and tell shelter staff about anything that worries you.

## By age

| Age | Their part | Your part |
|-----|-----------|-----------|
| **Babies and toddlers** | None | Always in the arms or reach of a named adult. ID on the child, not just in the bag. Carrier, diapers, feeding supplies, comfort item. |
| **About 3 to 5** | Hold hands. Know their full name and a parent's full name. Stay with their grown-up. | Card in a pocket, number on a wristband, password practice as a game. |
| **About 6 to 10** | Know a phone number, the address, the meeting spots, the password, how to call 911. Carry a small go-bag. | Practice the lost rule in real places. Use buddy pairs. |
| **About 11 to 13** | Help a younger child. Send a short check-in text: "I'm OK. At library." | Agree where they go if they cannot get home. |
| **Teens** | Can look after younger siblings and follow the plan alone. | Share the whole plan, the contacts and the password. Agree check-in times. |

**Children with disabilities or medical needs:** ID on the body at all times, a one-page care summary in the bag (diagnoses, medicines and doses, triggers, how they communicate, what calms them), and a helper who has practiced. A child who runs when frightened needs an adult within arm's reach in any crowd. See the Special Needs Preparedness guide.

## Child safety checklist

- [ ] Each child assigned to a named adult, with a backup
- [ ] Contact card on each child, sealed against water
- [ ] Current face photo on my phone and in my wallet
- [ ] Family password chosen and taught; pick-up adults know it
- [ ] Children can say their full name, a parent's name and one phone number
- [ ] Children old enough know how and when to call 911
- [ ] Lost rule practiced out loud: stop, find someone in charge, say who you are
- [ ] School and childcare pick-up lists and contact details up to date
- [ ] School reunification plan known
- [ ] Copies of birth certificates and custody papers stored outside the home
- [ ] Go-bag for each child: clothes, snacks, medicines, comfort item (see the Go-Bag Essentials by Family Member guide)
- [ ] Home fire drill done, including who carries the youngest

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Play "What's our word?" at dinner. First, each child says their full name, a parent's full name and one phone number (younger ones can sing it). Second, choose or review the family password. Third, act out the pick-up: one adult plays a friendly neighbor who says "Your mom sent me to get you, hop in." The child's line is "What's our word?" and, with no word, "No thank you," and they walk to another adult. Swap roles and let the children test you. Finish by filling in one contact card per child and putting it in tomorrow's backpack.

**What would you do?**

1. *You are in a long evacuation line at a bus pickup with your three children. Your eight-year-old says she left her backpack, with her inhaler, on a bench you can see about fifty yards back. The line is starting to move.*

   **Answer:** Do not send her, and do not go while the others wait alone. Either everyone goes together and you rejoin the line, or you tell a staff member and ask for help fetching it. A child sent back alone through a moving crowd is exactly how separations happen. Afterward, move the inhaler to the adult's bag.

2. *At a crowded shelter, a woman your five-year-old met yesterday offers to take him to the snack table while you fill in forms. He likes her and is already holding her hand.*

   **Answer:** Thank her and keep him with you, or go together when the form is done. She is very likely kind, but your rule is not about her: he goes nowhere with anyone who does not have the family word, and you keep him in sight in a place full of strangers. Holding the line when it feels rude is what teaches him the rule is real.

3. *An earthquake hits at 1 p.m. Phones are jammed. Your son's school is two miles away across a bridge, and you have heard nothing from it. Your neighbor is setting off on foot to get her own daughter and urges you to come.*

   **Answer:** Check the plan before your feet move. Schools generally hold children until a parent or listed adult arrives, so he is most likely supervised; the danger is you being hurt or stuck on the way, or arriving at an empty building because the school moved to its evacuation site. Text the out-of-town contact, try the school's emergency line and message system, and listen to local radio. If the route is known to be safe and the school is releasing students, go with ID. If the bridge is in doubt, the person on your pick-up list who lives on that side is the one to send.
