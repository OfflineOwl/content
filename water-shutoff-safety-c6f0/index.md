# Household Water Shutoff & Safety

> **One valve, two jobs.** Closed in seconds, your main water valve stops a burst pipe from flooding the house. Closed after an earthquake or a water main break, it traps the clean water already in your pipes and water heater, often 30 to 80 gallons, before it can drain away or be contaminated. Find it today, while the floor is dry.

## Do this now

If water is pouring from a pipe, hose, or appliance:

1. **Close the main water valve.** Lever handle: a quarter turn, until the lever sits crosswise to the pipe. Round wheel handle: turn clockwise until it stops.
2. **Keep yourself away from electricity.** If water is near outlets, appliances, or light fixtures, switch off power at the breaker panel, but only if you can reach the panel from a dry spot. Never touch the panel or anything electrical while standing in water.
3. **Open faucets to drain the pipes.** Turn on cold taps and flush toilets so the water still in the lines goes down a drain, not through the break.
4. **Turn off the water heater** so it can't heat while the water is off: the breaker for an electric heater, or the "off", "pilot", or "vacation" setting on a gas heater's control.
5. **Can't find the valve, or it won't turn?** Call your water utility's emergency number and ask for a shutoff at the meter. In an apartment, call the building manager or maintenance line.
6. **Photograph the damage, then start drying.** Mold can begin within 24 to 48 hours. See the Post-Event Cleanup Safety guide.

**After an earthquake or a reported main break,** do step 1 but skip step 3: keep the faucets closed so the clean water stays in your pipes and tank.

## Get emergency help if

- **You smell gas or hear hissing.** Leave at once, don't touch switches, and call 911 or the gas utility from outside.
- **Water has reached the electrical panel, outlets, or cords** and you can't cut the power from a dry place. Stay out and call the electric utility or an electrician.
- **Someone has had an electric shock.** Call 911 (or your local emergency number). Don't touch them until the power is off.
- **A ceiling is sagging or bulging with water.** Keep everyone out from under it.

## Why the main valve matters

A broken supply line runs at full pressure until someone stops it. The valve also protects your drinking water. Ready.gov warns that after a disaster, cracked lines can pollute the supply to your house, and gravity can drain your water heater and toilet tanks back out through the pipes unless you shut the main valve and trap that water inside. It recommends keeping the water off until authorities say it is safe to drink.

Everyone in the household who is old enough should know where the valve is and how it turns.

## The three places water can be shut off

| Shutoff | Where | Use it for |
|---------|-------|------------|
| **Fixture valves** | Under sinks, behind toilets, behind the washer, on the pipe into the water heater | A leak at one fixture. The rest of the house keeps its water |
| **Main house valve** | Where the supply line enters the building | Burst pipes, unknown leaks, earthquakes, main breaks, long absences |
| **Street valve (curb stop)** | In the meter box near the curb or sidewalk | The utility's valve. Leave it to them unless they tell you otherwise |

Ready.gov says plainly that the valve to learn is the one at the house, not the street valve, which is very hard to turn and needs a special tool. Many utilities allow only their own crews into the meter box. Ask yours what it permits before you need to know.

## Finding your main valve

The main valve is usually close to where the water line comes through the wall or floor. Check these places:

- **Outside, on the wall facing the street,** often near the front hose faucet. Common in mild climates.
- **In the basement or crawl space,** on the street side, often near the water heater. Common where the ground freezes.
- **In the garage or a utility closet.**
- **Still can't find it?** Find the water meter and picture a straight line from it to the house. The valve is usually near where that line meets the building. Your home inspection report may also show it, and some utilities will come out and help you locate it.

Special cases:

- **Apartments and condos:** you may have no valve of your own, or only the fixture valves under sinks and behind the toilet. Ask the manager where the unit or building shutoff is and who to call at night.
- **Older and manufactured homes:** some have no house valve at all. Ask a plumber to add one.
- **Private wells:** switching off the well pump's breaker stops the supply. There is usually also a valve on the house side of the pressure tank.
- **Home fire sprinklers:** closing the main may shut them off too. Ask your installer or fire department how yours is fed.

## Know your valve

| Type | Looks like | To close | Notes |
|------|------------|----------|-------|
| **Ball valve** | Straight lever handle | Quarter turn. Lever crosswise to the pipe is closed. Lever in line with the pipe is open | Fast and reliable. The best type for a main |
| **Gate valve** | Round wheel handle | Clockwise ("righty-tighty") until it stops, usually several full turns | Common in older homes. Can seize from disuse or fail to seal |

**Test it once.** Close the valve, then open a faucet at the lowest point in the house. The flow should slow and stop. If it keeps running, the valve is not sealing. If the handle will not move with firm hand pressure, stop. Forcing an old valve can snap the stem and leave you with a leak and no shutoff. Either way, have a plumber replace it. Ready.gov's advice is the same: a valve that is rusted open or won't close all the way should be replaced. A lever-handled ball valve is also much easier for someone with arthritis or a weak grip.

**Tag it.** Tie on a bright label that says MAIN WATER, and write which way is off.

## Turning the water back on

1. Close most faucets, leaving one open on the top floor.
2. Open the main valve **slowly**. Opening it fast slams water and air through the pipes.
3. When the open faucet runs steadily, go around and run each tap until the sputtering stops.
4. **Refill the water heater before restoring its power or gas** (see below).
5. Check the repair and every place you worked for drips.

After a main break or a boil-water notice, follow your utility's flushing instructions before drinking tap water. See the Signs of Contaminated Water guide.

## Your water heater is an emergency water tank

A tank water heater typically holds 30 to 80 gallons of drinking-quality water. A tankless heater holds almost none. To use it:

1. **Turn off the heat.** Electric: switch off its breaker. Gas: turn the control knob to "off" or "pilot". If the gas supply itself gets shut off, FEMA notes a professional will be needed to turn it back on.
2. **Close the cold-water inlet valve** on the pipe going into the top of the tank.
3. **Let the water cool.** It can scald. The Consumer Product Safety Commission warns that 150°F (66°C) water causes third-degree burns in about 2 seconds, and 140°F (60°C) water in about 6 seconds. Wait a few hours, or draw it very carefully. Keep children back.
4. **Open a hot-water faucet** somewhere in the house. This lets air in so the tank can drain.
5. **Open the drain valve** near the bottom of the tank and fill clean containers. A short hose helps, since the valve sits close to the floor.
6. **Let the first water settle.** It often carries harmless mineral sediment. Pour the clear water off the top or strain it through a clean cloth.
7. **Before you turn the heat back on, refill the tank.** Close the drain, open the inlet valve, and leave a hot faucet running until water flows without spitting air. Heating an empty tank can ruin it.

This water is safe to drink as it is if it was in the tank before the supply was contaminated and you closed the main in time. If you are not sure, treat it: a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 ft or 1,500 m). See the Complete Water Purification Guide.

**Other water already in the house:** open the highest faucet to let air in, then collect from the lowest faucet. Expect a few gallons. See the Finding Safe Water Sources guide for toilet tanks, ice, and what never to drink.

## Gas and water are not the same

People who learn one valve tend to shut everything off after a disaster. Don't.

| | Water | Natural gas |
|---|-------|-------------|
| **When to shut off** | Any leak, burst, main break, or doubt | **Only** if you smell gas, hear hissing or blowing, or see a broken gas line |
| **How** | By hand at the house valve | With a 12-inch or larger adjustable wrench, a quarter turn in either direction until the valve is crosswise to the pipe |
| **Where** | Where the water line enters the house | On the pipe that rises from the ground to the gas meter |
| **Turning it back on** | Do it yourself, slowly | **Never yourself.** The gas utility or a qualified professional must inspect, restore service, and relight pilots |
| **Practice** | Close and reopen it once a year | Find the valve and fit the wrench, but do not actually turn it |

If you shut off gas that was not leaking, you may wait days after a big disaster for the utility to restore it, with no heat, hot water, or stove in the meantime. If you do smell gas: get everyone out first, shut the valve from outside only if it is safe, and call from a distance. Keep the wrench with your emergency supplies or near the meter, not hanging on the gas piping.

For electricity, Ready.gov advises switching off the individual breakers first, then the main.

## Frozen pipes

Water expands as it freezes, and that is what splits pipes. The leak often appears only when the ice melts.

**Prevent it:** insulate pipes in attics, crawl spaces, and basements. Disconnect garden hoses and cover outdoor faucets. In a hard freeze, open cabinet doors under sinks and let faucets run at a slight drip. If you go away, leave the heat on low, not off.

**Thaw it:** open the faucet first, then warm the pipe with a hair dryer, heating pad, space heater, or cloths soaked in hot water. **Never use a torch or any open flame.** Keep going until full pressure returns. Know where the main valve is before you start, in case the pipe has already split. If you can't find or reach the frozen section, call a plumber.

## Fixture shutoffs

- **Toilet:** oval or round knob on the wall or floor below the tank. Turn clockwise. For an overflowing bowl, take the lid off the tank and push the rubber flapper down over the hole in the bottom, then close the valve.
- **Sinks:** two valves under the basin, hot on the left and cold on the right.
- **Washing machine:** two valves on the wall behind it. Burst washer hoses are a classic cause of house floods, so close these when you travel.
- **Dishwasher and refrigerator ice maker:** small valves under the kitchen sink or behind the appliance.
- **Water heater:** the valve on the cold pipe entering the tank.

After a strong earthquake, don't flush toilets until you know the sewer line is intact.

## Find, tag, and equip

- [ ] Main water valve found, tested, and tagged
- [ ] Every adult and older child has closed and opened it once
- [ ] Water heater: breaker or gas control, inlet valve, and drain valve identified
- [ ] Fixture valves under sinks and behind toilets turn freely
- [ ] Gas meter valve located, and a wrench that fits stored nearby
- [ ] Breaker panel located and breakers labeled
- [ ] Water utility, gas utility, plumber, and landlord numbers written down
- [ ] By the valve: flashlight, adjustable wrench, bucket, old towels, a short hose

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a timed shutoff. Start a stopwatch in the kitchen, walk to the main valve by flashlight, and close it. Have someone open the kitchen cold tap and call out when the water stops. Reopen the valve slowly and run the tap until the air is out. Then let each person in the household do it. If an old wheel valve resists, stop and book a plumber. Don't force it for the sake of a drill.

**What would you do?**

1. *A strong earthquake has just ended. Your house looks fine and you smell nothing. A neighbor is going door to door with a wrench, offering to shut off everyone's gas "to be safe."*

   **Answer:** Say no thanks. Gas is shut off only when you smell it, hear it, or see a damaged line, because only the utility can safely turn it back on and that can take days after a major quake. Sniff around the meter and appliances, and keep checking after aftershocks. What you should close is the water main, to keep the clean water in your tank and pipes if street lines are broken.

2. *A pipe bursts in the upstairs bathroom. Water is dripping through the kitchen ceiling light. The breaker panel is in the dry garage, and the main water valve is in the basement.*

   **Answer:** Water valve first: it stops the source, and every second adds more water. Then go to the garage and switch off the kitchen and bathroom circuits, or the main. Don't touch the wet light switch or stand under the sagging ceiling. Then open faucets to drain the lines. If the panel had been in a wet room, you would stay out of it and call the utility.

3. *After three days without water, you drained your electric water heater for drinking. The water is back on. You want a hot shower, so you head for the breaker.*

   **Answer:** Not yet. The tank is empty, and an electric element switched on in air burns out in moments. Close the drain valve, open the cold inlet, and let a hot faucet run until it flows smoothly with no sputtering. Only then turn the breaker on. If the outage followed a main break, check for a boil-water notice before you drink from the tap.
