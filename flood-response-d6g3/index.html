Flood Response & Water Safety

Turn Around, Don't Drown. Flooding kills more people than any other thunderstorm hazard, and over half of flood drownings happen when someone drives into floodwater. Six inches of moving water can knock you down, and a foot can carry away a car.

Do this now

If flooding is happening or a Flash Flood Warning is issued for your area:

  1. Get to higher ground immediately. Don't wait to be told if water is rising around you.
  2. Never drive, walk, or swim through floodwater. Turn around and find another way. You can't tell how deep it is or whether the road underneath is gone.
  3. If you're told to evacuate, leave now by the route officials give you.
  4. Trapped in a building? Go to the highest level. Don't climb into a closed attic, where rising water can trap you. Go onto the roof only if you must. Call 911 (or your local emergency number), stay on the line until someone answers, and signal for help.
  5. Trapped in a car in moving water? Stay inside. If water rises inside the car, get onto the roof and call for help.

Know the Alerts (US National Weather Service)

Alert What it means What to do
Flood Watch Conditions are favorable for flooding. It may or may not happen. Be ready to move to higher ground. Charge phones, check supplies, know your route.
Flood Advisory Flooding is expected that may be a nuisance but isn't expected to be severe enough for a warning. Use caution, especially on roads.
Flood Warning / Flash Flood Warning Flooding is happening or about to happen. Flash floods can rise in minutes. Move to higher ground now.
Flash Flood Emergency Exceedingly rare. Extreme rain is causing a severe threat to life and catastrophic damage. Get to higher ground immediately. Life-threatening.

Outside the US, follow your national weather service's alerts. The safety advice is the same.

Why Floodwater Is So Dangerous

Water Moves More Than You Think

Turn Around, Don't Drown

6 inches Of fast-moving water can knock an adult off their feet, and can reach the bottom of most cars, causing loss of control and stalling
12 inches Of rushing water can carry away most cars
2 feet Of rushing water can carry away SUVs and pickup trucks. Some NWS guidance says as little as 18 inches can carry away large vehicles.

Over half of flood-related drownings happen when a vehicle is driven into hazardous floodwater. Water often hides a road that has washed out underneath, and it's especially hard to judge depth at night. Even shallow standing water can drown a small child.

Types of Flooding

Type What to know
Flash flood Fast, violent rises, usually within 6 hours of heavy rain, or suddenly from a dam or levee failure or rain on a wildfire burn scar. The most dangerous kind, with the least warning.
River flood Water rises more slowly as rivers overflow, and can last days or weeks.
Coastal flood / storm surge Ocean water pushed ashore by storms. Can rise fast and be deadly once it arrives.
Urban flood Heavy rain overwhelms drains. Underpasses, basements, and low roads fill fast.

Flash floods can happen far from where the rain falls. A storm upstream or in the mountains can send a wall of water into dry creek beds, canyons, and low spots where it's not raining at all.

Before a Flood

Know Your Risk

  • Find out if your home, work, and school are in a flood zone and how high they sit compared with nearby rivers, creeks, and the coast.
  • Learn your area's evacuation routes and where higher ground is.
  • Sign up for local alerts, and turn on emergency alerts on your phone.
  • Standard homeowners and renters insurance usually doesn't cover flood damage. In the US, a new National Flood Insurance Program policy typically has a 30-day waiting period before it takes effect, so buy it before you need it.

Prepare Your Home and Family

  • Have a go bag ready with water, food, medications, documents, a flashlight, a battery radio, and a phone charger.
  • Know how to turn off electricity, gas, and water.
  • Store important documents in a waterproof container, up high.
  • Move valuables and supplies to higher floors when flooding is possible.
  • Clear gutters and drains. Consider a sump pump with battery backup.
  • Make a plan for pets and for anyone who needs help evacuating.

When a Flood Watch Is Issued

  1. Monitor weather radio, phone alerts, or local news.
  2. Charge phones and power banks.
  3. Move important items to a higher floor.
  4. Fill the car's gas tank, and store drinking water in clean containers. (Water in a bathtub is for washing and flushing only, not drinking.)
  5. Be ready to leave quickly. Don't wait until the last minute.

During a Flood

If You're Told to Evacuate

  • Leave immediately, using the routes officials give you. Shortcuts may be blocked or flooded.
  • Turn off utilities at the main switches or valves if you're told to and have time. Don't touch electrical equipment if you're wet or standing in water.
  • Bring your go bag, medications, and pets.

On the Road

  • Turn Around, Don't Drown. Never drive around barricades or into water covering the road.
  • Don't drive over bridges over fast-moving water. Floodwater can wash out supports.
  • Be extra careful at night, when it's hardest to see flooded roads.
  • If water rises around your car but isn't moving fast, and you can get out safely, leave the car and move to higher ground.
  • If your car is trapped in rapidly moving water, stay inside. If water rises inside the car, climb onto the roof and call or signal for help.

On Foot

  • Don't walk through moving water. Six inches can knock you down.
  • If you must walk in water, go where it isn't moving and use a stick to check the ground in front of you.
  • Stay off bridges over fast-moving water and away from streams, drainage channels, and storm drains.

If You're Trapped in a Building

  1. Go to the highest floor.
  2. Don't go into a closed attic. If you must go up, only use an attic with a way to get onto the roof.
  3. Go onto the roof only if water keeps rising and there's no other option.
  4. Call 911 (or your local emergency number) with your exact location and stay on the line until someone answers. Signal from a window or roof with a bright cloth or flashlight.
  5. Don't try to swim to safety through floodwater.

Camping or Hiking

  • Know the forecast for the whole area upstream, not just where you are.
  • Camp away from creek beds and canyon bottoms.
  • If you hear a roar upstream or see water rising fast or turning muddy, climb to higher ground immediately. Don't try to outrun it along the canyon floor.

After a Flood

Floodwater Is Contaminated

Assume all floodwater contains sewage, animal waste, chemicals, fuel, and debris, and that it may be electrified by downed or submerged lines.

  • Stay out of floodwater when you can. If you must enter it, wear rubber boots and waterproof gloves, and a life jacket if it's deep or moving. Don't swallow it or put your head under.
  • Don't let children play in floodwater or with toys that touched it until they're disinfected.
  • Wash with soap and clean water after any contact, especially before eating or drinking. Use hand sanitizer if you don't have clean water.
  • Keep cuts and wounds out of floodwater. Cover them with waterproof bandages. Wounds exposed to warm coastal floodwater or storm surge can develop serious infections. If a wound is exposed, wash it with soap and clean water, and get medical care for redness, swelling, oozing, or fever.
  • Tetanus: floodwater itself doesn't raise your risk, but a dirty wound does. Make sure your tetanus shots are up to date (adults need a booster every 10 years), and ask a healthcare provider about a booster if you get a wound.
  • Watch for animals. Snakes, rodents, and other displaced animals may be in and around flooded buildings.
  • Floodwater can hide sharp objects, open manholes, and washed-out ground.

Returning Home

  • Go back only when officials say it's safe, and in daylight so you can see hazards.
  • Don't enter a flooded building if the electricity may be on. Have an electrician check wiring and appliances that got wet before you use them.
  • If you smell gas or hear hissing, turn off the main gas valve if you can, open windows, leave, and call the gas company from outside.
  • Look for structural damage (cracks, shifted foundations, sagging ceilings) before you go in.
  • Don't move propane tanks you find in flooded areas; report them. Car batteries in floodwater may still hold a charge.
  • Photograph damage before cleanup, for insurance.

Water and Food Safety

  • Don't drink tap water until officials say it's safe. Use bottled, boiled, or treated water. To boil, bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 ft). Boiling and disinfecting don't remove fuel or chemicals.
  • Private well flooded? Don't drink the water. Turn off power to the pump before clearing debris, and have the well disinfected (ideally by a well or pump contractor) and tested before using it.
  • Throw out food that may have touched floodwater, including anything not in a waterproof container: food with screw caps, snap lids, pull tops, or crimped caps, cardboard boxes, home-canned food, and damaged or bulging cans. Also throw out wooden cutting boards, baby bottle nipples, and pacifiers that touched floodwater.
  • Undamaged commercial all-metal cans and retort pouches can be saved: remove the labels, wash with soap and water, then sanitize (soak 15 minutes in 1 tablespoon of unscented bleach per gallon of clean water, or boil 2 minutes), air-dry, and relabel.
  • Throw out refrigerated perishable food if the power was out more than 4 hours. When in doubt, throw it out.
  • See the Water Purification and Food Safety Without Refrigeration guides for details.

Cleanup Safety

  • Never run a generator, pressure washer, or other gas engine indoors or in a garage, even with the doors open. Carbon monoxide can kill. Keep them outside, at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents.
  • Wear rubber boots, waterproof gloves, and eye protection. Wear at least an N95 respirator around mold.
  • Dry everything you can within 24 to 48 hours to limit mold. Open doors and windows, and use fans and dehumidifiers once power is safe.
  • Throw out porous items that stayed wet more than 48 hours and can't be cleaned and dried, such as mattresses, carpeting, upholstered furniture, and wet drywall and insulation.
  • Clean hard surfaces with soap and water. If you use bleach, use no more than 1 cup per gallon of water. Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners. The fumes are toxic.
  • For mold covering more than about 10 square feet, consider professional help.
  • Children shouldn't help with flood or mold cleanup. People with asthma, other lung conditions, or weakened immune systems should avoid it too.
  • Take breaks, and stay hydrated.
