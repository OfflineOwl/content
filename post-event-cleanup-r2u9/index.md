# Post-Event Cleanup Safety

> **The storm is over, but the injuries are not.** After floods, hurricanes, and tornadoes, people are hurt and killed during cleanup: by carbon monoxide from generators, electric shock, falls, chainsaws, and contaminated water. Then mold moves in, starting within 24 to 48 hours. Slow down at the door, then move fast on drying.

## Do This Now

Before you step inside a damaged or flooded home:

1. WAIT FOR THE ALL-CLEAR. Return only when officials say you may, and in daylight. If the building was badly damaged, stay out until an inspector has checked it.
2. LOOK FROM OUTSIDE FIRST. A roof that sags, walls that lean or bulge, new cracks, or missing supports mean stay out until a qualified professional says it is safe.
3. SMELL GAS OR HEAR HISSING? Leave at once. Don't touch light switches or anything that could spark. Call the gas company or 911 from well away.
4. MAKE SURE THE POWER IS OFF before you go in. Never touch the breaker panel, a switch, or any appliance while standing in water or on a wet floor. If you would have to wade to reach the panel, call an electrician or the utility.
5. PHOTOGRAPH EVERYTHING before you move, clean, or throw away anything. Then call your insurer.
6. GEAR UP: N95 respirator, goggles, heavy gloves, waterproof boots, long sleeves and long pants.
7. AIR IT OUT, THEN START DRYING. If the house was closed up for days, open doors and windows and wait at least 30 minutes before staying inside. Wet things need to be out or dry within 24 to 48 hours.

## Get Emergency Help If

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- You smell gas or hear it escaping. Get out first, then call.
- Anyone has a headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, chest pain, or confusion while a generator, pump, pressure washer, or fuel-burning heater is running. This may be carbon monoxide. Get everyone into fresh air right away.
- Someone has had an electric shock, or is in water that may be electrified. Do not go in after them. Cut the power first.
- Someone is trapped, or part of the building shifts, creaks, or begins to give way.
- Someone working in the heat becomes confused, faints, or has hot skin and a racing pulse.
- Someone has a deep or heavily bleeding wound, chest pain, or trouble breathing.

Get medical care the same day for any puncture wound, a wound with dirt, wood, or metal stuck in it, or a wound that turns red, swollen, or starts to ooze. Ask whether you need a tetanus booster.

## Who Should Stay Away

Cleanup is not a family activity.

- CHILDREN should not take part in disaster cleanup. If they must be at the house, keep them outdoors, away from the work. Crawling and putting things in their mouths exposes them to more mold, lead, and germs than adults.
- PREGNANT WOMEN should not take part in flood cleanup.
- PEOPLE WITH ASTHMA, other breathing problems, or weakened immune systems should stay away from moldy buildings altogether.
- PETS should be kept away until the work is done.

If that leaves no one to do the work, ask your local emergency management office or a disaster relief group for help.

## Protective Gear

The basic kit for debris and flood work (CDC):

- [ ] Hard hat
- [ ] Goggles. For mold and dust, choose goggles WITHOUT open vent holes.
- [ ] N95 respirator, or one with a higher protection level
- [ ] Heavy work gloves. For wet work and mold: rubber, nitrile, or vinyl gloves.
- [ ] Waterproof boots with a steel toe and insole. Sneakers do not stop nails.
- [ ] Long pants and long sleeves
- [ ] Earplugs or ear muffs around saws, pumps, and generators
- [ ] Fire extinguisher, first aid kit, and drinking water

AN N95 filters dust and mold spores when it seals against your face. It does nothing against carbon monoxide, gas, or chemical fumes, and it is not asbestos protection. For long days of tearing out moldy drywall, CDC recommends a half-face or full-face respirator.

WHEN YOU STOP WORK: wash with soap and clean water. Shower and change before going where your family is staying. Wash cleanup clothes in hot water and detergent, separately from other laundry.

## Electricity and Gas

- Treat every downed line as live. Stay far back and call the power company.
- If there is standing water and you can switch off the main breaker from a DRY place, do it. If not, stay out and call an electrician.
- Never turn power on or off, or use an electric tool or appliance, while standing in water.
- Have an electrician check a flooded home before power is restored. Wet appliances must be completely dry, and checked if in doubt, before use.
- Never plug a generator into a wall outlet or wire it to the house without a proper transfer switch. It can start a fire and can electrocute utility workers down the line.
- GAS: if you smell it or suspect a leak, turn off the main gas valve if you can do it safely, open windows, and leave. Call the gas company or fire department from outside, and stay out until you are told it is safe. Only the gas utility or a qualified professional turns gas back on. See the Household Water Shutoff & Safety guide.

## Carbon Monoxide: The Cleanup Killer

Generators, gas-powered pumps, and pressure washers all make carbon monoxide (CO), a gas you cannot see or smell that can kill in minutes.

- Run them OUTDOORS ONLY, at least 20 feet (6 meters) from every door, window, and vent.
- NEVER in a home, basement, crawl space, garage, carport, porch, or camper. An open garage door or open windows are not enough.
- The same goes for charcoal grills and camp stoves.
- Keep a battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarm where people sleep.
- Headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, or confusion: get to fresh air and call 911. People who are asleep can die without ever waking.

See the Generator Safety guide.

## Document the Damage First

Insurers and disaster aid programs pay for what you can prove.

1. Take photos AND video before moving, cleaning, or discarding anything. Shoot each room wide, then close up.
2. Capture the high-water marks on walls, furniture, and appliances, and damage to floors, ceilings, and the foundation.
3. Write down or photograph the make, model, and serial number of appliances and electronics.
4. Keep small samples of ruined materials: a square of carpet, a piece of drywall, a swatch of curtain.
5. Contact your insurer as soon as you can. Talk to them before you sign a repair or cleanup contract.
6. Save every receipt for supplies, equipment rental, repairs, and replacement items.

DON'T WAIT FOR THE ADJUSTER TO START DRYING. Once it is photographed, throw out anything that is a health risk and get wet materials out. Standard US National Flood Insurance policies do not cover mold damage in most cases.

WATCH FOR SCAMS: get more than one written estimate, check licenses and references, and be wary of anyone who demands full payment before starting. Renters: photograph your belongings and talk to your landlord and insurer.

## Getting the Water Out

- Once the power is safe, use a wet-dry vacuum or a pump.
- A FLOODED BASEMENT must be pumped out gradually, not all at once. The soaked ground outside is still pushing on the walls, and EPA warns that removing water too quickly can make walls and floors crack and collapse.

---

## Mold: The 24 to 48 Hour Clock

If your home was flooded and could not be dried within 24 to 48 hours, assume it has mold, including where you can't see it: under carpet, behind walls, inside cushions.

DRY IT OUT:

- Open doors and windows when the weather allows. Open closets and cabinets, and pull out drawers to dry.
- When electricity is safe, run fans and dehumidifiers. Set fans in a window or doorway blowing OUT, so they push spores outside, not through the house.
- If mold is already growing, don't run fans across it. They spread it.
- Don't turn on the heating or air conditioning if it was flooded or may hold mold. Have it checked and cleaned first.
- Drying a structure takes days to weeks. Don't close up walls, paint, or lay new flooring until the materials are dry all the way through. EPA's benchmark is 15 percent or less on a moisture meter.

THROW OUT what soaked up floodwater and can't be cleaned and fully dried within 24 to 48 hours. That usually means:

- Carpet and carpet padding
- Mattresses, pillows, and upholstered furniture
- Wet drywall, insulation, and ceiling tiles
- Stuffed animals and baby toys
- Cardboard, books, and paper you don't need to keep

CLEAN hard, non-porous things (metal, glass, hard plastic, tile, concrete): scrub with water and detergent until no mold is visible, disinfect with the bleach mix below if you wish, and dry right away. Painting or caulking over mold does not stop it.

HOW MUCH IS TOO MUCH TO DO YOURSELF? EPA's rule of thumb: if the moldy area is under about 10 square feet (roughly 3 feet by 3 feet), most people can handle it. Call a professional with mold experience if the area is larger, if there was a lot of water damage, if sewage was involved, or if the heating and cooling system is contaminated.

YOU ARE NOT DONE if you can still see or smell mold.

## Bleach: The Right Mix, and What Never to Mix

Use regular unscented household bleach (5% to 9% sodium hypochlorite). Clean with soap and water first: bleach does not work well through dirt.

```
  JOB                               BLEACH       IN WATER
  Dishes, counters, and other       1 tablespoon 1 gallon
    food surfaces                   (15 mL)      (3.8 L)
  Floors, sinks, tools, hard toys,  1 cup        5 gallons
    and undamaged food cans         (240 mL)     (19 L)
  Mold on hard surfaces             1 cup        1 gallon
                                    (240 mL)     (3.8 L)
```

Let surfaces air dry.

NEVER MIX BLEACH WITH AMMONIA OR ANY OTHER CLEANER. Bleach plus ammonia (found in many glass and floor cleaners) releases toxic gas. Floodwater residue can react with it too. Open windows and doors, wear gloves and eye protection, and never use bleach in a closed room. If your eyes or throat burn, get out into fresh air.

## Floodwater, Wounds, and Washing

Assume floodwater and the mud it leaves carry sewage, fuel, and chemicals.

- Keep it out of your mouth and off your skin. Wash hands with soap and clean water before eating or drinking. Use alcohol-based sanitizer if there is no clean water.
- Cover any open cut with a waterproof bandage before you start. Stay out of floodwater if you have an open wound.
- If a wound gets wet or dirty, wash it well with soap and clean water as soon as possible.
- Don't let children play with toys that touched floodwater until they are disinfected. Throw away those that can't be cleaned.

---

## Food and Water After a Flood

- Throw away any food that touched floodwater, perishable food that was not kept cold during the outage, and anything with an odd smell, color, or texture. Food can make you sick even when it looks and smells normal. When in doubt, throw it out.
- Undamaged, commercially sealed metal cans can be saved: remove the labels, wash the cans, and sanitize them in the 1 cup per 5 gallons bleach mix.
- Don't use tap water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, washing dishes, or baby formula until local officials say it is safe.
- A private well that was flooded must be tested and disinfected before use. Don't switch on a flooded well pump: it is a shock hazard.

See the Food Safety No Fridge, Complete Water Purification, and Signs of Contaminated Water guides.

## Asbestos and Lead in Older Homes

Tearing out walls and floors in an older building can release two poisons you cannot see.

ASBESTOS: EPA says structures built before 1975 may contain significant amounts. It can be in pipe, furnace, and boiler insulation, old vinyl floor tiles, and some wall and roofing materials. You can't tell by looking.

- Leave suspect material alone if it is undamaged.
- Don't saw, sand, scrape, or drill it. Don't dust, sweep, or vacuum debris that may contain it. Don't track it through the house.
- If it is damaged or has to come out, hire a trained and accredited asbestos professional.

LEAD PAINT: many homes built before 1978 have it. EPA estimates 87 percent of homes built before 1940 do, 69 percent of those built 1940 to 1959, and 24 percent of those built 1960 to 1977.

- Dry sanding, scraping, and demolition turn old paint into dust that poisons children most of all.
- Don't dry sweep. Wet-wipe and wet-mop, and use a HEPA vacuum for debris.
- Keep children and pregnant women away from the work area and the dust.
- For anything beyond a small repair, use a lead-safe certified contractor. In the US, the National Lead Information Center is 1-800-424-LEAD (5323).

## Chemicals, Fuel, and Batteries

- Call the fire department about leaking propane tanks, fuel, pool chemicals, or containers you can't identify. Don't move them yourself.
- Never pour cleaners, paint, fuel, or other chemicals down drains, storm sewers, or toilets. Never burn them. Never combine the contents of damaged containers.
- Follow your city or county's rules for sorting debris and disposing of household chemicals. Don't burn debris yourself.
- FLOODED LITHIUM BATTERIES are a fire risk. Move flooded e-bikes, scooters, and battery packs out of the house and garage, and don't charge them. A flooded electric or hybrid car should be towed, not switched on.

## Work Safely and Pace Yourself

- LIFTING: use teams of two or more for anything heavy or bulky, and don't lift more than 50 pounds (23 kg) per person. Wet carpet and furniture are far heavier than dry: cut carpet into strips.
- NEVER WORK ALONE, and tell someone where you are.
- HEAT: do heavy work in the cooler hours. In the heat, drink about 1 cup (8 oz, 240 mL) of water every 15 to 20 minutes, and skip alcohol. Rest in shade. See the Extreme Heat Survival guide.
- CHAINSAWS: follow the manual, wear protective gear, and keep bystanders well back. Check for power lines before cutting. Bent or trapped limbs can whip free when cut. If you have never used one, storm-tangled trees are not the place to learn.
- ROOFS AND LADDERS: falls cause serious cleanup injuries. Leave roof work to professionals when you can.
- TAKE BREAKS. Tired people make the mistakes that cause injuries. Do the most important jobs first and let the rest wait.

## Stress and Grief

Trouble sleeping, short temper, sadness, and feeling overwhelmed are normal after a disaster, and sorting through a ruined home makes them worse.

- Talk with someone about how you feel. Accept help when it is offered.
- Eat, rest, and keep what routine you can.
- Children may go back to earlier behaviors such as bedwetting or clinging. Stay close, let them talk, keep routines, and limit how much disaster news they see.
- In the US, the Disaster Distress Helpline offers free crisis counseling at any hour: call or text 1-800-985-5990.
- If you or someone else is thinking about suicide, call or text 988 in the US. Other countries have their own crisis lines.

See the Mental Health Crisis guide.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Make your "before" record today, while nothing is wrong. Walk through your home with your phone, filming every room slowly and opening closets and cabinets. Say out loud what the big items are. Photograph the serial-number plates on your three most expensive appliances. Email the files to yourself or save them to cloud storage, so they survive if the phone and the house do not. Then count your N95 masks, goggles, and work gloves, and note what is missing.

### What Would You Do?

1. Your basement has two feet of water in it. The neighborhood's power is on, and your breaker panel is on the basement wall. You have rubber boots, and the sump pump would work if you could just reset the breaker.

   **Answer:** Stay out of the water. Rubber boots do not make it safe to stand in a flooded room with live circuits, and you must never operate a panel while standing in water. Call the utility or an electrician to cut power at the meter. Then pump the basement out gradually. The delay costs you some drying time. Wading in could cost your life.

2. It is raining, and you need the generator to run pumps and a dehumidifier. Your brother sets it just inside the garage with the big door wide open, "so it stays dry and the fumes blow out."

   **Answer:** Move it. An open garage door does not clear carbon monoxide, and the gas seeps into the house. The generator goes outdoors, at least 20 feet from any door, window, or vent, on a dry surface under an open-sided canopy, with the exhaust pointing away. Run a heavy outdoor-rated cord inside, and keep a battery CO alarm near where people sleep.

3. The insurance adjuster can't come for a week. The carpet and the bottom of the drywall are soaked. A friend warns you not to touch anything until the adjuster has seen it, or the claim will be denied.

   **Answer:** Document, then act. In a week the house will be full of mold, which flood policies generally don't cover. Photograph and video everything, including the water line. Record serial numbers, cut and keep samples of the carpet and drywall, and tell your insurer what you are doing. Then pull out the wet carpet and padding and start drying today. Wet drywall has to come out too, but in an older home read the asbestos and lead section before you cut into walls.
