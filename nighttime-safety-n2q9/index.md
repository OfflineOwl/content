# Nighttime Safety During Emergencies

> **Emergencies get more dangerous after dark.** You can't see the stairs, the smoke, the water on the road, or the wire across the path, and you are asleep for the hours when fire and carbon monoxide do their worst. A few things set up before bedtime cover most of the risk.

## Start Here

Do these tonight. They take about ten minutes.

1. Put a flashlight or headlamp, sturdy shoes, and your phone within arm's reach of every bed.
2. Press the test button on every smoke alarm and carbon monoxide (CO) alarm.
3. Clear the floor between each bed, the bathroom, and the way out.
4. Decide where everyone meets outside if you have to leave in the dark.
5. Sleep with bedroom doors closed. "Close before you doze."
6. Turn on emergency alerts on every phone, so warnings can wake you.

---

## Get Emergency Help If

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- A smoke alarm sounds or you see or smell fire. Get everyone out first and call from outside. Do not go back in.
- A CO alarm sounds, or people in the home get a headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, or confusion together. Get everyone into fresh air first, then call.
- Someone has fallen and can't get up, hit their head, or is in severe pain.
- Someone is trying to get into your home. Get everyone into one room, lock the door, and call. Do not go out to confront them.
- You see a downed power line. Stay at least 35 feet (10 m) away.

Outside the US, know your local emergency number before you need it.

## Why Night Is Different

- YOU CAN'T SEE HAZARDS. Stairs, clutter, broken glass, floodwater, and downed wires all look like plain darkness.
- YOU ARE ASLEEP. Smoke may not wake you, and carbon monoxide has no smell at all. Alarms are the only things awake.
- HELP IS SLOWER. Fewer people are around to notice, roads are emptier, and in a disaster responders are stretched.
- YOU ARE TIRED. Tired people trip, forget steps, and make worse decisions. Do the difficult jobs in daylight when you can.

None of this calls for anything exotic. It calls for light, working alarms, a clear path, and a plan you made while it was still light.

## Light Your Way

A dark house causes falls. Ready.gov's advice for power outages is a flashlight for every member of the household.

WHERE TO PUT LIGHT:

- By every bed, where a hand can find it without getting up.
- On the path from bedroom to bathroom, in the bathroom, and on any stairs. A dim battery night light or glow stick left on all night in these spots prevents more injuries than a bright light in the living room.
- At the main door.

A headlamp is the most useful kind, because it leaves both hands free for the handrail, a child, or a first aid kit.

MOVING AROUND IN THE DARK:

- Light first, then move. Don't take the stairs until the light is on and in your hand or on your head.
- Sit on the edge of the bed for a moment, then stand up slowly.
- Put on shoes. After an earthquake or storm there may be broken glass on the floor.
- Remove loose rugs and cords from the paths you use at night, and keep those paths clear.
- Use the dimmest setting that does the job. Your eyes adjust to low light, and a bright beam in the face undoes that for you and everyone else.

USE BATTERY LIGHT, NOT FLAME. Never leave a candle burning when you go to sleep, and never use one in a bedroom. The Off-Grid Lighting Options guide covers lights, batteries, and candle rules in detail.

## Fire at Night

A fire that starts while you sleep leaves very little time. The U.S. Fire Administration says you may have less than 2 minutes to get out once the smoke alarm sounds.

BEFORE BED:

- Smoke alarms belong inside and outside every bedroom and sleeping area and on every level of the home. Test them monthly.
- Working alarms matter more than anything else here. The National Fire Protection Association found that nearly three out of five home fire deaths (2018 to 2022) were in homes with no smoke alarms or alarms that did not work.
- Never take the battery out of a smoke alarm to use it elsewhere or to stop it chirping.
- Turn portable heaters off when you leave the room or go to bed, and keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet (1 m) from every heat source.
- Put out candles, lamps, and fires before you sleep.
- Charge phones and power banks on a hard surface, not on a bed or sofa.
- Close bedroom doors. In tests by the UL Fire Safety Research Institute, a closed door could mean about 100 degrees F (38 C) in the bedroom against about 1,000 degrees F (540 C) outside it, and carbon monoxide of about 1,000 parts per million against 10,000.

IF THE SMOKE ALARM SOUNDS:

1. Get up and get out. Don't stop to dress or collect things.
2. Know two ways out of every room. Use the one away from the smoke.
3. Stay low under smoke. Heat and smoke rise.
4. Close doors behind you as you go.
5. Go to your outside meeting place and call 911 from there.
6. Stay out.

See the Fire Safety During Blackouts guide.

## Carbon Monoxide at Night

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas with no color or smell, made by anything that burns fuel. It is the classic night-time killer of power outages, because people run generators and makeshift heaters and then go to sleep. The CDC warns that people who are asleep can die from CO poisoning before they ever have symptoms.

- Install battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas.
- Run a generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet (6 m) from the house, with the exhaust pointing away from windows, doors, and vents. Never in the home or the garage, even with doors and windows open.
- Never heat your home with a gas oven or stovetop.
- Never burn charcoal or use a camp stove indoors.
- If you sleep in a vehicle, don't leave the engine running for long periods, and never run it in an enclosed space. In snow, clear the exhaust pipe before running it at all.

SYMPTOMS: headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, sleepiness, and confusion. They are easy to mistake for flu or tiredness. If several people feel ill at once, or the alarm sounds, get everyone outside into fresh air, then call 911.

See the Generator Safety and Emergency Heating Without Power guides.

## Sleeping Arrangements

IN AN OUTAGE OR AFTER A DISASTER:

- Sleep where you can get out. Keep the route from every bed to an exit short and clear, and avoid rooms whose only way out is blocked or damaged.
- In the cold, bring everyone into one small room, wear layers and a hat to bed, and use sleeping bags or several blankets. Body heat adds up. See the Extreme Cold Survival guide.
- In the heat, sleep on the lowest floor and go to a cooling center if the home stays dangerously hot.
- Keep each person's essentials by their bed (see the checklist).
- Tell everyone where the meeting place is before lights out.
- Pets sleep inside with you, with leashes and carriers by the door.

IF AN EARTHQUAKE WAKES YOU: stay in bed. Lie face down, cover your head and neck with a pillow, and hold on until the shaking stops. You are less likely to be hurt there than walking through falling objects and broken glass in the dark. Then put on shoes and pick up your light before you go anywhere.

REST MATTERS. Long emergencies are won by people who slept. Share the night jobs, such as tending a fireplace or checking on someone who is ill, so that nobody goes without sleep for days.

## Getting Warnings While You Sleep

Many tornadoes, flash floods, and wildfire evacuations come at night. Outdoor sirens are meant for people outdoors and may not wake you.

- Leave Wireless Emergency Alerts switched on, and keep the phone charged, face up, and within reach.
- Keep a NOAA Weather Radio with a tone alert and battery backup by the bed in storm season. It sounds an alarm when a warning is issued for your area.
- When severe weather is forecast overnight, lay out shoes, light, and car keys before bed and decide where you would go.
- People who are deaf or hard of hearing can use smoke alarms and alert devices with strobe lights or bed shakers.

See the Understanding Sirens & Alert Systems guide.

## If You Have to Go Out After Dark

The safest night travel in an emergency is none. If it can wait for daylight, let it wait. If it can't:

ON FOOT:

- Carry a light and switch it on. It shows you the ground and shows drivers where you are.
- Wear something light-colored or reflective.
- Go with another person, and tell someone your route and when you expect to be back.
- Stay at least 35 feet (10 m) from any downed or sagging wire, and out of any water near one. You cannot tell a live wire from a dead one, and you may not see it at all at night.
- Never walk through moving water. Six inches (15 cm) can knock an adult down.

DRIVING:

- Slow down and use low beams in fog, smoke, or dust.
- Floodwater is nearly invisible on a dark road. If water covers the road, turn around.
- Dark traffic signals mean stop and take turns.
- If you are drowsy, stop somewhere safe and rest or change drivers.

See the Emergency Defensive Driving guide.

## Noises and Security at Night

Every sound seems louder and more threatening at night, and most turn out to be wind, animals, or neighbors. Simple habits cover the rest:

- Lock all outside doors and reachable windows before bed, and make sure each still opens from the inside without a key.
- If you have power, leave an outside light on. If not, a solar path light by the door helps.
- If something outside wakes you, look from a window. Do not go out to investigate.
- If someone is trying to get in, gather everyone in one room, lock or block the door, call 911, and stay on the line.

See the Lockdown Procedures guide. This guide does not cover confronting anyone. Laws on that vary by place, and the safe move is a locked door and a phone call.

## People Who Need Extra Thought

CHILDREN: Give each child their own light or glow stick, and let them practice switching it on. Keep bedtime routines as normal as you can. Tell them the plan in one sentence: "If the alarm goes, we go out the front door and meet at the mailbox." Children can be hard to wake, so plan for an adult to go to each child.

OLDER ADULTS AND PEOPLE UNSTEADY ON THEIR FEET: Light the whole path to the bathroom all night, keep a phone at the bedside, and consider sleeping on the ground floor.

PEOPLE WHO USE GLASSES, HEARING AIDS, CANES, OR MEDICAL DEVICES: Keep them in the same spot by the bed every night. Talk to your medical provider about a power outage plan for powered devices.

PEOPLE WHO USE MEDICAL OXYGEN: No candles or any open flame in the home.

## Bedside Checklist

- [ ] Flashlight or headlamp that works
- [ ] Sturdy shoes
- [ ] Phone, charging on a hard surface, alerts on
- [ ] Glasses, hearing aids, cane, or other aids
- [ ] Medications you may need at night
- [ ] Whistle
- [ ] Car and house keys in a known place
- [ ] Clear path to the door and to the bathroom
- [ ] Bedroom door closed

FOR THE HOME:

- [ ] Smoke alarms inside and outside every sleeping area, tested
- [ ] CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas
- [ ] Night lights or glow sticks for stairs, hall, and bathroom
- [ ] Heaters off, candles and fires out
- [ ] Outside doors and windows locked
- [ ] Everyone knows the outside meeting place

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a night walk-through. After dark, with the power on, switch off every light in the house and have everyone lie on their beds. On "go," each person finds their light and shoes without help, and you all walk to the exit and on to the outside meeting place. Nobody uses stairs without a light in hand. Time it. Then press a smoke alarm test button while someone lies in each bedroom with the door closed, to check that they can hear it. Fix what you found: a dead flashlight, a blocked hallway, an alarm nobody could hear.

### What Would You Do?

1. An ice storm has cut the power, the house is getting cold, and it is nearly bedtime. Your neighbor suggests running your generator in the garage with the big door open, so it stays dry and you can run the furnace fan all night.

   **Answer:** No. A generator in a garage can fill the house with carbon monoxide even with the door open, and sleeping people can die before they feel anything. It goes outdoors, at least 20 feet (6 m) from the house, with the exhaust pointing away. Check that your CO alarms work. If you can't run it safely, put everyone in one room in layers and blankets, or go somewhere that has heat.

2. The smoke alarm wakes you at 3 a.m. The hallway is hazy. Your wallet, laptop, and car keys are downstairs in the kitchen.

   **Answer:** Leave them. You may have less than two minutes. Wake everyone, stay low, use the exit away from the smoke, close doors behind you, and meet outside. Call 911 from there. Nobody goes back in for anything, including pets. Tell the firefighters who or what is still inside.

3. Violent shaking wakes you in the dark. Your children are in the next room and you can hear things falling.

   **Answer:** Stay in bed, face down, with a pillow over your head and neck, until the shaking stops. Running through a dark house full of falling objects and broken glass is how people get hurt in earthquakes, and your children are safer staying in their beds too. When it stops, put on shoes, take your light, and then go to them. Teach them the same rule beforehand.
