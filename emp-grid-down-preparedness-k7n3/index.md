# EMP & Grid-Down Preparedness

> **Prepare for the long outage and the rare causes are covered too.** Storms, heat waves, and equipment failures leave millions of people without power for days every year. A solar superstorm or an electromagnetic pulse (EMP) is far less likely, and what you would do at home is nearly the same: water, food, light, safe heat, a radio, and a plan for anyone who depends on electricity.

## Do this now

If the power is out and it looks widespread:

1. **Deal with danger first.** Move anyone on powered medical equipment to backup power and note when it will run out. Stay far away from downed lines.
2. **Find out what is happening.** Turn on a battery or hand-crank radio. Text one out-of-area contact, then put your phone in low power mode.
3. **Keep the refrigerator and freezer shut.** A closed refrigerator stays cold for about 4 hours; a full freezer for about 48 hours (24 if half full).
4. **Switch off and unplug appliances and electronics.** Power can return with damaging surges. Leave one lamp switched on so you know when it is back.
5. **If it may last, store water now.** Fill clean containers while the taps still run, especially if your water comes from a well pump.
6. **Decide early whether to leave.** In dangerous heat or cold, or if medical equipment or medicine is at risk, go to a place with power before nightfall, not after.

**Never** run a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors or in a garage, and never heat your home with a gas range or oven.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **A carbon monoxide alarm sounds, or people feel headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, chest pain, or confusion** while anything fuel-burning is running. Get everyone into fresh air first.
- **Someone in the heat is confused, faints, or has a very high body temperature.** This may be heat stroke.
- **Someone in the cold is confused, very drowsy, or has a temperature below 95°F (35°C).** This may be hypothermia.
- **A medical device's backup power is about to run out** and you cannot reach a place with power.
- **Someone has had an electric shock,** or you see fire or a downed power line.

If phones are not working, send someone to the nearest fire station, police station, or hospital.

## What Is Likely, and What Is Not

| Cause | How often | What to expect | Warning |
|-------|-----------|----------------|---------|
| **Storms, heat, ice, wildfire, equipment failure** | Every year | Hours to days; sometimes longer after major hurricanes and ice storms | Usually days, from weather forecasts |
| **Severe geomagnetic (solar) storm** | The top "G5" level is reached about 4 times in each 11-year solar cycle | Stress on the power grid through long transmission lines and large transformers. At G5, NOAA's scale lists possible grid collapse and transformer damage | Hours to days |
| **High-altitude nuclear EMP** | Never used in an attack. US agencies call it a low-probability, high-consequence event | Unknown in detail (see below) | Little or none |

**The ordinary case is bigger than most people think.** In 2024, the average US electricity customer went about 11 hours without power, nearly double the average of the previous decade, and 80% of those hours came from major events. Hurricane Helene alone cut power to 5.9 million customers across 10 states, and customers in South Carolina averaged nearly 53 hours without power for the year.

**Solar storms are real and watched.** Eruptions on the sun can send a cloud of charged particles toward Earth. When it arrives, it disturbs Earth's magnetic field and drives stray currents through long power lines, which can overheat large transformers and trip protective equipment. In March 1989, a storm blacked out Québec's grid for nine hours. In October 2003, another caused a blackout in Sweden. The 1859 Carrington storm, the strongest known, interfered with telegraph systems widely; nothing that strong has struck the modern grid. In May 2024, the first G5 storm since 2003 arrived after forecasters had warned grid operators in time for them to take protective steps.

NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center (spaceweather.gov) forecasts these storms and rates them from G1 to G5. The fastest eruptions reach Earth in 15 to 18 hours and slower ones take several days, so there is usually time to charge devices and fill the car. A solar storm threatens the grid. It does not reach into a drawer and damage a flashlight, a radio, or a phone.

## EMP: What Is Known and What Is Not

A high-altitude EMP is a burst of electromagnetic energy from a nuclear weapon detonated far above the ground. It is the scenario with the most alarming claims and the least public evidence.

**What official sources say**

- The US cybersecurity and infrastructure agency (CISA) describes extreme EMP events as low-probability and high-consequence. Its assessment is that an attack by a country with basic nuclear and missile capabilities could be disruptive regionally but is unlikely to cause catastrophic damage across the continent, while a country with highly developed capabilities could cause widespread harm, particularly during an escalating international conflict.
- **Vehicles.** The congressional EMP Commission tested 37 cars built from 1986 to 2002. None that were switched off were affected. Of those running, three stalled at the highest field levels and one needed a dashboard repair; most of the rest showed only nuisance glitches such as blinking dash lights. Of 18 trucks, three stalled and one needed a tow. The Commission's worry was crashes when some engines cut out at speed, not a nation of dead cars.
- **Radios.** In the same tests, handheld and vehicle radios showed no damage even at the strongest fields available; some that were switched on needed to be turned off and on again. Desktop computers and network equipment were much more vulnerable.
- **Small devices.** Federal EMP guidelines written for critical infrastructure operators state that small handheld electronics are relatively immune unless they have long antennas or power cords attached, and that battery-operated radios are relatively resilient.

**What is not known**

- How today's cars, phones, solar inverters, and home medical devices would fare. The public vehicle tests are two decades old.
- How much of the grid would fail and how long repairs would take. Estimates range widely and none has been tested.
- What households should do. We found no official EMP checklist for the public; the federal guidance that exists is written for infrastructure operators.

Be wary of anyone who claims certainty in either direction, especially if they are selling something.

**Cheap precautions that match federal guidance**

These come from the lowest-cost tier of those federal guidelines:

- **Keep a battery-operated AM/FM/weather radio.** Broadcast alerts are expected to be the main source of official information.
- **Store spare electronics unplugged,** with power cords, antennas, and cables disconnected, and batteries removed. Long wires are what collect the energy.
- **Use surge protectors** on equipment that stays plugged in. They also earn their keep against lightning and ordinary surges.
- **If you want to shield a spare radio,** the guideline's own method is simple: put it in a plastic or paper bag, then wrap it completely in heavy-duty aluminum foil with overlapping seams, two or more layers. It adds that metal trash cans usually do not give reliable protection unless modified to close the gaps around the lid, and that ordinary Mylar food bags give little. It also calls shielding "of secondary importance" for small handheld devices.
- **Store a week of food, water, and supplies.**

That last item is the one that matters. Everything below applies to any long outage, whatever caused it.

## Getting Ready for a Long Outage

Aim to live for two weeks with no grid power, and with shops, fuel, and tap water uncertain.

**Water.** Store at least 1 gallon (about 4 liters) per person per day. A private well stops when its pump stops. City water can lose pressure or become unsafe to drink; follow any boil-water notice. See the Water Storage & Rotation and Water Purification guides.

**Food.** Keep food that needs no cooking or refrigeration, and a manual can opener. After an outage, throw out perishable food that has been above 40°F (4°C) for 2 hours or more, and never taste food to test it. See the Emergency Food Storage Basics, Food Safety Without Refrigeration, and Cooking Without Power guides.

**Light.** Use flashlights, headlamps, and battery lanterns, not candles, which are a fire risk. See the Battery Management & Rotation guide.

**Heat and cold.** No outdoor stove, grill, or generator may be used indoors, and a gas oven is not a heater. In heat, fans alone may not protect you when it is very hot: spend the worst hours somewhere air-conditioned. In cold, gather in one room, layer up, and go to a warming center if the house keeps cooling. See the Extreme Heat Survival, Emergency Heating, and Extreme Cold guides.

**Information.** A battery or hand-crank radio that receives NOAA Weather Radio, with spare batteries. Your car radio works too. See the Hand-Powered & Solar Devices guide.

**Power.** A charged power bank for each phone. If you own a generator: outdoors only, at least 20 feet (6 m) from the house, and never plugged into a wall outlet. See the Generator Safety and Solar Power for Emergencies guides.

**Medical needs.** Ask your care team now for an outage plan for any powered device or refrigerated medicine: how long the device's battery lasts, what else can power it, and where you would go. Refrigerated medicine that has been without cooling for a day or more should normally be replaced; if a life depends on it, keep using it only until you can get a new supply, and call a doctor or pharmacist. See the Special Needs Preparedness guide.

**Money, fuel, and access.** Keep some cash in small bills, because card readers and ATMs need power. Keep the car's tank at least half full, because fuel pumps do too. Learn to use the manual release on an electric garage door. Keep phone numbers and key documents on paper.

**Sanitation and neighbors.** See the Emergency Sanitation & Waste Disposal guide. Check on older neighbors and anyone living alone. Heat, cold, carbon monoxide, and failed medical equipment are the main dangers of a long outage, and a knock on the door can catch all four.

### If a Space Weather Alert Is Issued

- Charge phones and power banks, fill the car's tank, and check your kit.
- If officials ask, cut electricity use to help the grid avoid rolling blackouts, and unplug appliances if told to.
- Follow Emergency Alert System instructions and keep phone use short.
- Check spaceweather.gov before believing a social media post. "G1" and "G2" storms happen hundreds of times in each solar cycle and pass unnoticed by most people.

### When Power Returns

- Check food temperatures before eating anything perishable. When in doubt, throw it out.
- Replace refrigerated medicines as soon as possible.
- Plug things back in a few at a time.
- Restock what you used, and write down what you wished you had.

## Myths

- **"Every car will stop forever."** In the only public tests, cars that were switched off were unaffected, and most that stalled restarted. Newer cars are untested in public.
- **"A metal trash can makes a Faraday cage."** Federal guidance says it usually does not give reliable protection.
- **"A solar storm will fry my phone."** The hazard is to the power grid and to satellites, not to small devices on a shelf.
- **"EMP needs its own special kit."** It needs the same two weeks of water, food, light, and medicine as an ice storm.

## Two-Week Outage Checklist

- [ ] Water: 1 gallon per person per day
- [ ] No-cook food and a manual can opener
- [ ] Flashlight or headlamp for each person, with spare batteries
- [ ] Battery or hand-crank radio with weather band
- [ ] Charged power bank for each phone
- [ ] Battery-backup carbon monoxide alarms on every level
- [ ] Written outage plan for medical devices and refrigerated medicine
- [ ] Cash in small bills
- [ ] Car fuel kept above half
- [ ] Paper list of phone numbers and a paper map
- [ ] Warm bedding, and a plan for where to go in extreme heat or cold
- [ ] Sanitation supplies

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** After dark, switch off the lights and start a timer. Using only what is in your kit, each person finds a light. Then turn on the radio and tune in a local news station and the weather band. Find the manual release on the garage door opener and read its label (do not pull it unless the door is fully closed). Finish by finding your paper list of phone numbers. Anything that took more than a minute to find needs a better home.

**What would you do?**

1. *It is the second night of an ice-storm outage and the house is down to 50°F (10°C). A neighbor suggests running the gas oven with the door open, or bringing the charcoal grill into the kitchen "just to take the chill off."*

   **Answer:** Neither. Both fill the house with carbon monoxide, which cannot be seen or smelled and kills people in their sleep. Put everyone in one small room with blankets and layers, check that the carbon monoxide alarm works, and go to a warming center or a home with power if the temperature keeps falling. See the Emergency Heating guide.

2. *A post is spreading online: "NOAA confirms massive solar storm tonight. Grid down for months. Wrap your phone in foil NOW."*

   **Answer:** Check the source. NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center publishes the real storm level; strong storms are forecast many times in each solar cycle, and none has taken down the US grid for months. Do the useful things: charge devices, fill the tank, check your water and food. Leave the phone unwrapped, since foil only stops it from receiving alerts. See the Verifying Information During Emergencies guide.

3. *The power has been out for 30 hours in summer. Your father's insulin has been in the dead refrigerator the whole time. The pharmacy is closed and his next dose is due.*

   **Answer:** Do not skip a life-sustaining medicine because it got warm, and do not assume it is fine either. Public health guidance is to keep using a medicine a life depends on only until a new supply is available, and to replace it as soon as possible. Reach a doctor, pharmacist, or hospital today for advice and a replacement. Next time, have a cooler and ice plan agreed with his care team.
