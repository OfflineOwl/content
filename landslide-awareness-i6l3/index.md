# Landslide & Mudflow Awareness

> **If you wait to see it, you are too late.** A debris flow (mudslide) is a slurry of mud, boulders, and trees that typically moves about 10 mph (16 km/h) and can exceed 35 mph (56 km/h). You cannot outrun one. The people who survive are the ones who left before the storm, or who moved the moment something sounded wrong.

## Do this now

If you hear a rumble that keeps growing, trees cracking, or boulders knocking together, or you see mud or water surging toward you:

1. **Get out of its path immediately.** Don't stop for belongings. Move to the side, away from the channel or slope, and up onto the nearest high ground. Never run down the valley ahead of it.
2. **Indoors with no time to leave? Go up.** Move to the top floor, on the side of the building facing away from the slope. In a single-story home, climb onto a sturdy counter or table and hold on. Don't run outside into a slide that is already hitting the building.
3. **Outdoors with nowhere to go? Climb.** Get as high as you can before it arrives: uphill, onto a roof, or up a sturdy tree.
4. **Never drive or walk across flowing mud or water,** and never start across a bridge when a flow is coming.
5. **Once you are safe, call 911** (or your local emergency number) and warn neighbors below and beside you.
6. **Stay away.** Slides often come in pulses. The first surge may not be the last.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 or your local emergency number when:

- Anyone is trapped, buried, injured, or missing
- A slide has hit a building or road, or you see one moving
- You see the warning signs below on a slope above homes or a road, and it is getting worse
- You smell gas, or see downed or sagging power lines
- You are cut off by a slide or flood and cannot reach safety

If the danger is not immediate (slow cracks, a tilting wall), report it to your local fire, police, or public works department and have a geologist or geotechnical engineer look at it.

## Fast Slides and Slow Slides

| | Fast: debris flow (mudslide, mudflow) | Slow: deep-seated slide, earthflow |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | About 10 mph (16 km/h), sometimes over 35 mph (56 km/h) | A little at a time, though a few speed up suddenly |
| Usual trigger | Intense rain, rapid snowmelt, rain on burned ground | Weeks to years of water soaking in: wet seasons, broken pipes, bad drainage |
| Warning | Seconds to minutes, sometimes none | Days to months of cracks and tilting |
| Main danger | Death and destroyed buildings | Wrecked homes, roads, and utility lines |
| Your move | Be gone before the storm | Notice the signs, report them, get an expert |

Debris flows start on steep slopes but keep going. They funnel into channels, merge, pick up boulders and trees, and spread out when they reach flatter ground at the canyon mouth. That is why homes on apparently level ground get buried.

Deep, slow slides run on a delay. Water soaks down for weeks, and a slope can fail days, weeks, or months after the rain has ended.

## Know Your Risk

You are in a hazard area if you live or travel:

- **On or below a steep slope**
- **In a canyon bottom, beside a stream channel, or at the mouth of a canyon.** Flows spread out and drop their load where a canyon opens onto flatter ground.
- **Below a hollow or swale** in a hillside, where runoff converges
- **Where it has slid before.** A past landslide is the strongest predictor of another one. Huge boulders scattered across a landscape can be the calling card of old debris flows.
- **Near road cuts, fills, and excavated slopes,** which fail in milder storms than natural hillsides
- **Where water is concentrated:** below culverts, along roads, under roof and driveway drains
- **Below or downstream of a wildfire burn area**

Landslides happen in every US state, and wherever there are hills. No mapped landslide does not mean no hazard. Ask your city or county planning office, state geological survey, or emergency management office what is known about your slope.

Rain thresholds vary by region. As one example, the USGS found that southern California hillsides become prone to debris flows after about 10 inches (250 mm) of rain in a season. After that, more than 2 inches (50 mm) in 6 hours in the lowlands, or 4 inches (100 mm) in 6 hours in the mountains, can set them off.

## After a Wildfire: the Rules Change

A burned slope sheds rain almost like pavement. Burned soil repels water and there are no plants to slow it, so runoff races downhill and takes ash, soil, rocks, and burned trees with it.

- **It takes very little rain.** Debris flows from burn areas can start after only a few minutes of heavy rain, at about half an inch (13 mm) per hour or more. In southern California, as little as 0.3 inches (7 mm) in 30 minutes has done it. Intensity matters more than the storm's total.
- **It happens fast.** Flows can start within minutes of the downpour, about as fast as radar can detect the rain. A warning may not arrive first.
- **It reaches far.** Flows can travel many miles and hit places that did not burn and did not get rain.
- **It lasts.** The danger is greatest in the first two years. Debris flows are unusual after the second rainy season, but flooding can stay worse for several years.

A simple test from the National Weather Service: **if you can look uphill and see a burned area, you are at risk.**

What to do differently below a burn scar:

- **Leave before the storm arrives,** not when it starts. Watch the forecast for the burn area, not just for your street.
- Sign up for local alerts and keep a weather radio on. In the US, the National Weather Service issues flash flood warnings for burn areas, but flooding can begin before a warning is out. Do not wait for one if heavy rain starts.
- Know a way out that does not follow the canyon bottom or cross the stream.

See the Wildfire Preparedness and Flood Response guides.

## Warning Signs

### It is coming now (seconds to minutes)

- **Sound:** a faint rumble that grows louder, cracking or snapping wood, boulders knocking, the ground groaning. Survivors describe a roar like a freight train or a low-flying jet.
- **Feel:** the ground shaking or vibrating
- **A creek that changes suddenly:** it rises fast, turns from clear to muddy, or **drops or stops while it is still raining.** A falling creek can mean debris has dammed it upstream, and the dam can break.
- **A trickle of mud or small rocks** coming down a slope or channel. Small flows often come before large ones.

Any of these means move now.

### It is developing (days to months)

- New cracks or bulges in the ground, pavement, or sidewalks
- Doors and windows that stick or jam for the first time
- New cracks in plaster, tile, brick, or the foundation
- Stairs, walkways, or outside walls pulling away from the house
- Fences, retaining walls, utility poles, or trees tilting, and utility lines sagging or going tight
- Water seeping, flowing, or ponding where it never did before
- Broken underground water, sewer, or septic lines
- Ground bulging at the bottom of a slope
- Small slides, or fresh rock and soil tumbling down

Report these and get the slope assessed. If they are getting worse quickly, leave.

## Before a Storm

- **Decide early and leave early.** If you live in a hazard area and a major storm is forecast, staying somewhere else that night is the safest plan. Travel during the storm is dangerous in its own right.
- **Stay awake.** Many landslide deaths happen while people sleep. If you stay, keep someone up, listening to the rain and a radio.
- **If you can't leave, be upstairs,** on the side away from the slope, with shoes on and a flashlight in reach.
- **Watch for short, intense bursts of rain,** especially after long wet spells. That combination is the most dangerous.
- **Move people who need more time first:** children, older adults, anyone with limited mobility, and pets and livestock. Don't wait for an order.
- Leave if you are told to evacuate, or if you feel unsafe. In the US, text SHELTER and your ZIP code to 43362 to find an open shelter.

### Getting your household ready

- [ ] Find out your slope's history from local officials or your state geological survey
- [ ] Sign up for your community's emergency alerts
- [ ] Pick two ways out that avoid canyon bottoms and stream crossings
- [ ] Choose a meeting place on high ground outside the hazard area
- [ ] Keep a go-bag by the door, with a battery radio and flashlights
- [ ] Ask your insurance agent what is covered. In the US, debris flow damage may be covered by National Flood Insurance Program flood policies

### Looking after the slope

- [ ] See where your roof, gutter, and driveway water goes. Water dumped onto a slope soaks it. Pipe it to a storm drain or road ditch, after checking local rules.
- [ ] Keep or plant native vegetation on slopes. Roots help hold the ground
- [ ] Don't dump yard waste or debris on a slope, and don't cut into steep slopes
- [ ] Before building or regrading, hire a geologist or geotechnical engineer with landslide experience

Sandbags, walls, and concrete barriers can steer shallow mud and water away from a house. They cannot stop a debris flow, and turning a flow onto a neighbor's property can make you liable for the damage. Get professional advice, and do not let a barrier persuade you to stay.

## On the Road

- **Never drive across a road with mud or water flowing over it.** A foot (30 cm) of rushing water can carry away most cars, and a small muddy stream may be the front edge of something bigger.
- **Never start across a bridge if a flow is approaching.** It can grow faster than you can cross.
- Watch for fallen rocks, mud, and cracked or collapsed pavement. Road embankments and cut slopes fail often in storms.
- If the road ahead is blocked or flowing, turn around and get out of the canyon bottom.
- On foot, stay out of river valleys, gullies, and low ground until the storm is well past.

## After a Slide

- **Stay away from the slide.** More can come down, for days or weeks if rain continues.
- **Expect flooding.** The same storms cause both, and debris can dam and divert streams.
- **Check for injured or trapped people from the edge, without going onto the slide.** Guide rescuers to them. Give first aid if you are trained.
- **Help neighbors** who may need it: infants, older adults, and people with disabilities.
- **Report broken utility lines and damaged roads.** If you smell gas, leave and call from a distance. Power lines can be buried in the debris.
- **Don't dig out a blocked road yourself.** The deposit can hide downed power lines, and the slope above may not be finished.
- **Have professionals check** the foundation, chimney, and surrounding ground before you go back in.
- Photograph damage for insurance before cleanup.
- Replant bare ground as soon as you can. Bare slopes erode and slide again.
- Disasters are hard on people. In the US, the Disaster Distress Helpline is 1-800-985-5990 (call or text). Other countries have their own support lines.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Go outside and look uphill. Name what is above you: a steep slope, a canyon mouth, a gully, a road cut, a burn scar. Then follow the water: where does rain from your roof and driveway go, and where would water from that slope go? Finish by tracing your way out on a map and checking that it does not run along a canyon bottom or cross the stream. If it does, find a second route. Tell everyone in the household where "high ground" is.

**What would you do?**

1. *You live below a hillside that burned last summer. It is 11 p.m., the rain has just turned from steady to pounding, and no warning has been issued. The children are asleep. Waiting for an official alert seems reasonable.*

   **Answer:** Don't wait. Below a burn scar, flows can start within minutes of intense rain, sometimes before a warning can be issued. If the road out is still clear and does not cross the channel, wake everyone and go now. If mud or water is already running across the road, do not drive into it: stay, get everyone to the top floor on the side away from the hill, and stay awake. Next time, leave before the storm arrives.

2. *The creek behind your house has roared all evening. Around midnight, with rain still falling hard, it goes nearly quiet and the water drops.*

   **Answer:** That is a warning, not a relief. A creek that drops during heavy rain may be blocked by debris upstream, and the blockage can let go as a surge. Get everyone away from the channel and onto high ground immediately, warn the neighbors along the creek, and call 911 once you are safe.

3. *You are driving home up a canyon road in a storm. Ahead, a shallow stream of brown water and fist-sized rocks is running across the pavement. Home is a mile past it, and cars are lining up behind you.*

   **Answer:** Do not cross. Moving water is stronger than it looks, mud hides washed-out pavement, and a small flow can be followed by a much larger one. Turn around, drive out of the canyon bottom to higher, open ground, and wait it out or take another route. Being late is the correct outcome.
