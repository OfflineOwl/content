# Preserving Food Without Electricity

> **Every old method takes away one thing germs need: water, warmth, or a neutral pH.** Drying, fermenting, and cool storage can keep a harvest for months with no power at all. The same methods done by guesswork, especially with meat and low-acid vegetables, can make people seriously ill.

## Start here

1. **In an outage, eat first and preserve second.** Use refrigerated food first, then the freezer, then shelf-stable food. Preserving does not rescue food that has already sat warm: perishables held above 40°F (4°C) for more than 2 hours get thrown out.
2. **Match the method to the food.** Fruit: dry it. Cabbage: ferment it. Potatoes, roots, onions, squash, apples: store them cool. Meat and fish: cook and eat them.
3. **Use a tested recipe** for anything that depends on salt, vinegar, or heat. In those recipes the salt and acid are the safety system, not the seasoning.
4. **Label everything** with the date and method, check stored food weekly, and never taste food to see if it is safe.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) or go to an emergency room if anyone who ate home-preserved food has:

- Double or blurred vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, or trouble swallowing
- Muscle weakness spreading down the body
- Trouble breathing

These are signs of botulism. They usually start 12 to 36 hours after the meal, but can take from 4 hours to 8 days, and treatment works best early. For vomiting, diarrhea, and dehydration, see the Foodborne Illness guide.

## Which Method for Which Food

| Method | How it protects | Good for | What it needs | Real limit |
|--------|-----------------|----------|---------------|------------|
| Sun and air drying | Removes water | Fruit, herbs, beans left on the vine | Sun: 85°F (29°C) or hotter, humidity under 60 percent | Not safe for meat or most vegetables |
| Heat drying | Removes water | Vegetables, jerky | An oven or dehydrator holding 140°F (60°C) | Needs fuel and a thermometer |
| Fermenting | Salt lets acid-making bacteria win | Cabbage, cucumbers | Salt, a food-grade crock, a 60 to 75°F room | The salt cannot be reduced |
| Vinegar pickling | Added acid | Cucumbers and other vegetables | 5 percent vinegar, a tested recipe | Shelf-stable only if canned |
| Cool storage | Cold slows decay | Potatoes, roots, cabbage, onions, winter squash | A space at 32 to 40°F, or 50 to 55°F for squash | Only sound, whole produce |
| Curing and smoking | Salt, nitrite, drying | Meat, fish | Weeks at 36 to 40°F and a weighed cure | Needs refrigerator-level cold |
| Canning | Heat in a sealed jar | Most foods | Canner, jars, steady heat | See the Food Canning & Preservation guide |

## Drying

Bacteria, yeasts, and molds need water. Drying works when it removes that water faster than they can grow.

### Fruit

Fruit is the one food that is safe to dry in the open sun, because its sugar and acid protect it while it dries (NCHFP).

1. **Check the weather.** You need several hot, dry, breezy days: at least 85°F (29°C), with humidity under 60 percent.
2. **Slice evenly.** To slow browning, soak cut fruit for 10 minutes in equal parts lemon juice and cold water.
3. **Use safe racks.** Stainless steel, plastic, or Teflon-coated fiberglass screens, raised on blocks so air moves underneath. Do not use galvanized "hardware cloth" (it can leave zinc and cadmium on food), copper, or aluminum.
4. **Cover and protect.** Lay a second screen or cheesecloth over the fruit to keep off insects and birds. Bring the racks under cover at night, when cool air puts moisture back.
5. **Test.** Cool a few pieces. Dried fruit is leathery and pliable. A squeezed handful leaves no moisture on your palm and springs apart when you let go.
6. **Condition.** Pack the cooled fruit loosely in jars and shake them daily for 7 to 10 days. If moisture beads on the glass, dry the fruit some more.
7. **Kill insect eggs** in anything dried outdoors: freeze it at 0°F (-18°C) for 48 hours or, with no freezer, heat it in a single layer in a 160°F (71°C) oven for 30 minutes.

### Vegetables, Beans, and Herbs

Vegetables are low in sugar and acid, so outdoor drying is too slow to be safe. Dry them with controlled heat, at 140°F (60°C) in an oven or dehydrator, until brittle. If you cannot hold that temperature, use cool storage or fermenting instead.

**Beans are the exception.** Leave navy, kidney, lima, and similar beans on the vine until the pods are dry and the beans rattle. Shell them, finish drying any that still feel moist, then treat them for insects as in step 7 above.

**Herbs** dry best indoors, out of the sun, which bleaches their flavor and color. Hang sturdy herbs (rosemary, sage, thyme) in small bundles. Hang tender ones (basil, mint, oregano) in small bunches inside a paper bag with holes punched in it. They are done when the leaves crumble.

### Jerky

Hanging raw meat in the sun is not a safe way to make jerky. Warm, slowly drying meat is ideal for bacteria, and drying alone does not kill them. Safe jerky (NCHFP):

- Slice lean meat no thicker than 1/4 inch (6 mm) and trim off all fat.
- **Heat it to 160°F (71°C).** Either boil the strips in their marinade for 5 minutes before drying, or heat the finished jerky for 10 minutes in a 275°F (135°C) oven.
- Dry at 140°F (60°C) until a cooled strip cracks but does not break when bent.
- **Wild game and pork** can carry *Trichinella*. Freeze pieces 6 inches (15 cm) thick or less at 0°F (-18°C) for at least 30 days first. Freezing does not kill bacteria. Raw poultry is not recommended for jerky.
- Homemade jerky keeps about 2 weeks in a sealed container at room temperature.

Jerky therefore takes fuel, an oven, and a thermometer. It is a way to use a working gas oven, not a way around having none.

### Storing Dried Food

Cool it completely, then pack it tightly in clean, dry, insect-proof containers and keep it cool, dry, and dark. Dried fruit keeps about a year at 60°F (16°C) but only about 6 months at 80°F (27°C). Vegetables keep about half as long. Check often: food that has picked up moisture should be used or re-dried right away, and anything moldy thrown out.

## Fermenting

Salt holds back spoilage organisms long enough for acid-making bacteria, already on the cabbage, to take over. Their acid is what preserves the food. Too little salt and the wrong organisms win, so **never cut the salt** in a fermenting recipe.

**Sauerkraut (NCHFP method):**

1. Shred firm cabbage about as thick as a quarter. Work with 5 lb (2.3 kg) at a time.
2. Mix each 5 lb with **3 tablespoons of canning or pickling salt**. Pack it firmly into the container until juice rises.
3. Repeat. If the juice does not cover the cabbage, add cooled, boiled brine: 1 1/2 tablespoons of salt per quart (about 1 liter) of water.
4. Keep the cabbage 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 cm) under the brine with a plate weighted by sealed jars of water. Cover with a clean towel.
5. Skim any scum from the surface 2 or 3 times a week.

| Room temperature | Time to ferment |
|------------------|-----------------|
| 70 to 75°F (21 to 24°C) | 3 to 4 weeks |
| 60 to 65°F (16 to 18°C) | 5 to 6 weeks |
| Under 60°F (16°C) | May not ferment |
| Over 75°F (24°C) | May turn soft |

**Containers:** allow 1 gallon (about 4 liters) of container for each 5 lb of vegetables. Use a stone crock, glass, or food-grade plastic. Never line a container with a trash bag, and keep brass, copper, and zinc utensils away from the brine.

**Keeping it:** finished kraut keeps for several months tightly covered in a refrigerator, or it can be canned in a boiling-water canner. There is no tested keeping time for kraut left in a warm room, so with no cold storage, make small batches and eat them soon after they finish.

**Throw it out** if it is soft or slimy, moldy through the batch, or smells rotten instead of sour.

Fermented fish and meat are a different matter. They are low-acid, and they are a known cause of botulism. Do not improvise them.

## Vinegar Pickling

Pickling adds the acid directly. NCHFP's rule: the level of acidity is as important to a pickle's safety as to its taste.

- Use vinegar labeled **5 percent acidity**. Do not use homemade vinegar or any of unknown strength.
- Do not change the proportions of vinegar, water, and vegetables in a tested recipe.
- **Quick or "refrigerator" pickles must stay refrigerated.** A jar of vegetables in brine on a shelf is not preserved. To keep pickles at room temperature, use a tested recipe and process the jars in a boiling-water canner (see the Food Canning & Preservation guide).
- **Garlic or herbs in oil** are a botulism risk, because oil seals out air around a low-acid food. CDC says to refrigerate homemade flavored oils and throw them out after 4 days. With no refrigeration, do not make them.

## Cool Storage and Root Cellars

This is the one method that costs no fuel, salt, or jars. Many crops stay alive for months if they are kept cold, dark, and at the right humidity.

| Crop | Temperature | Humidity | Keeps about | Notes |
|------|-------------|----------|-------------|-------|
| Potatoes | 38 to 40°F (3 to 4°C) | High (85 to 95 percent) | 5 to 8 months | Cure 2 weeks at 50 to 60°F first. Store unwashed, in the dark. They sprout above 40°F |
| Carrots | 32 to 35°F (0 to 2°C) | Very high (90 percent or more) | 4 to 6 months | Trim tops to 1/4 inch. Pack in damp sand or sawdust |
| Beets | 32 to 35°F (0 to 2°C) | Very high | 1 to 3 months | Leave 1 inch of stem |
| Cabbage | About 38°F (3°C) | Very high | Up to 4 months | Late varieties. Wrap heads in newspaper |
| Onions | 32 to 40°F (0 to 4°C) | Dry (55 to 70 percent) | 5 to 8 months | Cure 2 to 3 weeks until the necks are dry. Hang in mesh bags |
| Winter squash | 50 to 55°F (10 to 13°C) | Dry | Acorn 5 to 8 weeks, butternut 2 to 3 months, Hubbard 5 to 6 months | Leave the stem on. Molds quickly where it is damp |

**Finding the space.** You need somewhere cold, dark, a little damp, and ventilated. An area about 4 by 6 feet (1.2 by 1.8 m) serves most families: a walled-off corner of a basement or garage with a window or vent works well. A dirt floor or pans of water keep humidity up. Screen the vents against rodents and hang a thermometer.

**Two zones.** Roots, potatoes, and cabbage want cold and damp. Onions and squash want dry air, and mold quickly in a damp cellar. Give them separate spaces.

**Keep apples away from potatoes and squash.** Ripening apples and pears give off ethylene gas, which makes potatoes sprout and shortens the life of squash.

**Store only sound produce,** and look it over every week. Use anything bruised or cut first, and remove anything starting to rot before it spreads.

## Salt Curing and Smoking

These are the methods people most want to improvise in an emergency, and the ones least suited to it.

**Curing needs the cold you do not have.** A traditional dry-cured ham is started in early December, held at 36 to 40°F (2 to 4°C) for about 1 1/2 days per pound, rested two more weeks, and still has to be cooked to 145°F (63°C) before eating (Virginia Cooperative Extension). The cure includes nitrate or nitrite at 2 ounces per 100 lb of meat, an amount that has to be weighed, not guessed. If you can hold meat at 36 to 40°F for a month, you have refrigeration. If you cannot, you cannot cure.

**Hot smoking is cooking, not preserving.** Keep the smoker at 250 to 300°F (121 to 149°C) and use a food thermometer: poultry to 165°F (74°C), ground meat to 160°F (71°C), whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, and fish to 145°F (63°C). Smoked food is as perishable as any other cooked food. Without refrigeration, eat it within 2 hours, or 1 hour if the air is above 90°F (32°C).

**Cold smoking** (under 90°F, 32°C) holds meat inside the 40 to 140°F (4 to 60°C) danger zone where bacteria multiply. It is only a finishing step for meat that has already been properly cured.

**Wood:** use hardwoods such as hickory, apple, or oak. Pine, cedar, spruce, and other needle-leaf woods give a bitter, resinous smoke. Never burn painted or treated lumber.

## Common Mistakes

- **Preserving food that is already unsafe.** Drying or smoking meat from a freezer that thawed yesterday preserves the bacteria too.
- **Sealing moist food away from air** in a jar, a vacuum bag, or under oil and calling it preserved. Low oxygen is what botulism bacteria need.
- **Trusting tradition without the conditions.** Old methods relied on cold winters, heavy salt, and years of practice. A first attempt in a warm house has none of those.
- **Storing everything together.** Apples next to potatoes, or onions in the damp root bin, shortens the life of both.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Find your coldest storage spot. Put a thermometer in each candidate place (a basement corner, a crawl space, the floor of an unheated garage), wait 10 minutes, and write down each reading with today's date. A spot at 32 to 40°F suits roots and potatoes, and one at 50 to 55°F suits squash. Check again in midwinter and midsummer so you know which months each spot works.

**What would you do?**

1. *It is day two of a summer outage. Ten pounds of beef in the freezer is thawing. A relative suggests slicing it thin and hanging it on the clothesline to make jerky.*

   **Answer:** Do not hang it. Sun-drying meat is unsafe: nothing brings it to 160°F, and warm, slowly drying meat grows bacteria. First check the meat. If it still has ice crystals or is at 40°F or colder, cook it on a grill outdoors and feed everyone, including the neighbors. If it has been above 40°F for more than 2 hours, throw it out. Real jerky is possible only with an oven that works: boil the strips for 5 minutes in marinade, then dry them at 140°F.

2. *Two weeks into your first crock of sauerkraut, a white film has formed on top of the brine. The cabbage underneath is crisp and smells cleanly sour.*

   **Answer:** Skim the film off, check that the cabbage is still 1 to 2 inches under the brine, and carry on. Surface scum comes from wild yeasts feeding at the surface. The mistakes go both ways: throwing out a good batch because of a film, or keeping a batch that has turned soft, slimy, or rotten-smelling. That one goes in the trash.

3. *You have bought 50 pounds of potatoes and a bushel of apples for the winter, and plan to stack them together in the cool basement corner.*

   **Answer:** Separate them. Apples give off ethylene, which makes potatoes sprout. Cure the potatoes for two weeks at 50 to 60°F, then keep them dark, unwashed, and at 38 to 40°F. Put the apples in another cold space, or as far away as the room allows, with ventilation between. Sort both first and eat the bruised ones now.
