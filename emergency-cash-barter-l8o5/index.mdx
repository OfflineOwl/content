# Emergency Cash & Barter Basics

> **Small bills beat a big balance.** When the power or the network goes down, card readers, ATMs and gas pumps go down with it. A modest stash of small bills buys fuel, food and a room while everyone else waits for the system to come back.

## Start here

1. **Set some cash aside this week,** in small bills. Even one tank of fuel's worth changes your options.
2. **Work out your own number** with the table below. There is no official dollar figure, and anyone who gives you one is guessing about your household.
3. **Store it properly:** in a waterproof bag, inside a fire-resistant box or safe, with your important documents. Put part of it in your go-bag.
4. **Tell one trusted adult** where it is.
5. **When a storm is forecast, act before the lines form:** top up the cash, fill the fuel tank, charge the phones.
6. **Write your bank and card phone numbers on paper.** A lost card is a phone call, if you have the number.

## Why cash still matters

Ready.gov puts it simply: ATMs and credit cards may not work during a disaster when you need to buy supplies, fuel or food. The FDIC, which insures US bank deposits, says that for a period of time you may only be able to pay for everyday purchases with cash.

A card payment needs three things at once: power at the shop, a working data connection, and the bank's systems on the other end. Lose any one and the reader is a paperweight. ATMs have the same weakness. Gas stations may be closed, or unable to pump at all, in a power outage. Banks in a damaged area may close lobbies or run drive-through only.

**Your money in the bank is still safe.** A closed branch is not a failed bank. Deposits at FDIC-insured banks are insured to at least $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. The problem in an outage is reaching your money, not losing it.

## How much to keep

Official guidance deliberately avoids a dollar amount:

- **Ready.gov:** keep a small amount of cash at home in a safe place.
- **FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit:** base the amount on your family's basic needs, such as food, fuel and the other things you use day to day.
- **FDIC:** do not keep more cash at home than you need for immediate needs after a disaster. Larger sums belong in an insured account.

So the amount is personal. Add up what a few days on cash would really cost your household:

| What you would need to pay for | Your figure |
|--------------------------------|-------------|
| One full tank of fuel (or transit and taxi fares) | |
| Groceries and drinking water for several days | |
| One or two nights of lodging, if you had to leave | |
| Prescription refills, copays, pharmacy items | |
| Baby and pet supplies | |
| A margin for the thing you did not think of | |
| **Total** | |

If the total is more than you can spare, build toward it a little at a time. Part of the amount is far better than none. Ready.gov also suggests an emergency savings account for the larger costs that follow a disaster, and that money belongs in the bank.

## Which bills

**Small ones.** Ready.gov stresses small bills for a reason: a shop running on a cash box and a notepad may have no change. If all you have is a large bill, a small purchase means overpaying or going without.

Keep mostly ones, fives, tens and twenties. Ask your bank for small bills on an ordinary day, not the day before the storm.

## Where to keep it

- **With your documents.** The FDIC suggests a waterproof bag kept in a fire-resistant box or safe. FEMA suggests keeping the cash in the same safe place as your document kit, so there is one thing to grab.
- **Some in the go-bag,** so leaving in a hurry does not mean leaving broke.
- **Split, not scattered.** Two known places are better than one, and better than six you have forgotten.
- **Quietly.** The fewer people who know there is cash in the house, the safer it is.
- **Check it twice a year** when you review your kit. Emergency cash has a way of being borrowed for pizza.

Cash at home that burns, floods or is stolen is not insured the way a bank deposit is. That is the reason to keep the amount modest.

**Burned or soaked bills are not always lost.** The US Bureau of Engraving and Printing examines damaged ("mutilated") currency as a free public service and pays full value when clearly more than half of a note is identifiable. Keep every fragment.

## Cards, ATMs and accounts during an outage

- **Carry a card anyway.** It may work a few towns away, outside the damaged area, and many hotels ask for one.
- **Withdraw early.** Once the power is out, ATMs may be dark or empty. Know your daily withdrawal limit.
- **Set up online and mobile banking now.** After a disaster it may be the only branch that is open.
- **Move paper checks to direct deposit.** A disaster can disrupt mail for days or weeks. Federal benefits can go to a bank account or a Direct Express prepaid card.
- **Lost or left-behind cards:** call the issuer, cancel, and ask for replacements.
- **Bills still come due.** If you may miss a mortgage, rent, loan or card payment, call the lender straight away and ask what flexibility they offer. Paying late without a word can mean fees and damage to your credit.
- **Payment apps need a network** on both ends. Before sending money to someone new, double-check the recipient and the amount.
- **Traveler's checks** are on Ready.gov's kit list as an alternative to cash. Confirm where they can be cashed before relying on them.

## Price gouging

Price gouging means sharply raising prices on essentials such as water, fuel, food, lodging or generators during an emergency. In the US it is handled mainly by state law, and the rules differ from state to state. A high price is not automatically an illegal one.

If you think you are being gouged:

1. **Buy what you truly need, if you can afford it.** Fuel to evacuate is worth more than the argument.
2. **Keep the receipt** and photograph the posted price. Note the date, time, business name and address.
3. **Do not confront anyone.** It will not lower the price and it can turn ugly.
4. **Report it** to your state attorney general or consumer protection office. That is where the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau sends price-gouging complaints.

Watch for scams dressed up as bargains. Be wary of "limited time only" pressure, and never pay a stranger by wire transfer, gift card or cryptocurrency: it is almost impossible to get that money back. Disaster fraud of any kind can be reported in the US to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to your state attorney general.

## When cash is not enough: barter

In nearly every real disaster, money keeps working and outside help arrives. What fills the gap in the first days is not a new currency. It is neighbors lending, sharing and swapping: an hour on a generator for a hot meal, a ride for a tank refill.

That is worth being ready for, and the Barter & Trade in Emergencies guide covers how to do it fairly and safely. Two points belong here:

- **Do not buy "disaster currency."** Stockpiling alcohol, tobacco, ammunition or precious metals to trade ties up money you need for cash, water and food, creates a theft and safety risk, and reselling some of these is restricted by law in many places. None of the official preparedness guidance behind this guide recommends it.
- **Cash first.** A stash of small bills will be accepted by more people, for more things, than anything else you could store.

## People who need extra thought

- **Anyone paid by paper check or mailed benefits:** switch to direct deposit before the season starts.
- **Older adults:** agree with a trusted relative where the cash is and who can get to the accounts, and agree that nobody pays a stranger at the door or on the phone without checking with the other first.
- **Teenagers who may be on their own:** enough in their bag for a meal and a fare home, plus a card with phone numbers.
- **People with little to spare:** do not borrow to build a cash stash. A small amount saved slowly, plus food and water at home, covers most short outages.
- **Outside the US:** the same approach works in your own currency, but deposit insurance, consumer law and reporting channels are national. Check your own regulator's advice.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a cash-only errand. On your next fuel or grocery stop, leave the cards in your pocket and pay with small bills, as if the readers were down. Note what a normal fill-up and a few days of food actually cost. Back home, fill in the table above, count the cash in the house, and see how far apart the two numbers are.

**What would you do?**

1. *Day two of a regional outage. One gas station is open, cash only. You need about a third of a tank to reach family out of town, and all you have is one large bill. The attendant has no change.*

   **Answer:** Your choices now are poor: overpay, fill containers you do not have, or drive on. The least bad is usually to team up, covering the next driver's fuel with the difference and settling in small bills or later. Do it calmly and without waving money around. The real answer is the one you fix at home: keep the stash in ones, fives, tens and twenties.

2. *A major hurricane is three days out. A coworker says banks could be shut for weeks and you should withdraw your entire savings in cash while you can.*

   **Answer:** No. Your deposits are insured and will still be there when the branch reopens; cash in a house in a hurricane's path is not. Withdraw what covers several days of real needs for your household, in small bills, and take it with you if you evacuate. Leave the rest where fire, flood and theft cannot reach it.

3. *After a flood, a man selling generators from a truck offers you the last one at four times the store price, payment by gift cards only, and says three other people want it.*

   **Answer:** Walk away. "Last one" and "others are waiting" are pressure tactics, and gift cards are a scammer's payment method because the money cannot be recovered. If you want to report him, note the place, time and what was offered and contact your state attorney general or the disaster fraud line. Do not argue with him.
