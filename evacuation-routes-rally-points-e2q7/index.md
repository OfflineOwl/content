# Evacuation Routes & Rally Points

> **Decide where before you need to know.** When the order comes, the roads fill, the phones jam, and your household may be in four different places. A plan made on a calm evening tells everyone which way to go and where to find each other.

## Start here

1. **Learn your zone and your local plan.** Ask your city or county emergency management office whether you live in an evacuation zone (common for hurricanes, tsunamis and wildfire) and which routes it uses. Sign up for local alerts.
2. **Pick your meeting places:** one right outside your home, one in your neighborhood, one outside your neighborhood, and one out of town.
3. **Choose two destinations in different directions,** and two routes out. A fire to the north means you go south.
4. **Mark it all on a paper map** and keep a copy in the car and in each go-bag.
5. **Adopt the fuel rule:** never below half a tank, and a full tank whenever evacuation seems likely.
6. **Share the plan** with everyone in the household and with your out-of-town contact, and put the meeting places on each person's wallet card.
7. **Travel it once.** A route you have driven is a route you can follow under stress.

Not sure whether you should leave at all? OfflineOwl's Stay or Go tool walks through that decision step by step.

## Call 911 if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number: 112 in much of Europe, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia) if:

- **You are trapped** by fire, water or debris and cannot get out. Give your location. Help may be delayed, so turn on lights to make yourself easier to find.
- **Your vehicle is caught in moving water.** Stay inside if the water is moving fast around it. If water is rising inside the car, get onto the roof.
- **Someone is injured,** or cannot be moved without help and danger is close.

If you will need help to evacuate because of a disability, a medical condition or no transport, do not wait for the emergency. Arrange it now with your local emergency management office.

## When evacuation seems likely

A storm watch, a wildfire nearby, a river rising: this is the time that decides how the evacuation goes.

- **Fill the tank.** Gas stations may close, and cannot pump at all in a power outage. Charge an electric vehicle fully.
- **Get cash in small bills** and charge every phone and power bank.
- **Load the car:** go-bags, medicines, documents, pet carriers and food. See the Go-Bag Essentials by Family Member guide.
- **Listen to local officials** on a battery radio, local broadcasts and phone alerts. They decide which routes are open.
- **Tell your out-of-town contact** your plan and likely destination.
- **Leave now if you need extra time.** No car, limited mobility, small children, a person on medical equipment, pets or livestock: go before the order, not with the crowd.

## When it is time to go

1. **Go when you are told to.** If officials order an evacuation for your area, leave immediately.
2. **Take** go-bags, medicines, documents and your pets.
3. **Dress for rough conditions:** sturdy shoes, long pants, long sleeves, a hat.
4. **If there is time:** close and lock doors and windows. Unplug small appliances, but leave refrigerators and freezers plugged in unless flooding is likely. Shut off water, gas and electricity only if there is damage and you are instructed to. Leave a note saying when you left and where you are going.
5. **Check on neighbors** who may need a ride.
6. **Take one car per household.** Fewer cars means less gridlock for everyone.
7. **Follow the recommended evacuation routes.** Do not take shortcuts. They may be blocked.
8. **Watch for hazards:** washed-out roads and bridges, downed power lines, flooded stretches.
9. **Text your out-of-town contact** once you are moving, and again when you arrive.

### Never drive into floodwater

More than half of flood drownings happen when a vehicle is driven into floodwater. The numbers explain why:

| Depth of moving water | What it can do |
|-----------------------|----------------|
| 6 inches (15 cm) | Knock over an adult |
| 12 inches (30 cm) | Carry away most cars |
| 2 feet (60 cm) | Carry away SUVs and trucks |

You cannot see how deep the water is or whether the road is still there under it. Turn around. Never drive around barricades, and stay off bridges over fast-moving water: they can wash out without warning.

## Meeting places (rally points)

A rally point is simply an agreed place to regroup. You need four, because emergencies come in four sizes.

| Where | Use it when | Good choices |
|-------|-------------|--------------|
| **Right outside your home** | Fire, gas smell, anything that empties the house in seconds | A specific tree, mailbox or neighbor's driveway, well clear of the building |
| **In your neighborhood** | You cannot get into your home or street | Library, community center, place of worship, a friend's house within walking distance |
| **Outside your neighborhood** | The neighborhood is closed off, or you are scattered at work and school | A relative's home or a familiar public place across town |
| **Out of town** | The whole area is evacuating | The home of family or friends who have agreed to it, away from the likely hazard |

Make each one work:

- **Name the exact spot.** "The flagpole at the front entrance", not "the school". Familiar and easy to find beats clever.
- **Ask permission** if it is someone's home or business.
- **Agree what happens next:** how long you wait, and that anyone who cannot get there goes on to the next place and texts the out-of-town contact.
- **Children must know all four** and carry them on a card.
- **Learn the plans where your family spends its days.** Schools, daycares and workplaces have their own evacuation plans. Find out where children would be taken and who is allowed to collect them.

The Family Emergency Action Plan guide covers the out-of-town contact and wallet cards in full.

## Planning your routes

**Start with the official routes.** Many areas sign their evacuation routes, and emergency management offices publish zone and route maps. Those are the roads officials will direct people onto.

**Then know a second way.** For each destination, find another route that leaves your neighborhood by a different road. Your alternate is for when the first choice is closed, not for outsmarting traffic. On the day, the route officials announce overrides the one on your map.

For each route, write down:

| Note | Why |
|------|-----|
| Distance and normal driving time | Expect it to take far longer in an evacuation |
| Fuel stops along the way | You may not be able to fill up near home |
| Low spots, underpasses and bridges | These flood or close first |
| The point where your two routes split | That is where you choose |
| The address and phone number at the far end | Phones and memory both fail |

**Use paper.** Phone maps need battery and signal, and both run short in an evacuation. Highlight the routes on a paper road map and mark the meeting places. The Offline Maps & Navigation and Compass & Map Navigation guides can help if you end up finding your own way.

**Keep the car ready.** Half a tank at all times, a full tank when trouble is forecast, and an emergency kit in the trunk. See the Vehicle Emergency Kit guide.

## If you do not have a car

Ready.gov's advice is to plan how you will leave before you need to.

- **Arrange a ride now.** Ask a neighbor, relative or coworker, agree where they will pick you up, and have a second person as backup.
- **Ask your local emergency management office** what transport it provides in an evacuation and whether you need to register for it in advance.
- **Know your public transit options** and where pickup points are likely to be.
- **Pack a go-bag you can carry** on foot or on a bus.
- **Leave early.** Without your own vehicle you have the least room for delay.

## Where you will stay

- **Friends or family outside the area** are usually the easiest option. Ask them now.
- **Hotels** can fill quickly along evacuation routes. Leaving early is what gets you a room.
- **Public shelters:** check with local officials for what is open. In the US the FEMA app lists open shelters, and you can text SHELTER and your ZIP code to 43362.

## Pets and animals

If you have to leave, so do your pets. Animals left behind may be lost, injured or worse.

- **Most public shelters accept only service animals,** and many hotels do not take pets. Find a place that will before you need it: a friend, a boarding kennel, a pet-friendly hotel on your route.
- **Have a carrier for each pet,** several days of food, water and medicine, vaccination records, and a photo of you with your pet.
- **Set up a buddy system** with a neighbor in case you are not home when the order comes.
- **Horses and livestock:** evacuate earlier than you would for people alone. Plan primary and secondary routes, a trailer, and a destination with feed and water.

See the Pet Preparedness & Care guide.

## People who need extra time or help

- **Disabilities and medical needs:** build a support network of people who know your plan. Ask local transit providers and your emergency management office about accessible transport, and whether there is a voluntary registry. If you depend on powered medical equipment or dialysis, talk to your provider about where you would go. See the Special Needs Preparedness guide.
- **Older adults:** practice the route to the nearby meeting place, and name the person who will check on them.
- **Young children:** rehearse it as a game, and make sure caregivers and babysitters know the plan.

## If you get separated

1. **In danger? Call 911 first.**
2. **Go to the meeting place** you agreed for this kind of emergency.
3. **Text, do not call.** Text your out-of-town contact where you are and where you are heading.
4. **Wait the agreed time,** then move to the next meeting place and update your contact.

## Coming home

Wait until officials say it is safe, and check with officials both where you are staying and back home before you travel. Returning before debris is cleared is dangerous. Fill the tank, carry water and food for the trip, stay away from downed lines, and tell family when you leave and when you arrive.

## Evacuation plan checklist

- [ ] Evacuation zone and official routes looked up
- [ ] Local alerts turned on for every phone
- [ ] Four meeting places chosen, exact spots named
- [ ] Two destinations in different directions, hosts asked
- [ ] Two routes marked on a paper map, copies in car and go-bags
- [ ] Fuel never below half a tank
- [ ] Ride arranged if there is no car, or for anyone who cannot drive
- [ ] Pet carrier, pet supplies and a pet-friendly place to stay
- [ ] School, daycare and workplace plans known
- [ ] Out-of-town contact has the plan
- [ ] Routes driven and meeting places walked this year

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Walk the whole household to your neighborhood meeting place, right now, without phones for directions. Time it. Each person touches the exact spot. On the way back, have the youngest person lead. Then put a date in the calendar to drive your second route out of town, in daylight, with the paper map.

**What would you do?**

1. *An evacuation order is in effect. The signed route is crawling. Your phone suggests a back road through the river valley that would save forty minutes.*

   **Answer:** Stay on the official route. Shortcuts may be blocked, flooded or simply closed with nobody there to tell you, and a low river road is the worst place to find that out. The official route is the one officials are watching and managing. Slow and moving beats clever and stranded. The real fix for traffic is leaving earlier next time.

2. *You are two miles from your sister's house. Brown water is flowing across a dip in the road. It looks ankle deep, and the truck ahead of you just made it through.*

   **Answer:** Turn around. You cannot see the depth or whether the road surface is still there, 12 inches of moving water can carry away most cars, and a heavier truck proves nothing about yours. Take your alternate route, or wait on higher ground. More than half of flood drownings begin with exactly this decision.

3. *A wildfire evacuation order arrives at 2 p.m. Your children are at school, your partner is at work across town, and you are home with the dog.*

   **Answer:** Do not wait at home for everyone to gather. Load the dog and the go-bags and leave. Follow the school's emergency plan, which you looked up in advance: it tells you whether students are moved and where parents collect them. Text your partner and your out-of-town contact which meeting place you are heading for. This is exactly what the outside-the-neighborhood and out-of-town meeting places are for.
