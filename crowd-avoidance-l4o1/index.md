# Crowd Safety & Avoidance

> **Crowds don't trample people. They squeeze them.** Most people who die in a crowd crush are not run over. They are pressed so tightly, often while still on their feet, that they cannot breathe. The danger is density, and you can feel it building in time to leave.

## Do this now

If the crowd around you is getting tight:

1. **Leave while you still can.** Head for the edge the moment you feel uncomfortable. Don't wait to see if it eases.
2. **Get your arms up in front of your chest, like a boxer.** This protects your ribs and keeps a little room for your lungs.
3. **Stay on your feet.** Plant them firmly. Don't bend down for a phone, a bag or a shoe.
4. **Don't push back against the flow.** Move with it and keep your balance.
5. **When the movement eases, work your way diagonally toward the edge,** a few steps at a time.
6. **If you fall, get up at once.** If you can't, curl into a ball and protect your head until you can.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number), and alert venue staff, security or police, if:

- **People near you are saying they can't breathe,** or someone has collapsed or gone limp while standing.
- **People are falling and others are piling onto them.**
- **Someone pulled out of a crush is not breathing normally.** Start CPR. See the CPR and Rescue Breathing guide.
- **Someone exposed to tear gas or pepper spray has breathing trouble that isn't easing in fresh air,** especially a person with asthma or other lung disease.

Anyone who was pinned and has chest pain, trouble breathing, or passed out needs to be checked by a medical professional, even if they feel better. Emergency numbers differ outside the US; learn the local one when you travel.

## How crowds become deadly

The word "stampede" gives the wrong picture. In most crowd disasters nobody is running. People are packed until the pressure from all sides stops their chests from expanding. The inquiry into the 1989 Hillsborough stadium disaster in England found asphyxiation behind the vast majority of the deaths, and survivors described knowing that people around them were dying while being unable to move.

Crowd scientists measure the risk in people per square meter. A square meter is about 11 square feet, roughly the floor of a small shower stall.

| Density | What it feels like | What to do |
|---------|--------------------|------------|
| Under about 3 people per square meter | You can walk without touching anyone | Fine. Know where the exits are. |
| 4 to 5 per square meter | You keep bumping into people without meaning to | No immediate danger, but move away from the center now. Crowd-safety professor G. Keith Still calls 5 the upper limit for a standing crowd. |
| About 6 or more per square meter | You are touched on all sides, can't freely move your hands or touch your face, and the crowd sways and moves you without your choosing | Acute danger. Protect your chest and get out. |

You will never count people per square meter in real life. Use your body instead: **if you can't easily lift your hands to your face, you should already be leaving.**

Density loads the trap, and something small usually springs it. Professor Still has pointed to a sudden downpour, a shout that someone has a gun, or a surge toward a performer that runs into a barrier. The CDC lists the conditions behind crushes as a venue holding more people than it safely can, weak crowd control, and blocked or missing exits. People at the back usually have no idea anyone at the front is in trouble, so they keep pressing forward.

## Before you go

The best crowd skill is declining to be in the dangerous one. As Professor Still puts it, if it feels too crowded or doesn't feel safe, don't go there.

- **Find the exits when you arrive,** and the first aid point. The way you came in is the exit everyone else will think of first.
- **Agree on a meeting place** in case your group is separated, somewhere outside the venue and easy to describe.
- **Stay toward the edges** or in less crowded areas. The view is worse and the odds are better.
- **Stand on level, dry ground** and watch for trip hazards such as trash, curbs and potholes.
- **Wear closed, sturdy shoes** and carry little. Charge your phone.
- **Go easy on alcohol.** Getting out of a tightening crowd takes early judgment.
- **Beat the exit rush.** Leave a little before the end, or stay put for ten minutes after it and let the gates clear.
- **With children:** take a photo of them that day, put your phone number in their pocket or on a wristband, and tell them to stand still and ask a staff member or a parent with children for help if they lose you. Keep them out of the dense middle entirely.

## Reading a crowd

Look around and listen. Crowd researchers say the warning signs come well before the emergency:

- **You are being touched on all sides,** or shuffling instead of walking.
- **The crowd sways** or moves in waves that carry you a step or two.
- **The sound changes:** shouts for help or cries of distress instead of cheering.
- **A bottleneck is ahead:** a tunnel, stairs, a gate, a narrowing street, or two streams of people meeting head-on.
- **Nobody is managing it:** no staff, no barriers or signs dividing the space, no announcements.
- **More people are arriving than leaving,** and there is nowhere for them to go.

Any one of these is a reason to move to the edge. Two or more is a reason to leave. Leaving also helps the people who stay, because every person who goes takes pressure out of the crowd.

## If you are caught in a crush

The steps at the top of this guide come from the CDC's advice for travelers and from researchers who study crowd disasters. Here is why each one works.

- **Arms up like a boxer.** Folded in front of your chest, your forearms take the squeeze instead of your ribcage and keep a few centimeters of space for your lungs to expand.
- **Save your breath.** Suffocation is what kills in a crush, so don't spend air on screaming unless it is to warn people or call for help. Breathe steadily.
- **Stay upright.** A person who goes down can bring down those around them like dominoes, and the weight of others can pin them before they can stand. If someone next to you stumbles, haul them up immediately.
- **Go with the flow.** Pushing against a crowd wastes energy you need. Let it carry you, keep your balance, and steer.
- **Move diagonally in the lulls.** Crowd pressure comes in waves. In the gaps between them, slide sideways and forward toward the edge, not straight against the stream.
- **Stay away from walls, fences, barriers and pillars.** People pinned against something solid have nowhere to give. Several stadium disasters, Hillsborough among them, happened against fences and walls.
- **Look up.** A ledge, a fence you can climb, a doorway or a raised platform can be a way out that nobody else is using.
- **If you fall and can't get up, curl into a ball** with your arms around your head, and get up the moment there is space.

Once you are out, keep going until you are in open space. Then check yourself and the people with you, and send help back by telling staff or police exactly where the crush is.

## When a crowd runs

Sometimes a crowd does bolt, away from a real or imagined threat: a bang, a rumor, a fight. The rush itself is often the greater danger. Don't run with the pack into the nearest bottleneck. Step out of the stream if you can, into a doorway or side street, take a breath, and work out what is actually happening and which way is clear. Then move to safety, staying as far from the densest part of the crowd as you can.

If there is a real attack, official US guidance is to get away first and to take cover and hide if you can't. See the Lockdown Procedures guide.

## Demonstrations and unrest

Even peaceful demonstrations can turn violent without warning, and police may close streets, impose curfews or use crowd-control agents at short notice.

- **Avoid them if you are not there on purpose.** Check local news before going out, and take another route.
- **If you find yourself in one,** stay on the outer edge, keep moving, and leave by a side street at the first sign of trouble: objects thrown, police lines forming, or people starting to run.
- **If violence starts,** stay calm, move away from its source, and head for the nearest way out. If you can't leave, shelter in a secure place such as an open business.
- **Follow police instructions** and keep your hands visible and empty.
- **Don't stop to film.** The time you spend filming is time you could spend leaving.
- **In a car,** turn around early and go another way. See the Emergency Defensive Driving guide.

## If tear gas or pepper spray is used

Riot control agents irritate the eyes, skin, nose and lungs within seconds: tearing, burning, blurred vision, coughing, chest tightness, a choking feeling, sometimes nausea. The CDC says the effects usually last only 15 to 30 minutes after you are away from the source and cleaned off. The cloud is dense and travels close to the ground.

1. **Get out of the cloud and into fresh air.** Outdoors, go to the highest ground you can reach, and move upwind if you can tell which way the air is drifting. Indoors, get out of the building.
2. **Don't rub your eyes.**
3. **Rinse your eyes with plain water for 10 to 15 minutes.** Take out contact lenses and throw them away; don't put them back in. Glasses can be washed with soap and water and worn again.
4. **Take off contaminated clothing.** Cut off anything that would have to be pulled over your head. Seal it in a plastic bag.
5. **Wash your skin with soap and water** as soon as you can.
6. **Get medical care if breathing trouble continues or gets worse,** or if eye pain doesn't settle. People with asthma or chronic lung disease are at higher risk and should carry their inhaler anywhere crowds and police may meet.

For advice in the US, Poison Help is 1-800-222-1222.

## People who need extra thought

- **Children** are small enough to disappear among adult bodies, where nobody can see that they are in trouble. Keep them at the edges and leave early.
- **Wheelchair users and people who can't stand for long:** use accessible viewing areas where they exist, and avoid being in the flow when a crowd arrives or leaves.
- **Older adults and pregnant women:** the edges, a seat, and an early exit.
- **People with asthma, heart or lung conditions:** carry medication on your body, not in a checked bag or a car.
- **People who are deaf or hard of hearing:** you may miss announcements and the change in crowd noise, so watch staff and screens and go with a companion who knows to alert you.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Do this at home with the people you go to events with. First, everyone stands and takes the boxer stance: feet apart, forearms up in front of the chest, three slow breaths. Second, agree on your standing rule for any event: "If we get separated, we meet at..." and practice naming a spot for the last place you went together. Third, the next time you walk into a store, station or venue, give yourself 30 seconds to find two exits before you do anything else.

**What would you do?**

1. *You are near the front at a concert. The headliner is about to come on, and people are pouring in behind you. You notice you can't easily lift your arms, and the crowd is starting to sway. Your friends want to hold the spot.*

   **Answer:** Go now, and take your friends with you. Not being able to raise your hands is the acute-danger sign, and it will get worse when the act starts and people surge forward. Arms up, and work diagonally toward the side in the lulls. Watching from the edge costs you a view. Staying costs you the option of leaving.

2. *After a match, the crowd funnels toward a tunnel and stops. Pressure is building from behind. There is a gap along the fence at the side, and people around you are starting to shove forward.*

   **Answer:** Don't go to the fence and don't shove. A fence or wall is where people get pinned, and pushing adds to the pressure on the people at the front. If you are not yet in the funnel, turn back to open space and wait ten minutes. If you are in it, get your arms up, keep your feet, go with the movement, and use each lull to move diagonally toward open ground. If there is something you can climb over into clear space, take it.

3. *You are walking home when a demonstration spills into the street ahead. Something lands nearby and a white cloud drifts toward you. Your eyes begin to burn. You are wearing contact lenses.*

   **Answer:** Move away from the cloud right away, uphill and upwind if you can tell, and down a side street instead of along the route the crowd is running. Don't rub your eyes. Once you are in clean air, take out the lenses and throw them away, then rinse your eyes with plain water for 10 to 15 minutes. Change clothes and wash with soap and water when you get home. If your breathing doesn't ease in fresh air, get medical care.
