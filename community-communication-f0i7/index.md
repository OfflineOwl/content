# Community Communication During Emergencies

> **Your neighbors are the first responders.** After a big disaster, fire, police and 911 are swamped with life-threatening calls, and the people who reach you first live within shouting distance. A block that can pass a message without phones finds its injured faster and wastes less effort on rumors.

## Start here

1. **Knock on three doors this week:** the homes on either side and the one across the street. Swap names and phone numbers on paper, not just in your phone.
2. **Start one group text for your block.** Texts get through when calls do not. Keep it for emergencies and tests so nobody mutes it.
3. **Agree on one meeting spot and two check-in times,** such as the corner mailbox at 9 a.m. and 5 p.m. A fixed time works even when every device is dead.
4. **Give every household an OK / HELP door sign** (see below). It lets one walk down the street replace twenty phone calls.
5. **Ask who would want a knock first:** people who live alone, use medical equipment that needs power, or have trouble getting around.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if someone is badly hurt, trapped, not breathing normally, or there is fire, a gas smell or a downed power line. Neighborhood networks are for everything else.

- **Calls will not connect?** Try a text to 911. It only works in some areas. If it does not, your phone gets an automatic bounce-back message telling you to use another method.
- **No phone works at all?** Send two people on foot or by bike to the nearest fire station, with the address and the problem written down.
- **Do not count on anyone official hearing a walkie-talkie.** FRS and GMRS radios reach your neighbors, not a dispatcher.

Outside the US the number differs: 112 works across the European Union and on mobile networks in many other countries.

## Why the block is the right size

Washington State's emergency management agency puts it plainly: in a disaster, your most immediate source of help is the neighbors living around you. Its Map Your Neighborhood program is built around one street at a time, about 15 to 20 homes, or an area you can walk and check in one hour. That is small enough that everyone knows a face, and large enough to hold a nurse, a chainsaw and a spare generator.

Bigger than that, split into two blocks with a link person between them.

## Build it in layers

No single method survives every failure, so stack them. Start at the top and drop down a row when one stops working.

| Layer | Works when | Reach | Weak point |
|-------|-----------|-------|------------|
| Group text | Cell network is up, even if jammed | Everyone at once | Dead batteries, towers down |
| Phone tree (calls or texts) | Any phone service, including landlines | Whole list in minutes | Breaks where one person is missing |
| FRS / GMRS radios | No phone service at all | Often a few blocks, sometimes more | Batteries, short range, no privacy |
| OK / HELP door signs | Always | As far as you can walk | Says nothing about what is wrong |
| Message board at the meeting spot | Always | Anyone who walks there | Only as current as the last visit |
| Check-in rounds on foot | Always | Every door | Slow, needs volunteers |

## Group text and phone tree

A **group text** is the fastest tool while the network is alive. Keep messages short, and do not send photos or video during a disaster: they load the network everyone is trying to use.

A **phone tree** is the backup for people who do not text, and for landlines. One person calls a small group, each of them calls a few more, and the news reaches everyone without any one person making 20 calls.

How to build one that holds up:

1. **Pick a coordinator and a deputy** on different streets or with different phone carriers.
2. **Give each person only a few names to call.** Three is a good number.
3. **Write the message down before the first call** so it is the same at the end as at the start: who it is from, what happened, what to do, when the next update comes.
4. **No answer? Leave the message, then call that person's names yourself** so the branch does not die. Tell the coordinator who was missed. That home gets a door knock.
5. **Close the loop.** The last person on each branch calls or texts the coordinator: "Branch three complete, could not reach number 14."
6. **Print it.** Every household keeps a paper copy. A phone tree stored only in phones is gone when the batteries are.

Test it twice a year. Numbers change faster than you expect.

## Two-way radios: FRS and GMRS

When phones are gone, cheap handheld radios are the only way to talk in real time. In the US, two services share the same 22 channels, and FRS and GMRS radios can legally talk to each other.

| | FRS (Family Radio Service) | GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service) |
|---|---|---|
| License | None. Any age may use one. | FCC license required. No exam, age 18 or older, $35, valid 10 years. |
| Who the license covers | Not applicable | The license holder and immediate family. **Not neighbors.** |
| Channels | 1 to 22 | The same 22, plus repeater channels |
| Power limit | 2 watts on channels 1 to 7 and 15 to 22; 0.5 watt on 8 to 14 | 5 watts on 1 to 7; up to 50 watts on 15 to 22 (mobile and base units); 0.5 watt on 8 to 14 |
| Antenna | Fixed, cannot be swapped | Can be upgraded; repeaters allowed |

What that means for a neighborhood:

- **Buy FRS radios for the block.** A GMRS license covers a family (spouse, children, parents, grandparents, siblings, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, in-laws), so every household that wants GMRS power needs its own. The one exception: a licensee may hand their radio to anyone to send an emergency message.
- **Ignore the range on the package.** "Up to 36 miles" assumes one mountaintop talking to another. An FCC-hosted training guide for CERT volunteers says reliable FRS contact beyond a mile is the exception, not the rule. Among houses and trees, plan on a few blocks and test it. The FCC says GMRS users can expect 1 to 25 miles depending on the equipment, terrain and repeaters.
- **Pick a primary and a backup channel from 1 to 7 or 15 to 22.** Channels 8 to 14 are capped at half a watt. An FCC-hosted CERT training guide suggests channel 1 as the channel neighbors turn to first; any choice works if everyone knows it.
- **Turn "privacy codes" off.** They do not make a conversation private. They only mute other users on the channel, including someone calling for help.
- **Listen at set times, not all day.** Five minutes at the top of each hour makes a set of batteries last days longer than leaving the radio on.
- **Use plain words.** Say who you are calling, then who you are: "Oak Street, this is number 12, over." GMRS users give their call sign at the end and every 15 minutes. Emergency traffic has priority on every channel.

The Radio Use for Emergency Updates guide covers receiving official broadcasts. The Emergency Codes & Signals guide has the phonetic alphabet and radio phrasing.

## Door signs and check-in rounds

The **OK / HELP sign** is used by Washington State's neighborhood program and many city programs. Each home has a card that reads OK on one side and HELP on the other, and hangs the right side on the front door or a street-facing window after a disaster. No printer? Write HELP or OK on paper with a marker.

A walking round then takes minutes:

1. **Go in pairs,** in daylight if you can, with a notepad, a whistle and a radio if you have them.
2. **HELP signs first.** Then homes with no sign at all, which may mean nobody is home or nobody is able to hang one. OK signs last.
3. **Knock, call out, and look from outside.** Do not go into a damaged building. If someone is hurt or trapped inside, that is a 911 call or a runner to the fire station.
4. **Write down what you find** and bring it back to the meeting spot: address, time, who you spoke to, what they need.

A sign is not a call for help. If you put up HELP, keep trying 911 too. Take the signs down when the emergency is over.

## The message board and meeting spot

Choose a spot that is central, out in the open, away from power lines and anything that could fall, and reachable by someone using a wheelchair or walker. Seattle formalizes this idea as Community Emergency Hubs: places where neighbors gather after a disaster to help each other.

Put up a board (a sheet of plywood, a garage door, a fence with clothespins) with four headings:

- **INFO:** official news only, with the source and time. "NOAA radio, 14:10: boil-water notice for the east side."
- **NEEDS:** "No. 9, insulin needs refrigeration."
- **OFFERS:** "No. 4 has a generator, can charge phones 10 to noon."
- **PEOPLE:** who has checked in, who has left and where they went.

One rule stops most rumors: **every note carries a name, a time and where the information came from.** A note without them gets a question mark, not a megaphone.

## Messages that survive being passed on

Every hand-off loses detail. Write messages so they still work on the fourth retelling:

- **Who, where, what, need, next.** "Nguyen family, No. 12. All four safe. Power out. Have water for 3 days. Need infant formula. Back at the board at noon."
- **Numbers and names, not feelings.** "Tree across Elm at No. 20, wires down, do not pass" beats "it is really bad down there."
- **Read it back.** The receiver repeats the key facts before passing it on. Radio operators call this the echo technique, and it catches most errors.

## Who gets checked first, and privacy

Ask now, while it is calm: who lives alone, who is older or has a disability, who depends on powered medical equipment, who does not speak much English, who is Deaf or hard of hearing (use texts, notes and door knocks, not shouting), and which homes may have children alone after school.

Only list what people agree to share. Keep the list on paper with the coordinator and deputy, not on a public social media page. An "everyone is away" list is useful to the wrong people too.

## Programs that will train your block

- **CERT (Community Emergency Response Team).** A FEMA-backed program with more than 3,200 local teams. Volunteers learn fire safety, light search and rescue, team organization and disaster medical operations. Ask your city or county emergency management office. FEMA's free online course IS-317 is an introduction.
- **Map Your Neighborhood and its successors.** Washington State's program teaches the OK / HELP sign, a skills and equipment inventory, a neighborhood map and a contact list. The state no longer prints its workbooks and now promotes "2 Weeks Ready" neighborhood materials, and many cities run their own versions.
- **Outside the US,** ask your local civil protection office or Red Cross or Red Crescent society.

## Block checklist

- [ ] Paper contact list for 15 to 20 homes, with a coordinator and a deputy
- [ ] Group text set up and tested
- [ ] Printed phone tree in every home
- [ ] OK / HELP sign in every home
- [ ] Meeting spot and two daily check-in times agreed
- [ ] FRS radios, spare batteries, primary and backup channel written down
- [ ] List of neighbors who want an early check, with their consent
- [ ] Board, markers, tape and paper stored near the meeting spot
- [ ] A battery or hand-crank radio for official news

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Run a block roll call. Send one test message to the group text: "TEST. Reply with your house number." Time how long it takes to hear from every home. For each one that has not answered after 10 minutes, walk over and knock. You will find the dead numbers, the muted chats and the neighbor who never got added, and you will have practiced the round you would walk for real.

**What would you do?**

1. *Second day of an outage. Everyone has answered the group text except the older man at No. 12, who lives alone. There is no sign on his door. Someone says, "He's probably at his daughter's."*

   **Answer:** "Probably" is how people get missed. No sign and no reply moves him to the top of the list. Two people go now, knock, call his name and look through the windows. If he answers, ask what he needs. If there is no answer and there is any reason to worry (car in the drive, lights or a radio on, mail piling up), call 911 and tell them exactly what you see. Do not break in on a guess.

2. *A neighbor offers to equip the street: a case of radios marked "36-mile range," a privacy code "so nobody can listen in," and everyone can "just use my GMRS license."*

   **Answer:** Thank him, then fix three things. The range claim assumes open line of sight; walk the block and test it. Privacy codes hide other people from you, not you from them, and they can hide a call for help, so switch them off. And his license covers his family, not the street. Plain FRS radios need no license and are the right choice for a block.

3. *An unsigned note appears on the board: "City says tap water is contaminated. Don't drink it."*

   **Answer:** Do not tear it down and do not repeat it as fact. Mark it "unconfirmed" with the time, and send someone to check an official source: the battery radio, a Wireless Emergency Alert, the city's text alerts. Until you know, the cautious choice costs little: use stored water, or treat tap water as the Water Purification guide describes. Then post what you found, with your name, the time and the source.

BLOCK COMMUNICATION

SIZE: 15-20 homes, 1 coordinator
      + 1 deputy, list on PAPER

LAYERS (top fails -> next one)
1. Group text (short, no photos)
2. Phone tree (3 names each,
   last person reports back)
3. FRS radio, channel ___ / ___
   5 min at top of each hour
   Privacy codes OFF
4. OK / HELP sign on door
5. Board at meeting spot
6. Rounds on foot, in pairs

ROUNDS: HELP first, then no sign,
then OK. Do not enter damaged
buildings.

EVERY MESSAGE
Who - Where - What - Need - Next

 2 W on 1-7 and 15-22
