# Emergency Nutrition for Children

> **Adults can get by on short rations for a while. Children cannot, and babies least of all.** For an infant, the wrong water, a stretched bottle, or a homemade formula can do real harm within days. Plan the children's food and water first, by age, and the rest of the household's around it.

## Start here

1. **Breastfeeding? Keep going.** It is the safest way to feed a baby in an emergency: no water, no bottles, no power.
2. **Using formula? Store ready-to-feed.** It is sterile and needs no water. Never water formula down, and never make your own.
3. **Set aside safe water** for mixing powdered formula and for children's drinking. Bottled water comes first.
4. **Store food your children already eat** that needs no cooking or refrigeration. A disaster is the worst time to introduce new food.
5. **Check every stored food against the choking list** below if you have a baby or young child.
6. **Learn the signs of dehydration.** Small bodies dry out fast.
7. **Allergies or a medical diet:** store safe versions, labeled, and keep epinephrine with the kit if prescribed.

## Get emergency help if

**Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if a child:**

- **Is choking** and cannot cough, cry, or breathe. Start choking first aid: see the CPR and Rescue Breathing guide.
- **Has signs of a severe allergic reaction:** trouble breathing or swallowing, swollen lips or tongue, widespread hives, pale or bluish skin, fainting. Give epinephrine right away if you have it, then call. Give a second dose if there is no improvement in 5 minutes.
- **Has signs of severe dehydration:** very sleepy or hard to wake, extremely fussy, sunken eyes, cool or discolored hands and feet, wrinkled skin, urinating only once or twice a day.
- **Has a seizure, goes limp, or cannot be woken.**
- **Has diabetes** and has passed out or cannot swallow.

**Call the doctor right away** (or go to an emergency room if you cannot reach one) for:

- A baby with fewer than 6 wet diapers a day, a dry mouth, no tears, or a sunken soft spot on the head
- A child who is vomiting and too sick to drink
- A baby under 3 months with a temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher
- Any baby who has been fed diluted or homemade formula and seems unwell

Outside the US, use your local emergency number.

## Babies under 12 months

### Breastfeeding

Breast milk needs no clean water, no fuel, and no sterilizing, and it helps protect the baby against diarrhea, vomiting, and other infections.

- **Stress does not dry up your milk.** It can slow the let-down, the release of milk, so it may take a few minutes longer. Milk production continues. Slow breathing, a quiet corner, and keeping the baby close all help.
- **You can breastfeed even when you are short of food.** Your milk still gives the baby what they need. The household should still make sure a nursing mother eats and drinks first: she needs more water than other adults.
- **Worried about supply? Feed more often.** Giving formula in place of feeds lowers supply.
- **Babies under 6 months need no water or other drinks,** even in hot weather. Breast milk or formula is enough.
- **Stopped recently? You may be able to restart.** Relactation is possible with frequent nursing or expressing and help from a lactation specialist. Keep giving formula as needed until supply is established, and have the baby's weight checked.
- **Learn hand expression** in case a pump has no power.

**Expressed milk without a fridge:**

| Where | How long |
|-------|----------|
| Room temperature, 77°F (25°C) or cooler | Up to 4 hours |
| Insulated cooler with frozen ice packs | Up to 24 hours |
| Refrigerator | Up to 4 days |
| Left over from a feeding | Use within 2 hours, then discard |

### Formula

**Ready-to-feed formula is the safest choice in an emergency.** It is sterile and needs no water. This matters most for babies under 2 months, premature babies, and babies with a weakened immune system, because powdered formula is not sterile.

If powder is what you have:

1. **Wash your hands** with soap and water, or use hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol.
2. **Use bottled water** until officials say tap water is safe. No bottled water? Boil tap water at a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 feet / about 2,000 m) and let it cool.
3. **Measure the water first, then add the powder,** using exactly the amounts on the container and the scoop that came with it.
4. **Shake, do not stir.** Warming is optional. Never use a microwave.
5. **Use it within 2 hours of mixing, and within 1 hour of the start of a feeding.** Throw away what is left in the bottle.
6. **No refrigerator? Make each bottle fresh** and make only what the baby will drink. With a working fridge, formula mixed and chilled right away keeps for 24 hours.

**For babies under 2 months, premature babies, or babies with a weakened immune system** who must have powder: boil the water, wait about 5 minutes, and mix while the water is still very hot, around 158°F (70°C). That heat kills germs that can live in the powder. Cool the bottle and test a few drops on your wrist before feeding.

**Rules that do not bend:**

- **Never dilute formula.** Too much water leaves a baby short of nutrients. Too little strains the kidneys and causes dehydration. Use the label amounts.
- **Never make homemade formula.** The FDA has reports of babies hospitalized with dangerously low calcium after being fed it.
- **No cow's milk as a drink before 12 months.** It can cause intestinal bleeding and is hard on a baby's kidneys.
- **Water that is chemically contaminated cannot be fixed** by boiling, filtering, or disinfecting. Use bottled water or ready-to-feed formula.
- **Throw away cans** that are dented, rusty, or bulging, or that touched floodwater. Use opened powder within 1 month.

**Running low?** Get help while you still have days left, not hours: shelter staff, a hospital or clinic, your pediatrician, a food bank, or the WIC program in the US. Ask for ready-to-feed.

### Bottles, cups, and cleaning

Bottles and nipples are hard to clean without safe water, and a dirty bottle can make a baby sick.

- **If you cannot clean bottles properly, feed from a small disposable cup,** a new one each time. Hold the baby upright, rest the rim on the lower lip, tip it until the milk just touches the lips, and let the baby lap or sip at their own pace. Do not pour milk into the mouth.
- **To clean:** take everything apart, wash in a clean basin (not the sink) with soap and safe water, rinse with safe water, and air-dry on a clean surface. Do not dry with a dish towel.
- **Sanitize at least daily for babies under 2 months or babies who are sick:** boil the items for 5 minutes, or soak them for 2 minutes in 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach per half gallon (8 cups / about 2 liters) of safe water. Air-dry and do not rinse.

### Starting solid food

From about 6 months, babies can have soft food alongside breast milk or formula, which stays the main food until 12 months.

- Mash or chop food finely, feed slowly in small portions, and watch them eat.
- **No honey before 12 months,** in food, water, or on a pacifier. It can cause infant botulism.
- **No juice before 12 months.**
- From about 6 months, a little water is fine: 4 to 8 oz (120 to 240 mL) a day.
- Throw away opened baby food you cannot keep cold.

## Choking hazards hiding in emergency food

Typical emergency snacks are some of the worst choking risks for babies and young children. From the CDC's list:

| Avoid for young children | Safer swap |
|--------------------------|------------|
| Whole or chopped nuts and seeds, trail mix | Nut butter spread thinly on bread or a cracker |
| Peanut butter by the spoonful or in lumps | The same, spread thin |
| Hard candy, gummy candy, chewy fruit snacks, marshmallows, gum | Applesauce, soft canned fruit |
| Whole grapes, cherry tomatoes, whole pieces of canned fruit | Cut into small pieces |
| Raisins and other uncooked dried fruit | Soft canned fruit, mashed banana |
| Popcorn, chips, pretzels, granola bars, crackers with seeds | Soft bread, plain crackers that dissolve, soft cereal |
| Hot dogs, meat sticks, tough meat, large cheese chunks | Finely chopped or shredded canned meat, small pieces of soft cheese |
| Whole beans, whole corn kernels | Mashed |

Children should sit upright to eat, not walk, play, or ride in a car or stroller. Keep meals calm and unhurried, and watch them the whole time.

## How much food

Children and pregnant women should not have their food rationed. If supplies are short, healthy adults cut back first.

| Age | Calories a day (range covers girls to boys, quiet to active) |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Under 2 years | Breast milk or formula on demand to 12 months, soft food from about 6 months, then small meals and snacks often. Do not count calories. |
| 2 to 4 years | 1,000 to 1,600 |
| 5 to 8 years | 1,200 to 2,000 |
| 9 to 13 years | 1,400 to 2,600 |
| 14 to 18 years | 1,800 to 3,200 |

Figures are from the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020 to 2025, and are estimates to adjust for size and activity. Two things stand out: a teenager can need more food than an adult, and small children need small amounts often, so plan snacks between meals.

## What to store

- **Familiar, no-cook basics:** ready-to-eat cereal, shelf-stable milk, canned fruit in juice, applesauce cups, canned beans, canned chicken and tuna, nut butter, bread or plain crackers, instant oatmeal, canned pasta and soups.
- **Low-salt choices where you can.** Salty food makes children thirsty when water is limited, and babies and toddlers should not have salty food.
- **A few comfort foods.** A familiar treat settles a frightened child. It should not replace meals.
- **Baby food pouches or jars and infant cereal** for babies over 6 months.
- **Size the kit to the child you have now.** Check it every few months: babies move from formula to solids, and teens eat more each year.

**A no-cook day for a school-age child:** cereal with shelf-stable milk and canned peaches; nut butter on bread with applesauce; canned chicken on crackers with canned green beans; canned pasta with canned carrots and a fruit cup; water with every meal and snack.

## Water and dehydration

Never ration a child's water. Needs go up in heat, with fever, and with diarrhea or vomiting.

| Age | Drinks a day (more in heat or when active) |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|
| Under 6 months | Breast milk or formula only. No water. |
| 6 to 12 months | Breast milk or formula, plus 4 to 8 oz (120 to 240 mL) of water |
| 1 to 3 years | About 4 cups (1 liter), water or milk |
| 4 to 8 years | About 5 cups (1.2 liters) |
| 9 years and up | About 7 to 8 cups (1.7 to 1.9 liters) |

**Early signs of dehydration:** playing less, a dry or sticky mouth, fewer tears, dark urine or fewer trips to the toilet, and in babies fewer than 6 wet diapers a day or a sunken soft spot. Severe signs are in the emergency list above.

**If a child is vomiting or has diarrhea:**

- Give small amounts of fluid every few minutes, by spoon, syringe, or small cup if needed. Aim for at least 1 oz (30 mL) an hour.
- **Babies:** keep breastfeeding or giving formula at normal strength. Do not give plain water to babies. Ask a doctor about oral rehydration solution if they cannot keep feeds down.
- **Age 1 and up:** oral rehydration solution is best. It comes as a liquid or as freezer pops, and keeps well in a kit.
- Sports drinks and juice are high in sugar, which can make diarrhea worse. If they are all you have for a child over 1, dilute them half and half with water.
- Food does not need to be held back for more than 24 hours.

**Juice limits:** none before 12 months; up to 4 oz (120 mL) a day for ages 1 to 3; 4 to 6 oz for ages 4 to 6; up to 8 oz (240 mL) for ages 7 to 18. Water and milk are the main drinks.

Diarrhea in a disaster can mean unsafe water. See the Water Purification guide.

## Allergies, diabetes, and medical diets

- **Food allergies:** donated and shelter food is the risk. Read every label every time, and when there is no label, do not serve it. Store several days of known-safe food. Tell shelter staff on arrival. Keep epinephrine with the child, check its expiry date, and never use an antihistamine in place of it for a severe reaction.
- **Diabetes:** pack fast sugar (glucose tablets, regular juice boxes), snacks, and supplies. In a shelter, tell staff the child needs to eat on a schedule. See the Managing Chronic Conditions guide.
- **Tube feeding, special formulas, thickened liquids:** keep at least a week on hand and a plan for the pump if the power fails. See the Special Needs Preparedness guide.

## Stress and eating

A frightened child may refuse food, eat only one thing, eat for comfort, or go back to younger habits such as wanting a bottle. All of this is normal for a while.

- Keep mealtimes regular and eat together. Routine is reassuring in itself.
- Offer familiar food in small portions and let them ask for more.
- Do not force, bribe, or punish with food.
- Let children help choose and prepare what they can. A task gives them some control.
- In the short term, drinking matters more than eating. A child who will not drink needs attention: see the dehydration signs.

See the Normalcy Kids guide for more on routines.

## Kids' food kit

**Babies**

- [ ] Ready-to-feed formula in small bottles, if not exclusively breastfeeding
- [ ] Bottled water set aside for the baby
- [ ] Small disposable cups, bottles, nipples
- [ ] Wash basin, dish soap, bottle brush, unscented bleach
- [ ] Baby food and infant cereal, for 6 months and up
- [ ] Soft spoons and bibs

**Toddlers and children**

- [ ] Shelf-stable milk
- [ ] Cereal, instant oatmeal, bread or plain crackers
- [ ] Nut butter (if no allergy)
- [ ] Canned fruit in juice, applesauce cups
- [ ] Canned beans, chicken, tuna, pasta, soup
- [ ] A few favorite snacks, checked against the choking list
- [ ] Child-size cups, bowls, spoons

**Everyone**

- [ ] Oral rehydration solution
- [ ] Manual can opener
- [ ] Hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol
- [ ] Allergy-safe food, labeled, and epinephrine if prescribed
- [ ] Extra drinking water for children and for a nursing mother

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Shelf dinner. Let the kids build tonight's snack plate using only the emergency shelf: no stove, no fridge, no microwave. While they choose, you do two checks. Hold each item against the choking list for your youngest child, and read one label out loud for allergens and the use-by date. Afterward, everyone gives each food a thumbs up or down. Replace the thumbs-down items with something they will actually eat, and write the date on the shelf.

**What would you do?**

1. *Day three of a regional outage. Stores are closed. There is half a can of powdered formula left for your four-month-old, enough for about a day. A relative says to add extra water so it lasts three days, and your grandmother offers her evaporated-milk recipe.*

   **Answer:** Neither. Diluted formula leaves a baby short of nutrients, and homemade formula has put babies in the hospital. Keep mixing at full strength and spend today finding more: the nearest shelter, hospital, or clinic, your pediatrician's emergency line, neighbors with babies, a food bank or WIC office. Ask for ready-to-feed. Say plainly that you have a baby and no formula.

2. *A boil-water notice is in effect after a flood. Your six-week-old is on powdered formula and the bottled water has run out. You have a gas stove and a pitcher filter. The filter would be quicker.*

   **Answer:** Boil. A pitcher filter is not made to remove germs. Bring the water to a rolling boil for 1 minute. Because the baby is under 2 months, wait about 5 minutes and mix the powder while the water is still very hot, then cool the bottle and test it on your wrist. Use it within 2 hours and discard leftovers. If the notice says the water is chemically contaminated or "do not use," boiling will not help: use only bottled water or ready-to-feed formula, and ask responders for some.

3. *At a shelter, a volunteer hands your hungry two-year-old a snack bag: trail mix with peanuts and raisins, hard candies, and a bunch of grapes. He is already reaching for it.*

   **Answer:** Take the bag before he does. Nuts, raisins, hard candy, and whole grapes are all on the choking list for a child his age, and peanuts are also a common allergen. Thank the volunteer and ask for something soft: applesauce, a banana, bread, cereal. If the grapes are all there is, cut them into small pieces. Sit him down to eat, and stay with him.
