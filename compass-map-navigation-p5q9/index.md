# Compass & Map Navigation

> **Find Your Way Without GPS** - When technology fails, batteries die, or satellite signals are unavailable, the ability to navigate with a compass and paper map becomes a critical survival skill. This guide covers everything you need to move confidently through unfamiliar terrain using time-tested methods that require no electricity, no cell signal, and no internet connection.

---

## Why Learn Manual Navigation

GPS devices and smartphone maps are convenient, but they depend on batteries, satellite coverage, and functioning hardware. In emergencies, any of these can fail. A quality compass and a paper topographic map will continue working in rain, cold, electromagnetic interference, and total grid failure.

Manual navigation is not a backup plan -- it is a foundational skill. Once you learn it, you carry it with you permanently.

---

## How to Read a Baseplate Compass

A baseplate compass is the standard tool for land navigation. Understanding its parts is the first step.

### Parts of a Baseplate Compass

- **Baseplate** - The flat, transparent plastic body. It has a straight edge used as a ruler and for drawing lines on maps.
- **Direction-of-travel arrow** - Printed on the baseplate, pointing away from you. This is the direction you will walk.
- **Rotating bezel (housing)** - The numbered ring you turn by hand, marked in degrees from 0 to 360.
- **Magnetic needle** - The freely spinning needle inside the housing. The **red end points to magnetic north**.
- **Orienting arrow** - Printed on the floor of the housing. You rotate the bezel until the magnetic needle sits inside this arrow.
- **Orienting lines** - Parallel lines on the housing floor that align with north-south grid lines on a map.
- **Declination scale** - Found inside the bezel on some models, used to correct for magnetic declination.
- **Ruler/scales** - Printed on the baseplate edges, used to measure distances on maps.

### Holding the Compass Correctly

1. Hold the compass flat in your palm at waist height.
2. Keep it level so the needle swings freely.
3. Point the direction-of-travel arrow away from your body.
4. Keep metal objects (belt buckles, knives, phones) away from the compass -- they cause false readings.
5. Do not stand near vehicles, power lines, or large metal structures.

### Reading a Basic Bearing

1. Hold the compass level and steady.
2. Wait for the magnetic needle to stop swinging.
3. Rotate the bezel until the **orienting arrow** frames the **red end** of the magnetic needle. This is often called "putting red in the shed."
4. Read the number on the bezel at the index line (where the direction-of-travel arrow meets the bezel). That number is your bearing in degrees.

## Magnetic Declination and How to Adjust

### What Declination Is

A compass needle does not point to true north (the geographic North Pole). It points to **magnetic north**, which is a shifting point in northern Canada. The angular difference between true north and magnetic north at your location is called **magnetic declination**.

- **East declination** means magnetic north is east of true north.
- **West declination** means magnetic north is west of true north.

Declination varies by location and changes slowly over time. In parts of the eastern United States, declination can be 10-20 degrees west. In parts of the western United States, it can be 10-20 degrees east. In some locations it is near zero.

### Why It Matters

A 1-degree error over 1 mile puts you about 92 feet off course. A 10-degree declination error over 5 miles puts you nearly a mile from your intended destination. In dense forest or low visibility, that is the difference between reaching safety and getting further lost.

### How to Adjust for Declination

**Method 1: Adjustable compass**

Many quality compasses have a small screw or tool that lets you set the declination mechanically. Once set, the compass automatically compensates. This is the preferred method because it eliminates math errors in the field.

**Method 2: Manual calculation**

If your compass lacks an adjustment feature:

- **East declination: subtract** the declination from your map bearing to get a compass bearing.
- **West declination: add** the declination to your map bearing to get a compass bearing.

Memory aid: **"East is least (subtract), West is best (add)"** when going from map to field.

**Method 3: Mark your map**

Draw magnetic north lines directly on your map using the declination value shown in the map's legend. Then use these lines instead of the printed grid lines when orienting the map.

### Finding Your Local Declination

- Check the margin of your topographic map (note the print date -- declination shifts over years).
- Look it up before your trip on NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information website.
- If you have no data, navigate using magnetic bearings consistently and avoid mixing true and magnetic references.

## Taking and Following a Bearing

### Taking a Bearing from a Map

1. Place the compass on the map so the **long edge of the baseplate** connects your current position and your destination.
2. Make sure the direction-of-travel arrow points toward your destination on the map.
3. Hold the baseplate firmly in place and rotate the **bezel** until the orienting lines align with the north-south grid lines on the map. Ensure the "N" on the bezel points to the top (north) of the map.
4. Read the bearing at the index line. This is your **map bearing**.
5. Apply declination correction to convert to a **compass bearing** if needed.

### Taking a Bearing from the Field

1. Hold the compass level and point the direction-of-travel arrow at a visible landmark.
2. Rotate the bezel until the orienting arrow frames the red end of the needle.
3. Read the bearing at the index line. This is your **field bearing** (magnetic).

### Following a Bearing

1. Set the desired bearing on the bezel.
2. Hold the compass level with the direction-of-travel arrow pointing forward.
3. Rotate your entire body until the red needle sits in the orienting arrow ("red in the shed").
4. Look up and identify a visible landmark along the direction-of-travel arrow -- a distinctive tree, rock, or hilltop.
5. Walk to that landmark. Do not stare at the compass while walking.
6. At the landmark, repeat the process: recheck your bearing, find the next landmark, walk to it.
7. Continue until you reach your destination.

### Tips for Accuracy

- **Use intermediate waypoints.** In thick forest, pick objects 50-100 meters ahead rather than trying to sight distant landmarks.
- **Go around obstacles.** When you cannot walk straight (cliff, lake, dense brush), make right-angle detours: turn 90 degrees, count your paces, turn back to your original bearing, walk past the obstacle, turn 90 degrees the other way, walk the same number of paces back to your original line, then resume your bearing.
- **Back bearing.** Periodically look behind you. Your back bearing should be 180 degrees from your forward bearing. If you are heading 045 degrees, looking back should show 225 degrees.

## Triangulation to Find Your Position

Triangulation (more precisely, **resection**) lets you determine where you are on a map by taking bearings to two or more known landmarks.

### Steps

1. Identify **two or three** landmarks you can see and also find on your map. The more widely separated they are, the better. Ideally, they are 60-120 degrees apart from your perspective.
2. Take a compass bearing to the **first landmark**.
3. Convert the field bearing to a map bearing by reversing the declination correction.
4. Calculate the **back bearing** (add or subtract 180 degrees) because you want to draw a line from the landmark to you, not the other way.
5. On the map, place the compass edge on the landmark with the direction-of-travel arrow pointing along the back bearing. Draw a line along the baseplate edge.
6. Repeat for the **second landmark**.
7. The intersection of the two lines is your approximate position.
8. A **third bearing** creates a small triangle (called a "cocked hat") that gives you a confidence area for your position. Your location is somewhere inside that triangle. A smaller triangle means higher accuracy.

### Common Mistakes

- [ ] Using landmarks that are too close together (less than 30 degrees apart), producing an unreliable fix
- [ ] Forgetting to correct for declination
- [ ] Confusing forward and back bearings
- [ ] Choosing a "landmark" you cannot positively identify on the map

## Reading Topographic Maps

### What a Topographic Map Shows

A topographic map represents three-dimensional terrain on a flat sheet. Unlike road maps, topographic maps show elevation, terrain shape, water features, vegetation, and man-made structures.

### Contour Lines

Contour lines are the brown curved lines covering the map. Each line connects points of **equal elevation**.

**Key principles:**

- **Contour interval** - The elevation difference between adjacent contour lines. Found in the map margin (common intervals: 10, 20, 40, or 80 feet). Every fifth line is thicker and labeled with its elevation -- this is an **index contour**.
- **Close together = steep slope.** Lines bunched tightly indicate a cliff or steep hill.
- **Far apart = gentle slope.** Widely spaced lines indicate flat or gently rolling terrain.
- **Closed circles = hilltop or depression.** Small closed loops at the center indicate a summit. If the loops have small tick marks pointing inward, it is a depression.
- **V-shapes pointing uphill = valleys and drainages.** Contour lines form V-shapes that point upstream in valleys.
- **V-shapes pointing downhill = ridges.** Contour lines forming V-shapes pointing to lower elevation indicate ridges or spurs.
- **Concentric circles = hills.** A series of nested contour loops indicates a hill, with the highest elevation at the center.

### Map Scale

Map scale tells you the relationship between distance on the map and distance on the ground.

| Scale | 1 inch equals | Common use |
|-------|--------------|------------|
| 1:24,000 | 2,000 feet | Hiking, detailed navigation |
| 1:50,000 | ~4,167 feet | Military, regional planning |
| 1:100,000 | ~1.6 miles | Road navigation, overview |
| 1:250,000 | ~4 miles | Regional overview |

For field navigation, **1:24,000** (also called 7.5-minute) maps provide the most useful detail.

### Map Legend and Margins

Every topographic map includes critical information in its margins:

- **Legend** - Symbol key explaining colors, line types, and icons
- **Scale bar** - Graphical ruler for measuring distances
- **Contour interval** - Elevation difference between lines
- **Declination diagram** - Shows the difference between true north, magnetic north, and grid north
- **Date of survey/revision** - Indicates how current the map data is. Trails, roads, and buildings may have changed since publication.
- **Grid reference system** - UTM coordinates, latitude/longitude, or township-range markers

### Color Coding on USGS Topographic Maps

- **Brown** - Contour lines (elevation)
- **Blue** - Water features (streams, lakes, swamps, glaciers)
- **Green** - Vegetation (forests, orchards)
- **White** - Open or cleared areas
- **Black** - Man-made features (buildings, roads, trails, boundaries)
- **Red/pink** - Major roads, urban areas, survey data
- **Purple** - Revisions made from aerial photos (not field-verified)

## Navigating Without GPS

### Orienting Your Map

Before you can navigate, orient your map so it aligns with the terrain around you.

1. Place the compass on the map with the baseplate edge along a north-south grid line.
2. Rotate the map and compass together until the magnetic needle points to magnetic north (adjusting for declination).
3. The map now matches the real world -- features to your left on the map are to your left on the ground.

### Terrain Association

This is the most practical day-to-day navigation technique:

1. Orient your map.
2. Identify features around you -- a ridge to the west, a stream crossing ahead, a saddle between two hills.
3. Find those same features on the map.
4. Track your progress by noting when you pass identifiable features.
5. Constantly ask: "What should I see next if I am on the right track?"

### Handrails and Catching Features

- **Handrail** - A linear feature (road, river, ridge, power line, fence) that runs roughly parallel to your direction of travel. Stay near it and you cannot stray far off course.
- **Catching feature** (backstop) - A large, obvious feature beyond your destination that tells you if you have gone too far. A major road, a lake shore, or a river makes a good catching feature.
- **Attack point** - An identifiable feature near your destination that you navigate to first. From the attack point, you make a short, precise move to your final target.

### Pace Counting

Pace counting lets you estimate distance traveled on foot.

1. Measure your pace: walk 100 meters on flat ground, counting every time your **left foot** hits the ground.
2. Most adults take 62-68 paces per 100 meters on flat terrain.
3. In the field, use a **pace count bead set** (ranger beads) to track distance. Pull one bead for every 100 meters.
4. Adjust for terrain: uphill, thick brush, snow, and sand increase your pace count. Downhill on a trail decreases it.

## Using Natural Landmarks

When map detail is limited or you have no compass, natural features can provide direction and location clues.

### Sun-Based Direction Finding

- The sun rises in the **east** and sets in the **west** (approximately -- it shifts seasonally).
- At solar noon in the Northern Hemisphere, the sun is due **south**. In the Southern Hemisphere, it is due **north**.
- **Shadow stick method:** Place a straight stick vertically in the ground. Mark the tip of its shadow. Wait 15-20 minutes and mark the new shadow tip. A line between the two marks runs roughly east-west, with the first mark being west.

### Vegetation and Terrain Clues

These indicators are **general tendencies**, not absolutes. Use multiple clues together.

- In the Northern Hemisphere, moss and lichen tend to grow thicker on the **north side** of trees and rocks (the shadier, moister side), but this is unreliable in dense forest or humid climates.
- Snow melts faster on **south-facing slopes** in the Northern Hemisphere.
- Prevailing wind patterns shape trees -- leaning trees or flagged branches indicate the dominant wind direction, which varies by region.
- River systems flow downhill and generally toward larger bodies of water. Following water downstream usually leads to human habitation eventually, but this can take days and involve difficult terrain.

### Using Linear Features

Roads, trails, power lines, fence lines, railroad tracks, and rivers are all linear features that lead somewhere. If you are lost and encounter one, following it is almost always safer than continuing cross-country.

## Night Navigation by Stars

### Northern Hemisphere: Finding Polaris (The North Star)

Polaris sits almost directly above the geographic North Pole and appears stationary while all other stars rotate around it.

**Finding Polaris:**

1. Locate the **Big Dipper** (Ursa Major), one of the most recognizable constellations.
2. Find the two stars forming the **outer edge of the cup** (farthest from the handle). These are called the "pointer stars."
3. Draw an imaginary line through these two stars, extending it about **five times the distance** between them, away from the cup opening.
4. You will arrive at a moderately bright star standing relatively alone. That is **Polaris**.
5. Polaris marks **true north**. Face it, and south is behind you, east is to your right, west is to your left.

**If the Big Dipper is below the horizon:**

Look for **Cassiopeia**, a W-shaped (or M-shaped) constellation on the opposite side of Polaris from the Big Dipper. Polaris lies roughly between the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia.

### Southern Hemisphere: Using the Southern Cross

There is no bright "South Star" equivalent. Instead, use the **Southern Cross** (Crux).

1. Find the Southern Cross -- four bright stars forming a small cross shape, with a fifth dimmer star offset to one side.
2. Identify the **long axis** of the cross (the line between the top and bottom stars).
3. Extend this line from the top of the cross through the bottom, continuing outward about **4.5 times** the length of the long axis.
4. That point in the sky is approximately above the **South Celestial Pole**.
5. Drop a vertical line from that point to the horizon. That spot on the horizon is roughly **due south**.

### General Star Navigation Tips

- Stars near the horizon rise in the east and set in the west, just like the sun. Place a stick in the ground and sight along it to a star near the horizon. If the star appears to rise, you are facing roughly east. If it sinks, you are facing west.
- Stars move about 15 degrees per hour across the sky. A star directly overhead is not useful for direction finding.
- Cloud cover eliminates star navigation. Always have a backup method.

## Basic Orienteering Techniques

### Aiming Off

When navigating to a point on a linear feature (such as a bridge on a river), deliberately aim to one side. When you hit the river, you **know which direction to turn** to reach the bridge. If you aimed straight for it and missed, you would not know whether to go left or right.

### Boxing Around Obstacles

When a direct path is blocked:

1. Turn 90 degrees from your bearing.
2. Count your paces as you walk parallel to the obstacle.
3. Turn back to your original bearing and walk past the obstacle.
4. Turn 90 degrees the opposite direction from step 1.
5. Walk the same number of paces counted in step 2.
6. Resume your original bearing. You are back on your original line of travel.

### Collecting Features

As you walk, note features you pass and check them off against the map. This running inventory confirms your position continuously.

- "Crossed a small stream" -- check.
- "Passed through a saddle between two hills" -- check.
- "Trail junction, left fork goes uphill" -- check.

If expected features stop matching, **stop immediately** and re-evaluate your position before continuing.

### Leapfrogging (Team Navigation)

With two or more people:

1. The navigator sets the bearing and directs the partner to walk ahead to a specific point along the bearing.
2. The partner stops at the point and becomes a visual reference.
3. The navigator walks to the partner, then directs them to the next point.
4. This ensures straighter travel through areas where intermediate landmarks are scarce.

## Emergency Navigation When Lost

### The STOP Protocol

If you realize you are lost, do not keep walking. Follow **STOP**:

- **S - Sit down.** Resist the urge to move. Panic causes poor decisions.
- **T - Think.** When did you last know your position? What features have you passed?
- **O - Observe.** Look at the terrain around you. Can you identify anything on the map? Check your compass bearing. Listen for roads, rivers, or other sounds.
- **P - Plan.** Make a deliberate decision based on what you know, not what you hope.

### Strategies When Lost

1. **Backtrack.** If you can retrace your steps to the last known position, do so. This is often the safest option.
2. **Navigate to a catching feature.** Pick a large, unmissable feature (a major road, a river, a coastline) and set a bearing toward it. Even with moderate error, you will reach it.
3. **Follow water downhill.** Streams lead to rivers, rivers lead to civilization. Be cautious -- stream valleys can become impassable gorges.
4. **Gain elevation.** Climbing to a high point gives you a better view of surrounding terrain and may allow you to identify your location or spot landmarks.
5. **Stay put and signal.** If you have told someone your plans and expected return time, staying in place may be the best option. Rescuers will look along your planned route.

### Navigation Checklist for Emergency Situations

- [ ] Confirm compass is functioning (needle swings freely, no bubbles blocking movement)
- [ ] Orient your map to the terrain
- [ ] Identify at least one known feature you can see and find on the map
- [ ] Establish your approximate position using resection if possible
- [ ] Choose a large, unmissable target to navigate toward
- [ ] Set a bearing and identify your first waypoint
- [ ] Begin pace counting to track distance
- [ ] Check your bearing frequently -- every 100-200 meters
- [ ] Note features you pass and match them to the map
- [ ] If nothing matches, stop and reassess before continuing

## Map Care and Waterproofing

A soggy, torn map is useless. Protect your maps before you need them.

### Prevention

- **Store maps in a waterproof map case.** Clear plastic cases with zip-lock or roll-top closures are lightweight and inexpensive.
- **Fold maps properly.** Fold along existing creases. Refolding in new directions weakens the paper and creates tears at intersections of fold lines.
- **Carry maps in a zip-lock bag** as a minimum level of protection if you do not have a dedicated case.

### Waterproofing Methods

**Method 1: Commercial map treatment**

Products like Nikwax Map Proof or Aquaseal Map Seal are spray-on or wash-in treatments designed specifically for paper maps. Follow the product instructions and allow full drying time before folding.

**Method 2: Clear packing tape**

Cover both sides of the map with overlapping strips of wide, clear packing tape. This is heavy but very effective. Best for small sections of a map that you use repeatedly.

**Method 3: Print on waterproof paper**

If you print your own maps, use synthetic waterproof paper (such as Rite in the Rain or similar brands). These sheets handle rain, sweat, and submersion without deteriorating.

**Method 4: Lamination**

Office supply stores can laminate map sheets. This adds bulk and stiffness but makes the map virtually indestructible in wet conditions. Best for maps you will use on multiple trips.

### Field Repair

- If a map tears, repair it immediately with tape on both sides to prevent the tear from spreading.
- If a map gets wet, lay it flat and let it air dry completely before refolding. Do not wring or crumple it.
- Carry a backup photocopy of the critical section of your map in a separate waterproof bag.

## Quick Reference: Bearing Conversion

| Situation | Calculation |
|-----------|------------|
| Map bearing to compass bearing (east declination) | Compass = Map - Declination |
| Map bearing to compass bearing (west declination) | Compass = Map + Declination |
| Compass bearing to map bearing (east declination) | Map = Compass + Declination |
| Compass bearing to map bearing (west declination) | Map = Compass - Declination |
| Forward bearing to back bearing | Back = Forward +/- 180 degrees |

## Navigation Kit Checklist

The following items should be in your pack whenever you venture into unfamiliar terrain:

- [ ] Baseplate compass (with adjustable declination if possible)
- [ ] Topographic map(s) of your area, in a waterproof case
- [ ] Pencil (not pen -- pencil works when wet and can be erased)
- [ ] Small notepad or margins of the map for recording bearings and pace counts
- [ ] Pace count beads (ranger beads) or a simple tally system
- [ ] Protractor or roamer for precise grid references (optional but useful)
- [ ] Backup compass (small button compass on a zipper pull or watchband)
- [ ] Red headlamp or flashlight (red light preserves night vision for star navigation)
- [ ] Current declination value written on the map or on a card in your kit

## Practice Before You Need It

Navigation is a perishable skill. Reading about it is not enough.

1. **Start in a park or known area.** Practice taking bearings to visible landmarks and checking your accuracy.
2. **Navigate a short course.** Set three or four waypoints on a map, navigate to each one, and see how close you get.
3. **Practice in limited visibility.** Try navigating in fog, dense forest, or after dark to build confidence.
4. **Time yourself.** As you improve, you will orient the map, take a bearing, and begin moving in under two minutes.
5. **Take a formal orienteering course.** Many outdoor clubs and search-and-rescue organizations offer navigation workshops.

The time to learn these skills is before an emergency, not during one. A compass and map weigh almost nothing, cost very little, and never run out of battery.
