# Compass & Map Navigation

> **A compass never loses signal.** A paper map and a baseplate compass work in rain, cold and dead-battery darkness. But they only work for someone who has used them before the day it matters.

## Start here

1. **Get the two tools:** a baseplate compass and a paper topographic map of the area you actually walk in. Keep them together in a zip-top bag.
2. **Look up your declination** (the gap between true north and magnetic north where you are) and write it on the map with today's date.
3. **Orient the map** once, outdoors: turn it until north on the paper matches north on the ground.
4. **Take one bearing and walk it** in a park or field.
5. **Count your paces over 100 meters** so you can judge distance.
6. **Before every trip, leave a plan** with someone at home: where you are going, your route, and when you will be back. Carry a whistle.

## If you are lost

The US Forest Service teaches one word: **STOP**.

- **S: Stop.** The moment you think you may be lost, stop, stay calm and stay put. Panic is the real danger.
- **T: Think.** How did you get here? What should you be able to see? Do not take a step until you have a specific reason to.
- **O: Observe.** Get out the compass and map. Work out the directions from where you stand. If you are on a trail, stay on it.
- **P: Plan.** Think through your options. Act on one only if you are very confident of it.

**Stay where you are if** you are not very sure of the route, night is coming, you are injured, or you are close to exhaustion. Moving makes you harder to find.

**Call 911** (or the local emergency number) if you have any signal, and give your last known location. Then signal:

- **Whistle:** three blasts, pause, repeat. Three blasts is a well-known emergency signal, and a whistle carries far for much less effort than shouting.
- **Light:** flash SOS, three quick, three slow, three quick.
- **Be seen:** bright clothing in an open spot, or a mirror flashed toward aircraft or searchers.

**Do not follow a stream downhill as a first idea.** The Forest Service lists it only as a last resort: it is often a hard route and can be very dangerous.

**Teach children to "hug a tree":** pick one tree, stay with it, blow three whistle blasts, count to 30, and do it again.

## Parts of a baseplate compass

| Part | What it does |
|------|--------------|
| **Baseplate** | Clear plastic base with straight edges and a ruler. You lay it on the map. |
| **Direction-of-travel arrow** | Printed on the baseplate. Point it where you want to go. |
| **Dial (bezel)** | The ring you turn, marked 0 to 360 degrees clockwise: N is 0, E is 90, S is 180, W is 270 |
| **Index line** | The mark at the base of the direction-of-travel arrow where you read the bearing |
| **Magnetic needle** | Swings freely. The red end points to magnetic north. |
| **Orienting arrow** | The outlined arrow on the floor of the dial. It turns with the dial. |
| **Orienting lines** | Parallel lines beside the orienting arrow. You line them up with north-south lines on the map. |

Two phrases cover most compass work. A **bearing** is a direction given as an angle from north, clockwise. **"Red in the shed"** means the red end of the needle is sitting inside the orienting arrow.

**Holding it:** flat and level, at waist or chest height, direction-of-travel arrow pointing straight away from you. Keep it away from metal and magnets: phones, knives, belt buckles, vehicles, steel in concrete, power lines. Any of them can pull the needle.

## Declination: the step people skip

Maps are drawn to **true north**. The needle points to **magnetic north**. The angle between them at your location is the **magnetic declination**. It is called east when magnetic north lies east of true north, and west when it lies west.

Declination depends on where you are, and it drifts over the years. In parts of the contiguous United States it is more than 10 degrees. That is not a rounding error:

| Error in your bearing | How far off after 1 mile | After 1 km |
|-----------------------|--------------------------|------------|
| 1 degree | about 92 feet | about 17 m |
| 5 degrees | about 460 feet | about 87 m |
| 10 degrees | about 930 feet | about 176 m |

**Where to get the current value:** NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information runs a free magnetic field calculator that works for any location in the world. Look it up before you lose internet and write it on the map. The diagram in a map's margin shows the declination for the year the map was printed, and the USGS warns that values on old maps may no longer be accurate.

**The arithmetic:**

| Converting | East declination | West declination |
|------------|------------------|------------------|
| Map bearing (true) to compass bearing (magnetic) | Subtract it | Add it |
| Compass bearing (magnetic) to map bearing (true) | Add it | Subtract it |

Memory aid for map to compass: **"East is least, west is best."**

Example: the map says your target is at 60 degrees. With a declination of 10 degrees east, set 50 on the compass. With 12 degrees west, set 72.

**The easy way:** many compasses have an adjustable declination setting. Set it once for your area and the compass does the arithmetic for you. If yours has it, use it, and skip the adding and subtracting in the steps below.

## Reading a topographic map

- **Scale.** On a standard USGS 1:24,000 map, 1 inch on paper is 2,000 feet on the ground (1 cm is 240 m). Use the bar scale in the margin with the ruler on your baseplate.
- **Contour lines** join points of equal height. The **contour interval**, printed in the margin, is the height difference between neighboring lines. Every fifth line is drawn bolder and labeled with its elevation.
- **Lines close together mean steep ground.** Lines far apart mean gentle ground.
- **Closed loops** are hilltops. Where contours form a V, the V points uphill in a valley or stream bed and downhill on a ridge.
- **North is at the top.** The left and right edges of the map run true north-south.
- **Check the date.** Trails, roads and buildings change. Current USGS maps (the US Topo series) are revised on about a three-year cycle and can be downloaded and printed.

## Orient the map

An oriented map is turned so that what is ahead of you on the ground is ahead of you on the paper. Most navigation mistakes disappear once the map is oriented.

**By eye:** if you can see a road, ridge, lake or building that is on the map, turn the map until they line up.

**With the compass:**

1. Turn the dial so N sits at the index line. Then allow for declination, unless your compass is already adjusted for it: for east declination, turn the dial to 360 minus the declination (10 east becomes 350). For west, turn it to the declination itself (10 west becomes 10).
2. Lay the compass on the map with a long edge along the map's left or right edge, direction-of-travel arrow toward the top of the map.
3. Turn the map and compass together until red is in the shed.
4. The map now matches the ground. Compare what you see with what is drawn.

## Take a bearing from the map and walk it

1. **Line it up.** Lay a long edge of the baseplate from where you are to where you want to go, direction-of-travel arrow pointing toward the destination.
2. **Turn the dial** until the orienting lines run parallel to the map's north-south lines, with N on the dial toward the top of the map. Ignore the needle for this step.
3. **Read the number** at the index line. That is the true bearing.
4. **Correct for declination,** unless your compass is already adjusted: subtract east, add west. Turn the dial to the new number.
5. **Lift the compass and hold it level.** Turn your whole body until red is in the shed. The direction-of-travel arrow now points along your bearing.
6. **Pick a landmark** on that line, such as a particular tree or rock, and walk to it. Then repeat.

Walk to landmarks, not with your eyes on the needle. People who stare at the compass drift.

## Take a bearing to something you can see

1. Point the direction-of-travel arrow at the landmark.
2. Turn the dial until red is in the shed.
3. Read the number at the index line. This is a magnetic bearing.

To use it on the map, convert it to true: add east declination, subtract west.

## Back bearings

A back bearing points exactly the opposite way: add 180 if your bearing is under 180, subtract 180 if it is over. The back bearing of 45 is 225. The back bearing of 300 is 120.

Use it to return the way you came, or to check your line by looking back at where you started. A shortcut that needs no arithmetic: leave the dial alone and turn until the **other** end of the needle, the one that is not red, is in the shed. You are now facing the back bearing.

## Find where you are from two landmarks

1. Pick two landmarks you can see and can also find on the map, in clearly different directions. Roughly at right angles is best.
2. Take a bearing to the first and convert it to true. Leave the dial set.
3. Lay the compass on the map with a long edge touching the landmark's symbol. Pivot the whole compass around that point until the orienting lines are parallel to the map's north-south lines, N toward the top.
4. Draw a line along that edge, from the landmark back toward you. You are somewhere on that line.
5. Do the same with the second landmark. You are where the lines cross. A third landmark makes a small triangle, and you are probably inside it.

If you are already on a trail, river or ridge shown on the map, one bearing is enough: you are where the line crosses it.

## Staying found

Orienteers use a handful of habits that make getting lost difficult:

- **Keep the map oriented** and tick off features as you pass them: the stream crossing, the fork, the saddle. If the next expected feature does not appear, stop and work out why.
- **Handrail:** a line feature that runs your way, such as a trail, stream, fence or power line. Follow it.
- **Catching feature:** something large and obvious beyond your target, such as a road or lake shore, that tells you that you have gone too far.
- **Attack point:** an easy-to-find feature close to a hard-to-find target. Navigate to it first, then take a short, careful bearing.
- **Aiming off:** when the target sits on a line feature, such as a bridge on a river, aim deliberately to one side. When you reach the river you know which way to turn.
- **Pacing:** measure out 100 meters on level ground. Walk it normally, counting each time your left foot lands, and repeat a couple of times. That is your pace count per 100 m. It rises on slopes, rough ground, in the dark and when you are tired. Keep the tally by moving a pebble from one pocket to the other every 100 m.
- **Going around an obstacle:** if you can see across (a pond, a clearing), sight a landmark on the far side on your bearing and walk around to it. If you cannot, turn 90 degrees, count your paces until you are clear, return to your bearing and pass the obstacle, then turn 90 degrees back and walk the same count to rejoin your line.

## Common mistakes

- **The 180-degree error.** The direction-of-travel arrow pointing at you, or the wrong end of the needle in the shed, sends you exactly the wrong way. Do a sanity check: the sun is in the east in the morning and the west in the evening.
- **Declination applied backwards,** or not at all.
- **Metal nearby.** Step away from the car and take the phone out of the hand holding the compass.
- **Believing a feeling over the needle.** Away from metal, the compass is right and your sense of direction is not.
- **Folklore.** Moss does not reliably grow on the north side of trees.
- **Phone only.** A phone compass and GPS are good tools with a battery attached. Carry the paper and plastic too. See the Offline Maps & Navigation guide.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** The triangle walk. In a park or field, put a coin on the ground at your feet. Set 40 degrees on the dial, put red in the shed, pick a landmark and walk 30 paces. Add 120 to the dial (160) and walk 30 paces. Add 120 again (280) and walk 30 paces. You should be standing on your coin. A few steps off is good. Far off means you watched the needle while walking or your paces changed. Do it again.

**What would you do?**

1. *You expected a trail junction twenty minutes ago and it has not appeared. There is about an hour of daylight left, and you feel sure the car park is just over the ridge to your left.*

   **Answer:** Stop. The feeling is the trap. Leaving the trail to cross a ridge on a hunch, near dusk, is how short walks become searches. Sit down, look at the map, and work out the last place you were certain of. You are on a trail, so stay on it: if you are confident, walk back along it to that last known point. If you are not, or the light is going, stay put, call 911 if you have signal, put on your layers and signal with three whistle blasts.

2. *The map says the lake is on a bearing of 90 degrees. The margin of your 1998 map shows a declination of 14 degrees east. Your friend says a few degrees cannot matter and sets 90.*

   **Answer:** It matters: an error of 14 degrees puts you about 1,300 feet off for every mile walked. With east declination you subtract, so the compass should read 76. Better still, an old map's declination may be out of date, so use the current value you looked up from NOAA before the trip. If you have no current value, the printed one is far better than ignoring it, and you should plan the leg toward something you cannot miss, like the lake shore, not a single point.

3. *In thick forest you are walking a bearing toward a footbridge over a river. You cannot see more than thirty meters.*

   **Answer:** Do not aim straight at the bridge. If you arrive at the river and see no bridge, you will not know which way to turn. Aim off: set your bearing a few degrees to one side, say upstream. When you hit the river, turn downstream and follow the bank, your handrail, to the bridge.
