FOOD SAFETY WITHOUT REFRIGERATION
==================================

When the power goes out, your refrigerator and freezer start a
countdown. This guide covers how long food stays safe, what to keep,
and what to throw out.

DO THIS NOW
-----------

1. Note the time the power went out.
2. Keep the refrigerator and freezer doors CLOSED.
   - Refrigerator: keeps food safe about 4 hours.
   - Full freezer: about 48 hours.
   - Half-full freezer: about 24 hours.
3. If the outage will last more than 4 hours, move milk, meat, fish,
   eggs, leftovers, and other perishables into a cooler with ice or
   frozen gel packs, and keep them at 40°F (4°C) or below.
4. Get dry ice or block ice for a long outage.
5. Never taste food to decide if it's safe.
6. When the power returns, check temperatures and follow the charts
   below. When in doubt, throw it out.

THE KEY NUMBERS
---------------

REFRIGERATOR: 40°F (4°C) or below
FREEZER: 0°F (-18°C) or below
DANGER ZONE: 40°F - 140°F (4°C - 60°C)
  Bacteria multiply rapidly in this range.

2-HOUR RULE:
Throw out perishable food left at room temperature for 2 hours or
more (1 hour if it's above 90°F / 32°C).

4-HOUR RULE:
Throw out refrigerated perishable food (meat, poultry, fish, eggs,
milk, cut fruit and vegetables, leftovers) after 4 hours without
power or another cold source like ice.

Keep an appliance thermometer in both the refrigerator and the
freezer. It's the only way to know the real temperature.

DURING THE OUTAGE
-----------------

REFRIGERATOR:
- Keep the door closed. An unopened refrigerator stays cold about
  4 hours, whether it's full or not.
- After 4 hours, move perishables to a cooler with ice.

FREEZER:
- Keep the door closed. A full freezer holds about 48 hours; a
  half-full freezer about 24 hours.
- Group food together so it stays cold longer.
- Post a list of contents on the door so you don't have to open it
  to look.

ICE AND COOLERS:
- Block ice lasts longer than cubes.
- Fifty pounds of dry ice should keep a full 18-cubic-foot freezer
  cold for 2 days.
- Dry ice is -109°F (-78°C): handle it only with gloves or tongs,
  and never let it touch food directly.
- Dry ice gives off carbon dioxide gas. Use it only in a ventilated
  space, never in a closed car or small closed room, and keep it
  away from children and pets.
- Keep a thermometer in the cooler. Add ice to keep it at 40°F or
  below.
- Keep the cooler in the coolest, shadiest spot and open it as
  little as possible.

PRIORITY FOR COLD STORAGE:
1. Medicines that need refrigeration
2. Breast milk and opened infant formula
3. Meat, poultry, fish, eggs, and dairy
4. Leftovers
5. Condiments last (many keep fine without cold)

REFRIGERATED MEDICINES:
- Check the label or call a pharmacist.
- FDA says insulin in vials or cartridges can be kept unrefrigerated
  at 59-86°F (15-30°C) for up to 28 days and still work. Keep it
  away from extreme heat and freezing.
- If a medicine needed to sustain life looks normal but got warm,
  FDA advises using it until you can get a replacement. Replace
  other refrigerated medicines that got warm as soon as you can.

BEFORE AN OUTAGE (IF YOU HAVE WARNING):
- Freeze containers of water. They keep food cold and become
  drinking water as they melt.
- Freeze leftovers, milk, and meat you won't need right away.
- Have coolers, ice, and gel packs ready.
- Store food and bottled water off the floor, above possible
  flood levels.

WHEN TO THROW OUT REFRIGERATED FOOD
-----------------------------------

These lists follow the FoodSafety.gov chart for food held above
40°F (4°C) for more than 2 hours.

THROW OUT:

Meat, poultry, seafood:
- Raw or cooked meat, poultry, fish, seafood, soy meat substitutes
- Thawing meat or poultry
- Meat, tuna, shrimp, chicken, or egg salads
- Gravy, stuffing, broth
- Lunchmeat, hot dogs, bacon, sausage, dried beef
- Pizza with any topping
- Canned hams labeled "Keep Refrigerated"
- Opened canned meat and fish
- Casseroles, soups, stews

Dairy and cheese:
- Milk, cream, sour cream, buttermilk, evaporated milk, yogurt,
  eggnog, soy milk
- Soft cheeses (brie, mozzarella, Monterey Jack, cottage, cream
  cheese, ricotta, queso fresco, blue, and similar)
- Shredded cheeses and low-fat cheeses
- Opened baby formula

Eggs:
- Fresh shell eggs, hard-cooked eggs, egg dishes, egg products
- Custards, puddings, quiche

Fruits and vegetables:
- Cut fresh fruit and cut fresh vegetables
- Pre-cut, pre-washed packaged greens
- Cooked vegetables, cooked tofu, baked potatoes, potato salad
- Opened vegetable juice
- Commercial garlic in oil
- Sliced or shredded coconut

Sauces and dressings:
- Opened creamy dressings
- Opened spaghetti sauce
- Fish sauce, oyster sauce
- Opened mayonnaise, tartar sauce, horseradish if held above 50°F
  (10°C) for more than 8 hours

Grains and baked goods:
- Cooked pasta, rice, and potatoes; fresh pasta; pasta salads
- Refrigerator biscuit, roll, and cookie dough
- Cheesecake, cream-filled pastries, pies with egg or milk fillings

USUALLY SAFE TO KEEP (if they look and smell normal):

- Hard cheeses (cheddar, Colby, Swiss, Parmesan, provolone, Romano)
- Processed cheeses; grated Parmesan or Romano in a can or jar
- Butter and margarine
- Uncut fresh fruit and vegetables; fresh mushrooms and herbs
- Opened fruit juice and opened canned fruit
- Dried fruit
- Peanut butter, jelly, relish, mustard, ketchup, olives, pickles
- Worcestershire, soy, barbecue, and hoisin sauces
- Opened vinegar-based dressings
- Bread, rolls, cakes, muffins, tortillas, waffles, pancakes, bagels
- Fruit pies

Throw out anything with an unusual smell, color, or texture, or
that feels warm, even if it's on the "keep" list.

FREEZER FOOD RULES
------------------

STILL HAS ICE CRYSTALS AND FEELS REFRIGERATOR-COLD, OR IS AT
40°F (4°C) OR BELOW:
- Safe to refreeze or cook. Quality may suffer.
- Exception: throw out ice cream and frozen yogurt.

THAWED AND HELD ABOVE 40°F FOR MORE THAN 2 HOURS:
- Throw out: meat, poultry, seafood, soups, stews, casseroles,
  milk, eggs and egg products, soft and shredded cheeses,
  cheesecake, frozen meals and pizza, cakes or pies with custard or
  cheese filling, and ice cream.
- OK to refreeze: hard cheeses; breads, rolls, muffins, and cakes
  without custard fillings; pie crusts and bread dough (quality
  suffers); flour, cornmeal, and nuts; waffles, pancakes, and
  bagels; fruit and fruit juice (throw out if moldy, yeasty-smelling,
  or slimy).
- Vegetables and vegetable juice: refreeze only if they were above
  40°F for less than 6 hours.

If your freezer thermometer reads 40°F or below when the power comes
back, the food is safe and can be refrozen.

FOOD THAT TOUCHED FLOODWATER
----------------------------

THROW OUT:
- Any food that may have touched floodwater or stormwater
- Food in packaging that isn't waterproof: cardboard boxes
  (including juice, milk, and formula boxes), screw caps, snap lids,
  pull tops, crimped caps
- Home-canned food
- Cans that are bulging, leaking, punctured, deeply rusted, or
  badly dented
- Wooden cutting boards, baby bottle nipples, and pacifiers that
  touched floodwater

CAN BE SAVED:
Undamaged, commercially prepared all-metal cans and retort pouches:
1. Remove the labels.
2. Wash off mud and silt, and rinse with clean water.
3. Sanitize: boil for 2 minutes, or soak 15 minutes in 1 cup of
   unscented bleach per 5 gallons of water.
4. Air-dry, re-label with the contents and expiration date, and use
   soon.

ASSESSING FOOD SAFETY
---------------------

DON'T RELY ON:
- Smell or appearance (many germs don't change either)
- Taste (never taste questionable food)

DO RELY ON:
- A food thermometer or appliance thermometer
- How long the food was above 40°F
- The charts above

WHEN IN DOUBT, THROW IT OUT.
Getting sick costs more than the food, especially when medical care
and clean water are hard to get.

SHELF-STABLE ALTERNATIVES
-------------------------

STOCK THESE FOR OUTAGES:

Instead of:          Stock this:
Fresh milk           Shelf-stable (UHT) milk, powdered milk
Fresh eggs           Powdered eggs (for cooking)
Fresh meat           Canned meat, fish, or poultry; jerky
Lunch meat           Canned tuna or chicken
Fresh vegetables     Canned or dried vegetables
Cheese               Grated Parmesan in a can or jar
Butter               Shelf-stable butter or cooking oil
Infant formula       Ready-to-feed formula (needs no water)

PRESERVING FOOD WITHOUT ELECTRICITY
-----------------------------------

SHORT OUTAGE (hours to a day or two):
- Keep the refrigerator and freezer closed
- Use coolers with ice for perishables
- Eat perishable food first, then frozen food, then shelf-stable
  food

LONGER OUTAGE:
- Rely on shelf-stable supplies
- Cook and eat thawing meat while it is still at 40°F or below
- Only cure, smoke, dry, or pickle food if you follow a tested
  recipe (USDA or the National Center for Home Food Preservation).
  Improvised curing and canning can cause botulism.

COOKING TEMPERATURES (USDA)
---------------------------

Use a food thermometer:
- Poultry (all, including ground): 165°F (74°C)
- Ground beef, pork, lamb: 160°F (71°C)
- Beef, pork, lamb steaks, chops, roasts: 145°F (63°C), then rest
  3 minutes
- Fish and shellfish: 145°F (63°C)
- Egg dishes: 160°F (71°C)
- Leftovers and casseroles: 165°F (74°C)

Cook outdoors only with grills and camp stoves. Never use them
indoors or in a garage (carbon monoxide). See the Cooking Without
Power guide.

QUICK REFERENCE
---------------

REFRIGERATOR WITHOUT POWER:
About 4 hours, door closed

FREEZER WITHOUT POWER:
Full: about 48 hours
Half-full: about 24 hours
Door closed

SAFE TEMPERATURES:
Refrigerator 40°F (4°C) or below
Freezer 0°F (-18°C) or below

2-HOUR RULE:
Perishables above 40°F for 2+ hours (1 hour above 90°F): throw out

REFREEZE:
Only if ice crystals remain or the food is 40°F or below

NEVER TASTE TO TEST.
WHEN IN DOUBT, THROW IT OUT.
