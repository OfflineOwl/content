VOLCANIC ASH SAFETY
===================

Volcanic ash is not soft like fireplace ash. It is tiny, jagged
pieces of rock and glass: hard, abrasive, mildly corrosive, and
able to conduct electricity when wet. It can fall far downwind
of an eruption, on places that never see lava. The ash itself
rarely kills. Collapsing roofs, falls during cleanup, and car
crashes in the gloom do.


DO THIS NOW
-----------

If ash is falling or forecast for your area:

1. GET INDOORS AND STAY THERE. Caught outside? Shelter in the
   nearest building or car. If a warning comes before the ash,
   go home so you are not traveling in it.
2. CLOSE UP. Shut doors and windows. Put damp towels along door
   thresholds and tape drafty windows. Turn off heat pumps, air
   conditioners, and fans that pull in outside air.
3. PROTECT LUNGS AND EYES if you must go out: a well-fitted N95
   (P2 or FFP2) mask, and glasses or goggles. Take out contact
   lenses.
4. DON'T DRIVE unless it is an emergency.
5. PROTECT WATER, PETS, AND VEHICLES. If you collect rainwater
   from the roof, disconnect the downpipe from the tank now.
   Bring pets in. Put vehicles under cover or a tarp.
6. LISTEN to local officials by radio. Keep phone lines free
   for emergencies.

EVACUATE IF TOLD TO. Sheltering in place is for ashfall. Near
the volcano there are faster killers, including hot flows and
volcanic mudflows (lahars) that follow river valleys. If
officials order you out, go.


GET EMERGENCY HELP IF
---------------------

Call 911 (in the US) or your local emergency number when:

- Someone has trouble breathing, wheezing that their usual
  inhaler doesn't relieve, or chest pain.
- Someone is hurt in a fall from a roof or ladder, or a roof or
  building has collapsed with people inside.
- You are stranded in a vehicle in heavy ashfall.
- Anyone has a headache, dizziness, or nausea in a closed-up
  home where a fuel heater, stove, or generator is running. That
  is carbon monoxide. Get outside first, then call.

Get medical care soon, but not by ambulance, for eyes that stay
painful, red, or gritty. Ash can scratch the surface of the eye.
Don't rub them.


WHY ASH IS A PROBLEM
--------------------

HEALTH: For most people the effects are short-term: a runny
nose, sore throat, dry cough, and itchy, gritty eyes. Serious
problems are rare. People with asthma, bronchitis, emphysema,
or heart disease are hit harder and can get wheezing and
shortness of breath that lasts for days. Children get a bigger
dose: they run, play in it, and don't take precautions.

WEIGHT: Ash is rock. It is much heavier than people expect,
and wet ash is up to twice as dense as dry.

DARKNESS AND ROADS: Heavy ashfall can turn day into night. As
little as 1 mm (1/32 inch) hides road markings and makes
pavement slippery, wet or dry.

MACHINES AND POWER: Ash clogs air filters, scratches glass and
paint, and wears out engines and brakes. Wet ash shorts out
electrical equipment, so power cuts are common.

IT COMES BACK: Wind, traffic, and sweeping lift settled ash
into the air again for weeks or longer. A clear day after
ashfall is not an all-clear for your lungs.


PROTECTING YOUR LUNGS
---------------------

Staying inside a closed building is better protection than any
mask. Use a mask when you have to be out in the ash or when you
are cleaning it up.

WHICH MASK (best to worst):
1. A certified N95 mask (called P2, FFP2, or DS2 in other
   countries) that fits your face. The rating is printed on it.
2. A standard pleated surgical mask, if it fits closely. If it
   gapes, it protects much less.
3. Flat non-pleated healthcare masks, hard-cup "nuisance dust"
   masks, and fashion masks: poor filtering or poor fit.
4. Cloth (bandana, T-shirt, scarf): the least protection. More
   layers help a little.

WETTING A CLOTH OR MASK DOES NOT HELP. Current guidance from
the International Volcanic Health Hazard Network (IVHHN), based
on laboratory tests, is that wetting does not improve
filtering. Older advice said otherwise.

FIT MATTERS AS MUCH AS THE FILTER:
- Top strap above the ears, bottom strap below them.
- Press the nose clip to the shape of your nose with both hands.
  Don't pinch it.
- Cover the mask with both hands and breathe out sharply. If you
  feel air at the edges, adjust and try again.
- Beards break the seal. So can glasses or goggle frames: check
  the seal again after you put them on.
- Tying a layer of cloth over a mask can improve the fit. Don't
  make it so tight that breathing is hard.

WHO NEEDS EXTRA CARE:
- People with heart or lung disease should ask a health
  professional whether a tight mask is suitable. Keep reliever
  and preventer medicines within reach and use them as
  prescribed.
- Most masks are too big for children. Keep children indoors,
  and stop them playing in ash or running when ash is in the
  air.
- Don't sleep in a mask. It won't stay sealed.

HOW LONG A MASK LASTS: Disposable masks can be worn until they
are clogged, harder to breathe through, or falling apart.
Between uses, keep the mask in a clean bag or box.


PROTECTING YOUR EYES AND SKIN
-----------------------------

- DON'T WEAR CONTACT LENSES. Ash trapped behind a lens scratches
  the eye. Wear glasses.
- For cleanup, wear goggles without side vents.
- Wear long sleeves and long pants. Ash irritates some people's
  skin, especially if it is acidic.


INSIDE THE HOUSE
----------------

- Use ONE door as the entry point. Leave ashy shoes and outer
  clothes outside it.
- Wipe ash up gently with damp cloths. An ordinary vacuum blows
  the finest ash back into the room. Use one only if it has a
  high-efficiency (HEPA) filter.
- Don't use fans or a clothes dryer while ash is around. They
  stir it up.
- Cover computers, TVs, and other electronics with plastic. Keep
  them covered until the room is free of ash.
- A closed house must not get dangerously hot. If it does,
  consider leaving for somewhere safer.
- NEVER use unvented gas heaters, patio heaters, barbecues, or
  generators indoors. They make carbon monoxide. See the "Indoor
  Air Quality" and "Generator Safety" guides.
- Expect power cuts. Have flashlights, a battery radio, and
  stored water. The "Air Filtration" guide covers cleaning
  indoor air.


ROOFS
-----

Roof collapse is the most direct way ash kills, and it is rare.
It does happen: in the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo, roofs
loaded with ash collapsed and killed nearly 300 people.

HOW MUCH IS TOO MUCH:
- Collapse usually takes a very thick layer, typically more than
  100 mm (4 inches).
- Wide, flat, or lightly built roofs (carports, sheds, barns,
  halls) are the most vulnerable and can fail at about 100 mm
  or less.
- Wet ash is up to twice as heavy. A common rule of thumb: most
  roofs can't hold more than about 4 inches (10 cm) of WET ash.
- Gutters fail first. A few millimeters of ash can clog them or
  pull them off.

WHAT TO DO:
- In heavy ashfall, don't shelter or park under wide flat roofs.
- DO NOT climb on the roof while ash is falling or in the dark.
  US government advice is to stay off the roof afterward too,
  unless you have guidance or training.
- Clearing a roof is one of the most dangerous jobs after an
  eruption. People have died falling from roofs and ladders and
  falling through weakened roofs. Your weight alone can tip an
  overloaded roof into collapse.
- Do it only if you can do it safely: ash has stopped, it is
  daylight, the ladder is tied off and reaches about 1 meter
  (3 feet) above the roof edge, the pitch is gentle, and someone
  nearby can call for help. Keep everyone out from below. If any
  of that is missing, wait for trained help.
- If you can do it safely, clear the roof before ash gets
  thicker than about 100 to 150 mm (4 to 6 inches).
- Sweep with a broom, from the top down, away from gutters. Step
  where the roof is supported and avoid skylights.
- NEVER hose or soak ash on a roof. The water adds weight and
  the ash sets into a hard crust.
- Wind and rain remove small amounts. Whatever is still there
  after about a week should come off, because ash corrodes
  roofing.


DRIVING
-------

Avoid it. Ash ruins engines, the road is slippery, and every
vehicle raises a cloud that blinds the next driver. After the
1980 Mount St. Helens eruption, drivers plowed into cars they
could not see inside those clouds.

If you truly must drive:
- Go slowly: below 35 mph (55 km/h), and slower in heavy ash.
- Use low-beam headlights.
- Leave a long gap to the vehicle ahead.
- Keep windows up. Turn the air conditioning off or set the
  vents to recirculate.
- Don't run wipers on dry ash. They grind it into the glass. Use
  plenty of washer fluid, or stop and rinse the windshield with
  bottled water and a cloth.
- Expect no lane markings, dead traffic lights, and poor
  braking.
- Carry water, masks, a flashlight, and warm clothes in case you
  are stranded.

Afterward: don't brush dry ash off paint or glass, because it
scratches. Rinse it off with water. Have the oil, oil filter,
and air filter changed sooner than usual, and have brakes
checked after driving in heavy ash.


WATER AND FOOD
--------------

Ash in water looks alarming, but it usually makes water taste
bad (metallic, sour, or bitter) before it makes it dangerous.

- Public supply: follow your water utility's advice.
- Ash in your water: let it settle, then pour off and use the
  clear water.
- Rainwater tank with ash in it: use your stored emergency water
  for drinking and cooking until officials advise otherwise. The
  tank water is fine for other uses.
- Reconnect a roof-fed tank only after the roof and gutters are
  clear of ash.
- Surface water you treat yourself (streams, ponds, open tanks):
  ash makes disinfection less reliable. Let it settle, filter
  it, then disinfect. See the "Water Purification" guide.
- Lots of ash in the supply? Don't run the dishwasher or washing
  machine.
- Cleanup uses huge amounts of water and can drain a town's
  supply. Use it sparingly.
- Store water ahead of time: 1 gallon (about 4 liters) per
  person per day, for at least 3 days and up to a week.
- Garden vegetables are safe to eat after washing.

ANIMALS: Keep pets inside, and brush them before letting them
back in. Give livestock clean feed and clean water. Some ash
carries fluoride that can poison animals grazing on ash-coated
pasture.


CLEANING UP
-----------

Wait until the ash stops falling, and follow your community's
cleanup plan.

WEAR:
[ ] N95 (P2 or FFP2) mask
[ ] Goggles without side vents (no contact lenses)
[ ] Long sleeves, long pants, sturdy shoes, gloves
[ ] Hard hat if working on a roof

WHO SHOULD NOT HELP: children, and people with asthma or other
lung conditions.

DO:
- Dampen ash LIGHTLY with a sprinkler or watering can before you
  move it, so it doesn't fly up.
- Shovel thick layers (more than about 1 cm). Sweep up the
  rest.
- Bag it in heavy-duty plastic bags. Keep each bag light. Ash
  is much heavier than it looks, and strained backs are common.
- Indoors: damp cloths, a damp mop, or a HEPA vacuum. Dab glass
  and screens instead of wiping, because ash scratches.
- Rinse ashy clothes, or shake them out outdoors, before
  washing.
- Replace furnace and air-conditioner filters often for months
  afterward. Wait until the yard is cleared before switching
  the system back on.
- Switch electronics off at the power source before cleaning
  them.
- Follow local instructions for disposal. Ash usually goes to a
  separate collection point, not in household trash.

DON'T:
- Don't soak ash. It cakes into a hard mass.
- Don't dry-sweep indoors or use leaf blowers or compressed air.
- Don't wash ash into gutters, sewers, or storm drains. It
  blocks pipes and damages treatment plants.
- Don't let children play in piled or drifted ash.


ASHFALL KIT
-----------

[ ] N95 (P2/FFP2) masks for each adult, and goggles
[ ] Drinking water: 1 gallon (4 L) per person per day
[ ] Food that needs no cooking, for people and pets
[ ] Extra prescription medicine, including inhalers
[ ] Battery radio, flashlights, spare batteries
[ ] Plastic wrap or sheeting and tape (electronics, drafts)
[ ] Old towels for door thresholds
[ ] Broom, shovel, heavy-duty bags
[ ] Vacuum with HEPA filter and spare bags
[ ] Spare furnace filters and a spare car air filter
[ ] Glasses, if you normally wear contact lenses
[ ] Some cash
[ ] Water, masks, and a blanket in the car


PRACTICE
--------

10-MINUTE DRILL:
Put on an N95 mask and do the seal check: both hands over the
mask, breathe out sharply, feel for leaks at the edges. Now add
your glasses or goggles and check again. Then walk through your
home and count every opening you would have to close when ash
is forecast: windows, pet door, vents, the outdoor-air setting
on the heating or cooling system. Find the switch that turns
that system off. If your roof feeds a rainwater tank, find
where the downpipe disconnects.

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

1. Ash has fallen all afternoon and about 2 inches (5 cm) lies
   on your pitched house roof. Rain is forecast tonight. Your
   neighbor is on his roof with a garden hose and shouts that
   you should do the same before it gets heavier.

   Answer: Stay off the roof and leave the hose alone. Hosing
   adds weight and turns ash into a crust. 5 cm is short of
   what usually collapses a house roof, even wet, while a fall
   in fading light on a slippery surface is a real risk right
   now. Move people and cars out from under any carport, shed,
   or wide flat roof instead, and keep an eye on the depth.
   When the ash has stopped and it is daylight, sweep the roof
   only if you can do it safely, or get help.

2. Your teenager is at a friend's house 5 miles (8 km) away.
   Light ash is falling and the road is gray. You want to fetch
   them before it gets worse. The windshield is already dusty.

   Answer: First ask whether the trip is needed at all. A
   teenager indoors at a friend's house is already sheltered,
   and staying put overnight is safer than two people on an
   ash-covered road. If you really must go: crawl, low beams
   on, long following distance, vents on recirculate. Wash the
   windshield with fluid or bottled water. Don't drag dry ash
   across it with the wipers.

3. You have no N95 masks. A relative tells you to soak a
   bandana and tie it over your face, like they did years ago.
   You are also wearing contact lenses.

   Answer: Skip the soaking. Wetting doesn't improve filtering.
   Cloth is the weakest option, so use the best you have: a
   close-fitting pleated surgical mask beats cloth, and several
   layers of cloth beat one. More important, stay indoors and
   keep trips short. Take the contact lenses out and wear
   glasses.


QUICK REFERENCE
---------------

ASH FALLING:
- Stay indoors. Doors and windows shut.
- Damp towels at doors. Outside-air HVAC off.
- No driving. Pets in. Cars covered.
- Disconnect rainwater tank downpipe.
- Evacuate if officials say so.

GOING OUT:
- N95 / P2 / FFP2, sealed to the face
- Wetting a mask or cloth does NOT help
- Glasses or goggles. NO contact lenses.
- Long sleeves and pants

ROOF:
- Collapse risk above about 100 mm (4 in);
  sooner for flat, wide roofs or wet ash
- Never during ashfall or at night
- Never hose it. Broom only.
- Not safe? Wait for help.

DRIVING (emergency only):
- Slow, low beams, long gap
- No wipers on dry ash
- Vents on recirculate

WATER:
- Let ash settle, use the clear water
- Bad taste comes before danger
- Use stored water if the tank has ash

CLEANUP:
- Mask, goggles, gloves
- Dampen lightly, never soak
- No dry sweeping, no blowers
- Never into drains
- Light bags. Ash is heavy.
