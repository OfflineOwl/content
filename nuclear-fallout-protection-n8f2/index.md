# Nuclear Fallout Protection

> **Get inside. Stay inside. Stay tuned.** After a nuclear explosion you will usually have 10 minutes or more before fallout arrives. Use that time to get into the best shelter you can reach. Do not stop to gather belongings.

## Do This Now

**First 10 minutes or more:** get inside before fallout arrives.

### 1. Get inside now

Get into the nearest sturdy building. **Brick or concrete is best.** Any building is better than staying outside. **A car is not a shelter.**

- **Outside when the blast happens?** Take cover behind anything solid and lie face down to protect exposed skin. When the blast wave has passed, get inside right away.
- **Can't get inside right away?** Cover your nose and mouth with a mask, cloth, or towel.

### 2. Go to the basement or the center of the building

Put as many walls and floors as you can between you and the outside. Stay away from windows, doors, outside walls, and the roof. In a tall building, go to the center of a middle floor.

**Best:**

- Basement (deeper and more central is better)
- Center of a large brick or concrete building
- Center of the middle floors of a tall building
- Underground parking garage, subway, or tunnel

**Poor:**

- Vehicles
- Single-story wood house without a basement
- Rooms by windows or outside walls
- Top floor (fallout lands on the roof)

```
          ROOF: AVOID
    (fallout lands on it)
 +---------------------------+
 | AVOID |   CENTER  | AVOID |
 | window|   better  | window|
 | wall  |           | wall  |
 +---------------------------+
 | BASEMENT: BEST PROTECTION |
 +---------------------------+
```

Basements and the center of the building protect best. Stay away from windows, outer walls, and the roof.

### 3. If you were outside, take off your outer clothing

Do it **before** you join others. Taking off your outer layer can remove **up to 90%** of the radioactive material. Bag it, then wash. See Decontamination below.

### 4. Close up the building

- Close and lock windows and doors
- Close fireplace dampers
- Turn off fans, air conditioners, and forced-air heating that pull in outside air

**You do not need to tape or seal the room.** Heavy materials and distance protect you from fallout; airtightness doesn't. Never delay getting to the basement or center to tape windows. (Sealing a room with plastic is for **chemical** releases, and that uses an above-ground room. See the Sealing a Room guide.)

### 5. Stay inside at least 24 hours

Stay sheltered for **at least 24 hours**, unless officials tell you otherwise or there is an immediate danger such as fire, a gas leak, or collapse.

### 6. Stay tuned

Use a battery or hand-crank radio, phone alerts, or TV, and follow official instructions. If you're told to evacuate, do it, and don't come back until officials say it's safe.

> **Don't go out for family.** Bring pets inside, but **do not go outside to collect family members.** Schools, daycares, hospitals, and nursing homes have their own shelter plans. Going out exposes you and them.

**Call 911** (or your local emergency number) for serious injuries, burns, fire, or someone not breathing. Networks may be jammed: keep trying for emergencies, and text family instead of calling.

## Where to Shelter: Protection Factors

Fallout settles on the ground and on roofs, and its radiation passes through walls. The more concrete, brick, or earth and the more distance between you and the outside, the safer you are.

The **Protection Factor (PF)** shows how much a spot cuts your dose. PF 10 means you get one-tenth of the dose you would get outside. FEMA counts **PF 10 or more** as adequate shelter. Values below are from FEMA's nuclear detonation planning guidance (Figure 24).

| Where you are | About PF |
|---------------|----------|
| Outside | 1 |
| Car | Very low |
| 1-story wood-frame house, main floor | 2-3 |
| 2-story brick-veneer house, main floor | 3 |
| 3-story brick/concrete building, middle floor | 7 |
| 1-story wood-frame house, basement | 10 |
| Large office/apartment building, top or ground floor | about 10 |
| 2-story brick-veneer house, basement | 20 |
| 5-story brick/concrete building, middle floor | 20 |
| 3-story brick/concrete building, basement | 50 |
| Large office/apartment building, center of the middle floors | 50-100 |
| 5-story brick/concrete building, basement | 100-200 |
| Large office/apartment building, underground | 200 |

A **car** gives very little protection and is not an adequate shelter.

**If your shelter is poor** (a car, or a wood house with no basement) and a much better building is within about 15 minutes, FEMA's planning guidance says to move there early, within roughly the first 30 minutes after the explosion, then stay put. If no better shelter is close, stay where you are, in the most central spot.

## How Fallout Decays: The 7-10 Rule

Fallout is most dangerous right after it arrives. It gives off over half of its energy in the first hour, then drops fast. **Rule of thumb:** every time the time since the explosion multiplies by 7, the radiation level drops to about one-tenth.

| Time after explosion | Radiation level (vs. 1 hour after) |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| 1 hour | 100% (most dangerous) |
| 7 hours | about 10% |
| 2 days (49 hours) | about 1% |
| 2 weeks | about 0.1% |

This is an estimate. Real levels depend on the weapon, the weather, and where fallout lands. Only officials with radiation meters can tell you an area is safe. Lower levels later on may make it safer to leave, not necessarily safe to stay outside.

## If You Must Move or Go Outside

Leave shelter early only for an immediate danger (fire, gas leak, collapse, a medical emergency) or when officials tell you to.

1. Spend as little time outside as possible
2. Cover your skin and hair; cover your nose and mouth with a mask or cloth
3. Don't touch or pick up things outside
4. Before going back in, take off your outer layer of clothing and shoes, bag them, and wash
5. Wipe sealed containers you bring in with a damp cloth

---

## Decontamination: Cleaning Up

Do this as soon as you get inside, before joining others. **Tap water is fine for washing** even if officials say not to drink it.

1. **Take off your outer layer** of clothing and shoes carefully, without shaking it, so dust doesn't get into the air. This can remove up to 90% of the radioactive material.
2. **Bag it.** Seal clothing in a plastic bag or container, away from people and pets.
3. **Shower** with warm water and lots of soap. **Do not scald, scrub, or scratch your skin**; broken skin lets contamination in. Keep cuts and scrapes covered.
4. **Wash your hair** with shampoo or soap. **Do not use conditioner**; it makes radioactive material stick to hair.
5. **No shower?** Wash hands, face, and uncovered skin at a sink. No sink? Use a moist wipe, clean wet cloth, or damp paper towel.
6. **Gently blow your nose**, then wipe your eyelids, eyelashes, and ears with a moist wipe or wet cloth.
7. **Put on clean clothes** that were stored inside. If you have none, shake or brush off your clothes, put them back on, and rewash hands, face, and exposed skin.
8. **Bag used wipes and towels** and keep them away from people and pets.

**Don't** use household cleaning wipes on skin, **don't** rely on hand sanitizer (it does not remove radioactive material), and **don't** touch your eyes, nose, or mouth until you've washed.

**Helping others:** wear waterproof gloves and a dust mask if you have them, keep their cuts covered, then rewash your own hands, face, and exposed skin.

## Potassium Iodide (KI)

- Take KI **only** if public health or emergency officials (or your doctor) tell you to.
- KI protects **only the thyroid**, and only from radioactive iodine. It does not protect the rest of your body or from other radioactive materials. Getting inside protects you far more.
- Infants, children, and pregnant or breastfeeding women benefit most. Adults over 40 should take it only when officials recommend it.
- **Never take more than directed.** Too much can cause severe illness or death.
- Table salt and iodine-rich foods are **not** substitutes.

## Food and Water

| Food or water | Safe to use? |
|---------------|--------------|
| Food and drinks that were inside the building | Yes |
| Bottled water and sealed drinks | Yes |
| Sealed containers that were outside | Yes, after wiping with a damp cloth (bag the cloth) |
| Tap water | Fine for washing; for drinking, follow official instructions |
| Garden produce, or food or water left uncovered outside | No, not until officials say it's safe |

Never ration drinking water unless officials tell you to.

---

## Pets

- Bring pets and service animals inside.
- If a pet was outside after fallout arrived: wear gloves if you can, gently brush its coat, then wash it with shampoo or soap and water and rinse well. Wash your own hands and face afterward.
- Sealed pet food is safe. Wipe containers before opening.

---

## Staying Informed

- **AM/FM radio** is the most reliable source. Keep a battery or hand-crank radio and spare batteries.
- **Wireless Emergency Alerts** on phones, **NOAA Weather Radio**, TV, and the internet if they work.
- Officials will tell you where to go for contamination screening and medical care.

**Do not** leave shelter to look for information. Use the radio.

---

## Special Situations

- **Children:** keep them away from windows and in the most central spot. Decontaminate them the same way, and help them wash so they don't rub their eyes or mouth. Keep them busy and reassured.
- **Older adults, people with disabilities or medical needs:** move them to the best spot first. Keep medications, glasses, hearing aids, and spare batteries for medical devices with you. Plan ahead for power-dependent equipment.
- **Sick or hurt:** call 911 for life-threatening emergencies. Otherwise follow official instructions on where to get care, and tell shelter staff.

---

## Signs of Radiation Sickness

Only a large dose in a short time causes radiation sickness. Early signs can start within minutes to days: **nausea, vomiting, headache, and diarrhea.** Skin redness or burns may appear within hours or be delayed for days. People often feel better for a while before getting sick again (tiredness, fever, loss of appetite).

Seek medical care as soon as officials say it is safe to travel. Call 911 for emergencies.

## Prepare Now

- [ ] Pick the best shelter spot at home, work, and school (basement or the center of a large building)
- [ ] Learn the shelter plans of your children's schools, care facilities, and your workplace
- [ ] Kit: water (1 gallon per person per day for several days), sealed food, medications, a battery or hand-crank radio, spare batteries, flashlight, first aid kit, a spare set of clothes for each person, and trash bags for contaminated clothing
- [ ] Make a family communication plan with an out-of-town contact and a meeting place
- [ ] Make sure your phone receives Wireless Emergency Alerts, and sign up for local alerts
- [ ] Near a nuclear power plant? Ask your local emergency management office about its plan and KI

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Pick your spot. Walk through your home and choose the most protective place in it: the basement if you have one, otherwise the most central room, away from windows, outside walls, and the roof. Stand in it together so everyone knows where it is. Then name the best spot at work and at school (a basement, or the center of a large brick or concrete building). Finish by checking that your battery or hand-crank radio works, and put a trash bag and a spare set of clothes for each person near the door you would come in by.

**What would you do?**

1. *There is a bright flash, and a while later a distant boom. You are at home in a brick house with a basement. Your children are at school, a ten-minute drive away, and every instinct says go and get them.*

   **Answer:** Stay inside. Schools have their own shelter plans, and going out exposes you and them to fallout. Go to the basement, close windows, doors, and dampers, turn off fans and forced-air heating, and bring pets in. Stay at least 24 hours unless officials say otherwise, listen to the radio, and text instead of calling.

2. *You are in slow traffic on a city street when the explosion happens, a long way off. The car is undamaged, the windows are up, and home is 40 minutes away.*

   **Answer:** Don't sit in the car and don't try to drive home. A car is not a shelter. You usually have 10 minutes or more before fallout arrives, so use them to get into the nearest sturdy building, brick or concrete if you can, and go to the basement or the center, away from windows and the roof. If fallout may have started before you got inside, take off your outer layer and bag it before joining others, then wash.

3. *You have been in the basement for eight hours. Nothing seems to be falling outside, the street looks normal, and you want to walk to the car for the phone charger and some snacks.*

   **Answer:** Stay put. How the street looks is not a measurement. By the 7-10 rule the level at 7 hours is about a tenth of what it was at one hour: lower, but only officials with radiation meters can tell you an area is safe. Stay inside at least 24 hours unless officials say otherwise or there is an immediate danger such as fire, a gas leak, or collapse. Food and drinks that were inside the building are safe to use.
