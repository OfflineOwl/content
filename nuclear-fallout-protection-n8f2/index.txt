NUCLEAR FALLOUT PROTECTION
==========================

GET INSIDE. STAY INSIDE. STAY TUNED. After a nuclear explosion
you will usually have 10 minutes or more before fallout arrives.
Use that time to get into the best shelter you can reach. Do not
stop to gather belongings.


DO THIS NOW
-----------

FIRST 10 MINUTES OR MORE: get inside before fallout arrives.

1. GET INSIDE NOW. Get into the nearest sturdy building. Brick or
   concrete is best. Any building is better than staying outside.
   A car is NOT a shelter.
   - Outside when the blast happens? Take cover behind anything
     solid and lie face down to protect exposed skin. When the
     blast wave has passed, get inside right away.
   - Can't get inside right away? Cover your nose and mouth with
     a mask, cloth, or towel.

2. GO TO THE BASEMENT OR THE CENTER OF THE BUILDING. Put as many
   walls and floors as you can between you and the outside. Stay
   away from windows, doors, outside walls, and the roof. In a
   tall building, go to the center of a middle floor.

   BEST:
   + Basement (deeper and more central is better)
   + Center of a large brick or concrete building
   + Center of the middle floors of a tall building
   + Underground parking garage, subway, or tunnel

   POOR:
   - Vehicles
   - Single-story wood house without a basement
   - Rooms by windows or outside walls
   - Top floor (fallout lands on the roof)

             ROOF: AVOID
        (fallout lands on it)
    +---------------------------+
    | AVOID |   CENTER  | AVOID |
    | window|   better  | window|
    | wall  |           | wall  |
    +---------------------------+
    | BASEMENT: BEST PROTECTION |
    +---------------------------+

   Basements and the center of the building protect best. Stay
   away from windows, outer walls, and the roof.

3. IF YOU WERE OUTSIDE, TAKE OFF YOUR OUTER CLOTHING. Do it
   before you join others. Taking off your outer layer can remove
   up to 90% of the radioactive material. Bag it, then wash. See
   DECONTAMINATION below.

4. CLOSE UP THE BUILDING.
   - Close and lock windows and doors.
   - Close fireplace dampers.
   - Turn off fans, air conditioners, and forced-air heating that
     pull in outside air.
   You do NOT need to tape or seal the room. Heavy materials and
   distance protect you from fallout; airtightness doesn't. Never
   delay getting to the basement or center to tape windows.
   (Sealing a room with plastic is for CHEMICAL releases, and
   that uses an above-ground room. See the Sealing a Room guide.)

5. STAY INSIDE AT LEAST 24 HOURS, unless officials tell you
   otherwise or there is an immediate danger such as fire, a gas
   leak, or collapse.

6. STAY TUNED. Use a battery or hand-crank radio, phone alerts,
   or TV, and follow official instructions. If you're told to
   evacuate, do it, and don't come back until officials say it's
   safe.

DON'T GO OUT FOR FAMILY. Bring pets inside, but do NOT go outside
to collect family members. Schools, daycares, hospitals, and
nursing homes have their own shelter plans. Going out exposes you
and them.

CALL 911 (or your local emergency number) for serious injuries,
burns, fire, or someone not breathing. Networks may be jammed:
keep trying for emergencies, and text family instead of calling.


WHERE TO SHELTER: PROTECTION FACTORS
------------------------------------

Fallout settles on the ground and on roofs, and its radiation
passes through walls. The more concrete, brick, or earth and the
more distance between you and the outside, the safer you are.

The PROTECTION FACTOR (PF) shows how much a spot cuts your dose.
PF 10 means you get one-tenth of the dose you would get outside.
FEMA counts PF 10 or more as adequate shelter. Values below are
from FEMA's nuclear detonation planning guidance (Figure 24).

Car                                               | Very low
1-story wood-frame house, main floor              | 2-3
2-story brick-veneer house, main floor            | 3
3-story brick/concrete building, middle floor     | 7
1-story wood-frame house, basement                | 10
Large office/apartment building, top or ground    | about 10
  floor                                           |
2-story brick-veneer house, basement              | 20
5-story brick/concrete building, middle floor     | 20
3-story brick/concrete building, basement         | 50
Large office/apartment building, center of the    | 50-100
  middle floors                                   |
5-story brick/concrete building, basement         | 100-200
Large office/apartment building, underground      | 200

A car gives very little protection and is not an adequate
shelter.

IF YOUR SHELTER IS POOR (a car, or a wood house with no basement)
and a much better building is within about 15 minutes, FEMA's
planning guidance says to move there early, within roughly the
first 30 minutes after the explosion, then stay put. If no better
shelter is close, stay where you are, in the most central spot.


HOW FALLOUT DECAYS: THE 7-10 RULE
---------------------------------

Fallout is most dangerous right after it arrives. It gives off
over half of its energy in the first hour, then drops fast.

RULE OF THUMB: every time the time since the explosion multiplies
by 7, the radiation level drops to about one-tenth.

Time after explosion   | Radiation level (vs. 1 hour after)
-----------------------|-----------------------------------
1 hour                 | 100% (most dangerous)
7 hours                | about 10%
2 days (49 hours)      | about 1%
2 weeks                | about 0.1%

This is an estimate. Real levels depend on the weapon, the
weather, and where fallout lands. Only officials with radiation
meters can tell you an area is safe. Lower levels later on may
make it safer to leave, not necessarily safe to stay outside.


IF YOU MUST MOVE OR GO OUTSIDE
------------------------------

Leave shelter early only for an immediate danger (fire, gas leak,
collapse, a medical emergency) or when officials tell you to.

1. Spend as little time outside as possible.
2. Cover your skin and hair. Cover your nose and mouth with a
   mask or cloth.
3. Don't touch or pick up things outside.
4. Before going back in, take off your outer layer of clothing
   and shoes, bag them, and wash.
5. Wipe sealed containers you bring in with a damp cloth.


DECONTAMINATION: CLEANING UP
----------------------------

Do this as soon as you get inside, before joining others. Tap
water is fine for washing even if officials say not to drink it.

1. TAKE OFF YOUR OUTER LAYER of clothing and shoes carefully,
   without shaking it, so dust doesn't get into the air. This
   can remove up to 90% of the radioactive material.
2. BAG IT. Seal clothing in a plastic bag or container, away from
   people and pets.
3. SHOWER with warm water and lots of soap. Do NOT scald, scrub,
   or scratch your skin; broken skin lets contamination in. Keep
   cuts and scrapes covered.
4. WASH YOUR HAIR with shampoo or soap. Do NOT use conditioner;
   it makes radioactive material stick to hair.
5. NO SHOWER? Wash hands, face, and uncovered skin at a sink. No
   sink? Use a moist wipe, clean wet cloth, or damp paper towel.
6. GENTLY BLOW YOUR NOSE, then wipe your eyelids, eyelashes, and
   ears with a moist wipe or wet cloth.
7. PUT ON CLEAN CLOTHES that were stored inside. If you have
   none, shake or brush off your clothes, put them back on, and
   rewash hands, face, and exposed skin.
8. BAG USED WIPES AND TOWELS and keep them away from people and
   pets.

DON'T use household cleaning wipes on skin, DON'T rely on hand
sanitizer (it does not remove radioactive material), and DON'T
touch your eyes, nose, or mouth until you've washed.

HELPING OTHERS: wear waterproof gloves and a dust mask if you
have them, keep their cuts covered, then rewash your own hands,
face, and exposed skin.


POTASSIUM IODIDE (KI)
---------------------

- Take KI ONLY if public health or emergency officials (or your
  doctor) tell you to.
- KI protects ONLY the thyroid, and only from radioactive iodine.
  It does not protect the rest of your body or from other
  radioactive materials. Getting inside protects you far more.
- Infants, children, and pregnant or breastfeeding women benefit
  most. Adults over 40 should take it only when officials
  recommend it.
- NEVER take more than directed. Too much can cause severe
  illness or death.
- Table salt and iodine-rich foods are NOT substitutes.


FOOD AND WATER
--------------

SAFE:
+ Food and drinks that were inside the building.
+ Bottled water and sealed drinks.
+ Sealed containers that were outside, after wiping them with a
  damp cloth. Bag the cloth.

TAP WATER: fine for washing. For drinking, follow official
instructions.

NOT SAFE until officials say so:
- Garden produce, or food or water left uncovered outside.

Never ration drinking water unless officials tell you to.


PETS
----

- Bring pets and service animals inside.
- If a pet was outside after fallout arrived: wear gloves if you
  can, gently brush its coat, then wash it with shampoo or soap
  and water and rinse well. Wash your own hands and face
  afterward.
- Sealed pet food is safe. Wipe containers before opening.


STAYING INFORMED
----------------

- AM/FM RADIO is the most reliable source. Keep a battery or
  hand-crank radio and spare batteries.
- Wireless Emergency Alerts on phones, NOAA Weather Radio, TV,
  and the internet if they work.
- Officials will tell you where to go for contamination screening
  and medical care.

Do NOT leave shelter to look for information. Use the radio.


SPECIAL SITUATIONS
------------------

- CHILDREN: keep them away from windows and in the most central
  spot. Decontaminate them the same way, and help them wash so
  they don't rub their eyes or mouth. Keep them busy and
  reassured.
- OLDER ADULTS, PEOPLE WITH DISABILITIES OR MEDICAL NEEDS: move
  them to the best spot first. Keep medications, glasses, hearing
  aids, and spare batteries for medical devices with you. Plan
  ahead for power-dependent equipment.
- SICK OR HURT: call 911 for life-threatening emergencies.
  Otherwise follow official instructions on where to get care,
  and tell shelter staff.


SIGNS OF RADIATION SICKNESS
---------------------------

Only a large dose in a short time causes radiation sickness.
Early signs can start within minutes to days: nausea, vomiting,
headache, and diarrhea. Skin redness or burns may appear within
hours or be delayed for days. People often feel better for a
while before getting sick again (tiredness, fever, loss of
appetite).

Seek medical care as soon as officials say it is safe to travel.
Call 911 for emergencies.


PREPARE NOW
-----------

[ ] Pick the best shelter spot at home, work, and school
    (basement or the center of a large building)
[ ] Learn the shelter plans of your children's schools, care
    facilities, and your workplace
[ ] Kit: water (1 gallon per person per day for several days),
    sealed food, medications, a battery or hand-crank radio,
    spare batteries, flashlight, first aid kit, a spare set of
    clothes for each person, and trash bags for contaminated
    clothing
[ ] Make a family communication plan with an out-of-town contact
    and a meeting place
[ ] Make sure your phone receives Wireless Emergency Alerts, and
    sign up for local alerts
[ ] Near a nuclear power plant? Ask your local emergency
    management office about its plan and KI


PRACTICE
--------

10-MINUTE DRILL: Pick your spot. Walk through your home and
choose the most protective place in it: the basement if you have
one, otherwise the most central room, away from windows, outside
walls, and the roof. Stand in it together so everyone knows where
it is. Then name the best spot at work and at school (a basement,
or the center of a large brick or concrete building). Finish by
checking that your battery or hand-crank radio works, and put a
trash bag and a spare set of clothes for each person near the
door you would come in by.

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

1. There is a bright flash, and a while later a distant boom. You
   are at home in a brick house with a basement. Your children
   are at school, a ten-minute drive away, and every instinct
   says go and get them.

   Answer: Stay inside. Schools have their own shelter plans, and
   going out exposes you and them to fallout. Go to the basement,
   close windows, doors, and dampers, turn off fans and
   forced-air heating, and bring pets in. Stay at least 24 hours
   unless officials say otherwise, listen to the radio, and text
   instead of calling.

2. You are in slow traffic on a city street when the explosion
   happens, a long way off. The car is undamaged, the windows are
   up, and home is 40 minutes away.

   Answer: Don't sit in the car and don't try to drive home. A
   car is not a shelter. You usually have 10 minutes or more
   before fallout arrives, so use them to get into the nearest
   sturdy building, brick or concrete if you can, and go to the
   basement or the center, away from windows and the roof. If
   fallout may have started before you got inside, take off your
   outer layer and bag it before joining others, then wash.

3. You have been in the basement for eight hours. Nothing seems
   to be falling outside, the street looks normal, and you want
   to walk to the car for the phone charger and some snacks.

   Answer: Stay put. How the street looks is not a measurement.
   By the 7-10 rule the level at 7 hours is about a tenth of what
   it was at one hour: lower, but only officials with radiation
   meters can tell you an area is safe. Stay inside at least 24
   hours unless officials say otherwise or there is an immediate
   danger such as fire, a gas leak, or collapse. Food and drinks
   that were inside the building are safe to use.
