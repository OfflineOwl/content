# Infection Prevention Without Modern Healthcare

> When clinics are closed and antibiotics are scarce, preventing infection is far easier than treating it. Clean hands, safe water, clean wounds, and keeping human waste away from food and water prevent most infections.

## Do this now

If you're in an outage or disaster right now, these habits matter most:

1. **Wash hands with soap and clean water for at least 20 seconds:** before preparing food or eating, before and after touching a wound, after the toilet or changing a diaper, and after handling garbage or caring for someone sick. No soap and water? Use hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol.
2. **Drink only safe water.** If you aren't sure it's safe, bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 ft / 1,500 m) and let it cool. Boiling doesn't remove fuel or chemicals.
3. **Clean every wound right away** with soap and clean water, cover it, and check it every day.
4. **Keep human waste away from water and food.** Use a lined bucket toilet or a latrine well away from water sources, and wash hands every time.
5. **Separate sick people** from everyone else if you can, and have someone who isn't at high risk care for them.
6. **Throw out risky food.** Perishable food that has been above 40°F (4°C) for more than 2 hours (1 hour if it's above 90°F / 32°C) isn't safe, even if it looks and smells fine.

## Get medical help urgently if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) or get to care by any means if someone has:

- **Possible sepsis:** an infection plus any of these: confusion or disorientation, shortness of breath, high heart rate or weak pulse, fever with shivering or feeling very cold, extreme pain, or clammy, sweaty skin. Sepsis is a medical emergency.
- **Red streaks** spreading from a wound, redness that's growing fast, or a wound with fever.
- **A dirty or deep wound** (soil, rust, puncture, crush) and no tetanus shot in the last 5 years.
- **An animal bite or scratch**, especially from a wild animal, stray, or bat. Wash it right away with soap and water for 15 minutes, then get medical advice urgently. Rabies is preventable with prompt treatment, but people who need the vaccine and don't get it before symptoms start will most likely die.
- **Diarrhea or vomiting with signs of dehydration** (very little urine, dizziness, confusion), bloody diarrhea, or a baby or older adult who can't keep fluids down.
- **Trouble breathing.**

## How Infections Spread

| Route | Examples | Main prevention |
|-------|----------|-----------------|
| **Contact** | Wound infections, skin infections | Hand hygiene, wound care, gloves |
| **Droplet** | Flu, colds, COVID-19 | Distance, ventilation, masks, covering coughs |
| **Airborne** | Measles, tuberculosis | Ventilation, respirators, separating the sick |
| **Fecal-oral** | Cholera, norovirus, hepatitis A | Handwashing, safe water, safe food, sanitation |
| **Vector** | Mosquito- and tick-borne diseases | Repellent, long sleeves, removing standing water |
| **Blood** | Hepatitis B, HIV | Gloves, avoiding contact with blood, safe sharps handling |

Risk goes up with crowding, poor sanitation, lack of clean water, open wounds, poor nutrition, exhaustion, and weakened immune systems (infants, older adults, pregnant people, people with chronic illness).

## Hand Hygiene: The Most Important Habit

### When to wash

- Before, during, and after preparing food
- Before eating
- Before and after caring for someone who is sick or treating a wound
- After using the toilet, changing diapers, or cleaning up after a child
- After blowing your nose, coughing, or sneezing
- After touching animals, animal food, or animal waste
- After touching garbage

### How to wash (CDC method)

1. **Wet** your hands with clean running water (warm or cold), turn off the tap, and apply soap.
2. **Lather** the backs of your hands, between your fingers, and under your nails.
3. **Scrub** for at least 20 seconds (hum "Happy Birthday" twice).
4. **Rinse** under clean running water.
5. **Dry** with a clean towel or air dry.

**No running water?** Have someone pour clean water over your hands, or use a container with a spigot, so you rinse under flowing water. Don't share a basin of standing water. Any washing with soap is better than none.

### Hand sanitizer

- Use one with **at least 60% alcohol.**
- Cover all surfaces of both hands and rub until dry. Don't wipe it off.
- **Sanitizer doesn't work well on visibly dirty or greasy hands** and doesn't remove chemicals. Wash with soap and water when you can.
- Sanitizer doesn't get rid of every germ. Soap and water work better against norovirus, *Cryptosporidium*, and *C. difficile*, all causes of diarrhea.
- Supervise young children using sanitizer so they don't swallow the alcohol.

### Homemade sanitizer: don't count on it

The CDC does not recommend making your own hand sanitizer. Home mixes easily end up below the 60% alcohol needed to work (for example, a 2:1 mix of 91% rubbing alcohol and aloe gel lands right at about 60%, and below it with 70% alcohol or any measuring error). Plain soap and water works. Stock commercial sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol instead.

## Wound Care to Prevent Infection

Clean wounds as soon as you can. Even imperfect cleaning helps.

1. **Wash your hands** and put on gloves if you have them.
2. **Stop the bleeding first** with firm, direct pressure.
3. **Rinse the wound** with plenty of clean running water (safe drinking water, or boiled and cooled) until you can't see any dirt. Plain water is better for this than antiseptics like iodine. Squeezing a clean bottle gives a stream that flushes out dirt.
4. **Remove visible dirt** and splinters with tweezers cleaned with alcohol. Don't dig deep.
5. **Wash the skin around the wound** with soap.
6. **Cover it** with a clean dressing. A thin layer of petroleum jelly or antibiotic ointment can keep a minor wound moist.
7. **Change the dressing** daily, or whenever it's wet or dirty, and look at the wound each time.

Keep wounds out of floodwater; cover them with a waterproof bandage if you have to go in. Have animal or human bites, deep punctures, and wounds with something stuck in them checked by a clinician.

### Signs of wound infection

Check for these every day after an injury:

- Redness that spreads or grows
- More swelling, warmth, or pain instead of less
- Pus or cloudy drainage, or a bad smell
- Red streaks spreading from the wound
- Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell

### If an infection starts and care isn't available yet

1. Keep cleaning the wound gently 2–3 times a day and use fresh dressings.
2. Apply clean, warm, moist compresses for comfort.
3. Raise the injured part if you can, and rest.
4. Drink plenty of fluids.
5. Mark the edge of the redness with a pen so you can tell if it's spreading.
6. **Don't take leftover or someone else's antibiotics** unless a clinician (in person, by phone, or by radio) tells you to.
7. **Spreading redness, red streaks, fever, or any sign of sepsis means get care now**, by any means available.

### Tetanus

Tetanus germs live in soil, dust, and dirt, and the disease is a medical emergency. After a wound, especially a dirty one, check your tetanus vaccine record. Get a booster if a dirty or deep wound happens more than 5 years after your last dose, or a clean minor wound more than 10 years after. Keep everyone's vaccination dates with your emergency documents.

## Respiratory Infections

### Reduce spread in a shared shelter or home

- **Separate** the sick person in their own room or corner if possible, and keep the door closed.
- **Bring in fresh air:** open windows when weather allows.
- **Cover coughs and sneezes** with a tissue or your elbow, throw the tissue away, and wash hands.
- **Keep your distance** from people who are sick, and don't share cups, utensils, or towels.

### Masks

- **Best protection:** a well-fitting NIOSH-approved respirator such as an N95.
- **Less protection:** disposable surgical masks and cloth masks.
- **Fit matters:** cover nose and mouth with no gaps.
- **The sick person should wear one** around others; it reduces spread.
- Change a mask when it's damp or dirty. Handle it by the straps and wash your hands after taking it off.

## Caring for a Sick Person at Home

**Set up a sick room:**
- Separate room and, if possible, separate bathroom
- A caregiver who isn't at high risk (not an older adult, pregnant, or chronically ill), and as few other visitors as possible
- Lidded, bag-lined trash can, tissues, a water bottle and cup that only they use, thermometer, and their own towel

**Caregiver precautions:**
- Wear a mask in the room and have the sick person wear one when you're close.
- Wear gloves when touching blood, vomit, stool, urine, or soiled linens.
- Wash hands before and after every visit and after removing gloves.
- Wear gloves to handle soiled laundry and wash your hands afterward.

**Watch for danger signs:** trouble breathing, chest pain, confusion, not drinking or urinating, a very sleepy child, or any sign of sepsis.

## Cleaning and Disinfecting

1. **Clean first** with soap or detergent and water to remove dirt. Disinfectants don't work well on dirty surfaces.
2. **Then disinfect** high-touch surfaces (doorknobs, counters, toilet handles, faucets) and anything with body fluids on it.

**Bleach solution:** 5 tablespoons (1/3 cup) of household bleach per gallon of room-temperature water, or 4 teaspoons per quart. Use bleach whose label says 5% to 9% sodium hypochlorite (don't use it if no strength is listed). Leave the surface wet for at least 1 minute, and mix a fresh batch each day.

**Never mix bleach with ammonia or any other cleaner or disinfectant.** It can release toxic gas. Use it with windows open, and wear gloves.

## Food Safety Without Refrigeration

- **The danger zone is 40–140°F (4–60°C).** Bacteria multiply fast in this range.
- **2-hour rule:** discard perishable food left out more than 2 hours, or 1 hour if it's above 90°F (32°C).
- **Power outage:** a closed refrigerator keeps food safe about 4 hours. A full freezer holds about 48 hours (24 hours if half full) if the door stays closed.
- **When in doubt, throw it out.** You can't see, smell, or taste the germs that cause food poisoning.
- Wash hands before handling food, keep raw meat away from ready-to-eat food, and cook meat thoroughly.
- **Throw away food that may have touched floodwater**, and food in packaging that isn't waterproof. Undamaged commercial cans and retort pouches can be saved: remove labels, wash, rinse, then sanitize by soaking 15 minutes in 1 cup of bleach per 5 gallons of water, or boiling for 2 minutes. Throw out bulging, open, or damaged cans.

**Keeps well without refrigeration:** canned goods (until opened), dry rice, pasta, and beans, peanut butter, crackers, nuts, dried fruit, honey (not for babies under 1).

**Spoils quickly:** meat, fish, poultry, milk and soft cheeses, eggs, cooked rice and pasta, cut fruits and vegetables, leftovers.

## Safe Water

- Treat water unless you know it's safe. Boiling is the most reliable way to kill germs: **a rolling boil for 1 minute** (3 minutes above 5,000 ft).
- Let cloudy water settle, or filter it through a clean cloth or coffee filter, before boiling or disinfecting.
- Store treated water in clean, covered containers, and pour from them rather than dipping cups in.
- Boiling and household disinfection **don't** make water safe from fuel, chemicals, or toxic floodwater. Find another source.
- See the Water Purification guide for chemical disinfection and filters.

## Sanitation Without Plumbing

### Bucket toilet

1. Line a 5-gallon bucket (or the toilet bowl) with two garbage bags.
2. After each use, add cat litter, sawdust, peat moss, or dry soil to soak up liquid and cut odor.
3. Keep the lid closed between uses.
4. Tie off full bags and store them in a covered container away from children, pets, wildlife, and water, until they can go out with the garbage or local officials say how to dispose of them.
5. Wash hands every time, and keep a handwashing station next to the toilet.

### Latrine (if you must dig one)

- Place it **at least 30 m (100 feet) from any well, spring, or other water source** (the WHO minimum), downhill from it if possible, and away from where you cook and sleep.
- Cover waste with soil after each use.
- Fill it in with soil before it's full, and dig a new one.

### Garbage

- Keep garbage in closed bags and lidded containers, away from living areas and water, to keep flies and rodents off it.

## Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

### Basic PPE kit

- Nitrile gloves (several sizes, plenty of them)
- N95 respirators and surgical masks
- Eye protection: goggles or a face shield
- Disposable gowns, or large garbage bags with holes cut for head and arms
- Hand sanitizer and soap

### When to use it

| Situation | Gloves | Mask | Eye protection |
|-----------|--------|------|----------------|
| Wound care | Yes | If splashing is possible | If splashing is possible |
| Caring for someone with a respiratory illness | For contact with fluids | Yes | If they're coughing near you |
| Cleaning up blood, vomit, or stool | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Handling garbage or human waste | Yes | Optional | If splashing is possible |

### Taking PPE off safely

The outside is contaminated. The CDC's usual order:

1. **Gloves:** peel off without touching the outside with bare skin.
2. **Goggles or face shield:** handle by the strap or earpieces.
3. **Gown:** pull away from your body, rolling the outside inward.
4. **Mask or respirator:** remove by the straps, without touching the front.
5. **Wash your hands** right away, and between steps if your hands get contaminated.

(The CDC also allows removing the gown and gloves together first, rolling them inside-out.)

## About "Natural" Remedies

Vitamin C, zinc, echinacea, elderberry, garlic, and similar products have not been shown to prevent or treat serious infections, and the evidence even for colds is weak or mixed. They're no substitute for hygiene, safe water, wound care, vaccination, and medical treatment. Never use them in place of care for a spreading or serious infection.

## Daily Prevention Checklist

- [ ] Wash hands at the key times (food, toilet, wounds, sick care)
- [ ] Drink only treated or known-safe water
- [ ] Keep wounds clean and covered; check them daily
- [ ] Eat only food that's been kept safely
- [ ] Keep human waste contained and away from water
- [ ] Keep sick people separated, with one caregiver
- [ ] Clean high-touch surfaces daily
- [ ] Sleep, eat, and drink enough; exhaustion and dehydration weaken defenses
