# Solar Power for Emergencies

> **Sunlight is free. Storing it is the hard part.** A modest panel and battery can keep phones, lights, and a radio running for as long as an outage lasts. The same kit will not run a heater, an oven, or your house. Ten minutes of arithmetic before you buy is the difference between a useful kit and an expensive disappointment.

## Start here

1. **List what you truly need in an outage** and how many watt-hours (Wh) a day it uses. For most households that is phones, a few LED lights, and a radio: well under 200 Wh a day.
2. **Buy the battery first.** A power bank or portable power station is useful with no sun at all: charge it from the wall before a storm. Size it to hold at least two days of your list.
3. **Add a panel sized for your worst month,** using the formula in this guide. December, not July.
4. **Test the whole chain on a sunny day.** Connect the panel, see what the battery reports, and run your real devices from it.
5. **Never connect any of it to your home's wiring or a wall outlet.** Plug devices directly into the battery.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **Someone depends on a powered medical device and the backup is running out.** Do not wait for the sun. Go somewhere with power or call for help while the device still works.
- **A battery, power station, or cable is smoking, burning, hissing, or swelling.** Get everyone out and call from outside. Do not fight a lithium-ion battery fire.
- **Anyone has had an electric shock,** or a power line is down near you. Stay away from the line and anything touching it.

## What a Small Setup Can and Cannot Run

Energy use is watts multiplied by hours. Read the watts from each device's label or manual; the figures below are examples.

| Load | Example | Wh a day | Small solar kit? |
|------|---------|----------|------------------|
| Phone, one full charge | 17 Wh battery | 17 | Yes |
| LED lantern | 3 W for 5 hours | 15 | Yes |
| Radio | 1 W for 2 hours | 2 | Yes |
| Laptop | 60 W for 2 hours | 120 | Yes, with a 100 W panel in good sun |
| Fan | 30 W for 8 hours | 240 | Borderline; needs a larger kit |
| CPAP or other medical device | Ask your supplier | Varies widely | Only with a tested plan (see below) |
| Refrigerator | Label says 500 kWh a year | About 1,400 | No. More than a 1,000 Wh station holds |
| Space heater | 1,500 W | 1,500 every hour | No |
| Electric stove, oven, kettle, hair dryer, air conditioner, well pump, water heater | Hundreds to thousands of watts; check the label | | No |

The pattern: anything that makes heat or cold with electricity is out of reach. A full 1,000 Wh power station runs a 1,500 W space heater for 40 minutes at the very best. The same station can recharge a phone dozens of times.

For a refrigerator, find the yellow EnergyGuide label, take the kWh per year, and divide by 365. A 500 kWh model needs about 1.4 kWh (1,400 Wh) every day. Feeding that from the sun takes several hundred watts of panels plus a large battery, which is a home installation, not an emergency kit. Plan to keep the doors shut instead: see the Food Safety Without Refrigeration guide.

## How Much Will a Panel Really Make?

```
Wh per day = panel watts
             x peak sun hours
             x 0.8
```

**Panel watts** is the nameplate rating. It is measured under 1,000 watts of sunlight per square meter, which is roughly a clear noon with the panel aimed straight at the sun. Most of the day you get less.

**Peak sun hours** squeeze the whole day's sunlight into the equivalent number of hours at that full intensity. They are not the same as hours of daylight. The table shows 30-year averages from the US national solar laboratory for a panel facing south and tilted at the local latitude.

| City | December | June | Year |
|------|----------|------|------|
| Phoenix, AZ | 4.9 | 7.3 | 6.5 |
| Miami, FL | 4.5 | 5.1 | 5.2 |
| Boston, MA | 2.9 | 5.5 | 4.6 |
| Chicago, IL | 2.4 | 5.7 | 4.4 |
| Detroit, MI | 2.1 | 5.6 | 4.2 |
| Seattle, WA | 1.4 | 5.2 | 3.7 |

Look at the spread. A panel in Seattle makes about a quarter as much in December as in June. These are also monthly averages: a run of heavily overcast days delivers far less, and the lowest December average in Seattle's 30-year record was 0.8.

**The 0.8** covers real-world losses. The laboratory's standard calculator assumes about 14% lost to dirt, shading, wiring, and connections, and a further 4% in the inverter, for a combined factor of about 0.83. That is for a well-installed, well-aimed system. A portable panel lying flat, getting hot, or catching a shadow does worse, so treat 0.8 as a ceiling.

**Worked example.** You need 150 Wh a day (two phones, two lanterns, a radio, and an hour and a half of laptop). You own a 100 W folding panel and live near Chicago.

- June: 100 x 5.7 x 0.8 = about 456 Wh a day. Three times what you need.
- December: 100 x 2.4 x 0.8 = about 192 Wh a day on an average day. Barely enough, and a cloudy week will not keep up.

So the December plan needs either a second panel, a smaller list, or a battery big enough to ride through several dark days. To size a panel directly, turn the formula around: panel watts = daily Wh / peak sun hours / 0.8. For 150 Wh in a Chicago December that is 150 / 2.4 / 0.8 = about 78 W, and a wise buyer rounds up by half again.

Outside the US, look up the solar resource for your location; the formula is the same. South of the equator, face the panel north.

## What to Buy

| Piece | What it is | Look for |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| **Power bank** | Pocket battery with USB ports, about 20 to 100 Wh | Capacity in Wh (or mAh x 3.7 / 1,000), a testing-laboratory listing |
| **Portable power station** | Larger battery with AC outlets, USB, and a 12 V port. Often sold as a "solar generator," though it generates nothing by itself | Capacity in Wh; AC output watts higher than your largest device; solar input watts and voltage range; a testing-laboratory listing such as UL |
| **Folding panel** | 20 to 200 W, with USB output or a cable for a power station | Connector and voltage that match your station's solar input; a way to prop it at an angle |

- **Capacity (Wh) is how long. Output (W) is how much at once.** A station with a 300 W outlet cannot start a 1,000 W appliance no matter how much energy it holds.
- **Use the USB and 12 V ports for small devices.** The AC outlets run through an inverter that wastes some energy as heat.
- **A panel wired to a bare battery needs a charge controller.** All-in-one power stations have one built in; follow the manual for which panels are compatible.
- **Tiny built-in panels are a trickle.** One crank radio's manual promises up to 45 minutes of radio per hour of ideal sun on its built-in panel. That is useful for a radio and hopeless for a phone.

**Power station or gas generator?** A power station makes no exhaust, so it is safe to use indoors, is silent, and needs no fuel. It also holds a small, fixed amount of energy. A generator can run a refrigerator or a sump pump for as long as you have fuel, and its exhaust can kill within minutes if it is used in the wrong place. If you own one, read the Generator Safety guide.

## Setting Up the Panel

- **Aim it.** Face the panel true south (in the Northern Hemisphere) and tilt it up from flat by about your latitude. Add 15 degrees in winter, when the sun is low; subtract 15 in summer.
- **Re-aim if you can.** Pointing the panel at the sun mid-morning, at noon, and mid-afternoon collects more than leaving it fixed.
- **No shade, at all.** Even a small shadow can cut output sharply. Watch for shadows that arrive later in the day.
- **Keep it clean** and clear of snow.
- **Panel in the sun, battery in the shade.** Lithium-ion batteries should only be charged between 32°F and 105°F (0°C and 40°C). Run the cable through a window or door and keep the battery indoors or shaded, and warm in winter.
- **Tie it down.** A folding panel is a sail.
- **Charge by day, use by night.** Top up phones and lanterns directly while the sun is out and save the stored energy for the dark.

## Never Backfeed Your House

Do not plug a power station, inverter, or generator into a wall outlet, and do not wire one into your electrical panel yourself. Power pushed into house wiring flows back out to the utility lines, where it can electrocute line workers and neighbors, and it can bypass the circuit protection that keeps your home's wiring from overheating.

- The safe method for a small kit is the simple one: plug devices straight into the battery, using extension cords rated for the load.
- To power household circuits from any backup source, a licensed electrician must install a transfer switch. The Generator Safety guide covers this.
- **Rooftop solar does not help in an outage unless it was built to.** Standard grid-connected systems are designed to shut off for safety when the grid goes down. Only systems with battery storage and the right inverter keep running. If you have rooftop panels, ask your installer which kind you own.

## Battery Care and Safety

Power stations and power banks are lithium-ion batteries, and the fire rules apply: use the maker's charger, charge on a hard surface away from anything that burns, stay present and awake while large packs charge, unplug when full, and stop using any pack that swells, smells, leaks, overheats, or makes noise. Never use or charge one that has been under water. The Battery Management & Rotation guide has the full list.

For long storage, manufacturers generally advise keeping a lithium-ion battery cool and about half charged, and topping it up every six months. For an emergency kit, a pack that is ready matters more than a pack that lasts an extra year: check it every three months and fill it whenever severe weather is forecast.

## Medical Devices

A small solar kit is a comfort item, not life support. If someone in your home relies on an oxygen concentrator, ventilator, powered wheelchair, home dialysis, or refrigerated medicine:

- Ask the equipment supplier how many watt-hours the device uses per day or night, whether it has an internal battery, and whether it can run from 12 V DC.
- Test the device on your backup power for a full night before you need it.
- Decide in advance where you will go if the power is out longer than your backup lasts.

See the Special Needs Preparedness and Managing Chronic Conditions guides.

- [ ] Daily Wh list written down
- [ ] Power bank or power station holding at least two days of that list
- [ ] Panel sized for December sun, with the right cable for your battery
- [ ] Something to prop and tie down the panel
- [ ] Charging cables for every device, plus a 12 V car adapter
- [ ] Extension cord rated for the load
- [ ] Full test run completed on a sunny day
- [ ] Battery checked and topped up every three months

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Do a label hunt. Find the watt rating on five things you would want during an outage (phone charger, lamp, router, laptop charger, fan, CPAP). Multiply each by the hours you would use it in a day and add up the watt-hours. Then find the Wh rating on your power bank or power station and divide: how many days do you really have? If you own a panel, finish by standing where you would set it up at midday and checking for shadows.

**What would you do?**

1. *The outage is on day two. A neighbor offers a cord with a plug on both ends so you can connect your power station to a wall outlet and "light up the whole house."*

   **Answer:** Refuse, and tell them why. That cord backfeeds your wiring and the utility lines, where it can kill a line worker, and the exposed prongs are live in your hand. A power station also cannot carry a house. Plug the few things that matter directly into it.

2. *It is the third overcast day of an ice-storm outage. Your 500 Wh station reads 30% and the panel is producing almost nothing. The freezer is starting to soften, and the kids want to charge a tablet for a movie.*

   **Answer:** You have about 150 Wh left: enough for phones, lights, and the radio for a day or two, and nowhere near enough to save a freezer. Spend it on communication and light. Keep the freezer shut (a full one holds its temperature for about 48 hours with the door closed) and cook what thaws; the Food Safety Without Refrigeration guide covers what to keep and what to throw out. Clear ice and snow off the panel and re-aim it. The movie waits for sun.

3. *The power is out on a freezing night and the house is cold. Your 1,000 Wh power station is full, and there is a 1,500 W space heater in the closet.*

   **Answer:** Leave the heater in the closet. It would drain the station in 40 minutes or less and leave you cold, dark, and unable to charge a phone. Put everyone in one small room, layer clothing and blankets, and keep the battery for light and communication. If the cold is becoming dangerous, go to a warming center or a home with power. See the Emergency Heating and Extreme Cold guides.
