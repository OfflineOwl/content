# Emergency Heating Without Power

> **Stay warm without poisoning the air.** In winter outages, people die from the cold, and from the carbon monoxide (CO) and fires caused by unsafe heating. Keep one room warm, dress in layers, and never bring outdoor fuel-burning equipment inside.

## Do this now

1. **Never heat your home with an oven or stove, a grill, a camp stove, a generator, or any outdoor heater.** Fuel-burning ones make deadly carbon monoxide, and all of them can start fires.
2. **Make sure you have working CO and smoke alarms** before using any fuel-burning heat. If a CO alarm sounds or anyone gets a headache, dizziness, nausea, or confusion, **get everyone outside and call 911**.
3. **Pick one small room to keep warm.** Close it off, cover windows, block drafts, and gather everyone there.
4. **Dress in loose layers**, including a hat, gloves, and warm socks, and use sleeping bags and blankets.
5. **If you can't keep your home warm, stay somewhere else** (a warming center, shelter, or friends with heat), especially with babies, older adults, or anyone who is ill. Babies under 1 year old should never sleep in a cold room.

## The Deadly Mistakes

These produce carbon monoxide or start fires. **Never use them indoors**, including in a garage, basement, tent, or camper, even with windows open:

- Ovens or stovetops used for heat (gas or electric)
- Charcoal or gas grills, including charcoal burned in a fireplace
- Camp stoves, lanterns, and outdoor propane heaters (patio heaters, "tank-top" heaters)
- Generators (outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the house)
- A car running in an attached garage, even with the garage door open

**CO is colorless and odorless.** Symptoms are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and confusion, often mistaken for the flu. If more than one person feels sick at once, suspect CO. People who are asleep can die without waking up.

## Safer Heating Options

### Fireplace

- Have the chimney inspected and cleaned every year. Don't use a fireplace that hasn't been used or checked in years until it's inspected; blocked or cracked chimneys cause fires and CO buildup.
- Open the damper before lighting.
- Burn only dry, seasoned wood. Never burn trash, cardboard, wrapping paper, or charcoal, and never use gasoline or other flammable liquids to start a fire.
- Use a sturdy screen or glass doors to keep sparks in.
- Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet away.
- Don't leave a fire burning when you leave the room or go to sleep.
- Keep the damper open until the ashes are cool. Then put the ashes in a metal container with a tight lid, wet them down, and keep the container outside at least 10 feet from your home. Never dump ashes into a trash can; they can stay hot for days.

Open fireplaces lose a lot of heat up the chimney. Close off the room and sit close to the fire to get the most warmth.

### Wood or Pellet Stove

- Use only stoves that are properly installed and vented to the outside.
- Keep the area around it clear of anything that can burn, at least 3 feet.
- Same rules as fireplaces: annual chimney inspection, dry wood, ashes in a lidded metal container outside.

### Electric Space Heaters (If You Have Power or a Generator)

Space heaters are involved in most home heating fire deaths. Use them carefully:

- **Plug directly into a wall outlet.** Never use an extension cord or power strip, and don't plug anything else into the same outlet. Don't run the cord under a rug.
- Keep at least **3 feet** of clear space around it. Keep it away from curtains, bedding, furniture, and paper.
- Choose a heater with **automatic shutoff** (turns off if tipped over or overheating), listed by a recognized testing lab such as UL, ETL, or CSA.
- Put it on a hard, level floor, not on a rug, bed, or table.
- **Turn it off when you leave the room or go to sleep.**
- Keep children and pets away.
- If running it from a generator, plug the heater into the generator with a heavy-duty outdoor cord rated for the load. Space heaters draw a lot of power.

### Fuel-Burning Indoor Heaters (Kerosene or Propane)

Unvented (ventless) kerosene and propane heaters release their exhaust into the room. Even models with an oxygen depletion sensor still give off carbon monoxide and other gases. Use them **only for emergency heat**, only if the manufacturer says the model is for indoor use, and follow every instruction on the label:

- Never bring a patio heater or outdoor "tank-top" propane heater inside.
- For kerosene heaters, use **only water-clear 1-K grade kerosene**. Never use gasoline.
- **Refuel only outdoors, after the heater is off and cool.**
- **Give it fresh air:** keep the door to the room open, or open a window about an inch.
- **Don't sleep in a room with an unvented heater running**, and turn it off when you leave the room.
- Keep 3 feet of space around it.
- **Have a working CO alarm nearby.**
- Portable kerosene heaters are illegal in some communities. Check local rules.

## Keep One Room Warm

### Set Up a Warm Room

1. Choose a small room with few windows and no exterior doors, if possible.
2. Close the doors to the rest of the house.
3. Block gaps under doors with rolled towels.
4. Cover windows with blankets, quilts, or plastic sheeting, and close curtains.
5. Hang blankets over open doorways.
6. Put rugs or blankets on the floor.
7. Gather everyone in this room. Body heat adds up.

### Use the Sun

- On sunny days, open curtains on sun-facing windows to let heat in.
- Close them at dusk to hold the heat.

## Keeping Your Body Warm

### Dress in Layers

1. **Base layer** that wicks moisture: wool, silk, or polypropylene. Avoid cotton next to the skin; it stays wet and cold.
2. **Insulating layer:** fleece, wool, or down.
3. **Outer layer** to block drafts.

Wear a hat, gloves or mittens (mittens are warmer), and warm socks. Loose layers trap more warm air than tight ones.

### Conserve Heat

- **Stay dry.** Change out of damp clothes right away.
- **Eat regular meals** and drink fluids. Warm, sweet drinks help. Your body needs fuel to make heat.
- **Move around** to stay warm, but don't get sweaty.
- **Avoid alcohol and caffeine.** Alcohol makes you feel warm while your body loses heat faster.
- Share body heat: huddle together under blankets.

### Sleeping Warm

- Sleep in the warm room, in sleeping bags or under several blankets.
- Wear a hat and dry socks to bed.
- A hot water bottle can help. Fill it with hot (not boiling) tap water, close it tightly, wrap it in a towel, and check that it doesn't leak. Don't use one on babies or on anyone who can't feel or move away from heat.
- Don't use candles or fuel-burning heaters while you sleep.

**Babies under 1 year old should never sleep in a cold room.** They sleep safest on their own firm, flat surface, not in an adult bed. Dress them in layers or a wearable blanket instead of loose blankets.

## Who's Most at Risk

- **Older adults:** they can lose body heat quickly and may not notice how cold they are. Even a mildly cool home, 60 to 65°F (16 to 18°C), can lead to hypothermia in older adults.
- **Babies:** they lose heat quickly. A cold baby may have bright red, cold skin and very low energy.
- **People with chronic illness, disabilities, or who take certain medications**, and anyone who has been drinking.
- **People living alone:** check on older neighbors and relatives often during outages.

## Recognize Cold Emergencies

**Hypothermia** (body temperature below 95°F / 35°C) is a medical emergency. Warning signs:

- Shivering, exhaustion, or feeling very tired
- Confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, or drowsiness
- In babies: bright red, cold skin and very low energy

If you see these signs, **take the person's temperature if you can. If it's below 95°F (35°C), get medical help immediately.** While waiting, move them to the warmest room, remove wet clothing, and warm the center of the body first (chest, neck, head, and groin) with dry blankets or skin-to-skin contact. Give warm, non-alcoholic drinks only if they're fully alert. See the Extreme Cold guide for full first aid.

## When to Leave

Leave for a warming center, shelter, or a home with heat if:

- You can't keep your warm room comfortably warm
- Anyone shows signs of hypothermia (and call 911)
- You have babies, older adults, or people with medical conditions and the outage will last
- You have no safe way to heat

Before leaving in freezing weather, protect your pipes: shut off the main water valve and open faucets to drain the lines, if you know how.

## Supplies to Keep

- [ ] CO alarms (battery or battery backup) outside sleeping areas and on every level, and smoke alarms, tested monthly
- [ ] Sleeping bags rated for cold, and extra blankets
- [ ] Warm layers, hats, gloves, wool socks for everyone
- [ ] Flashlights and lanterns (not candles) with spare batteries
- [ ] Plastic sheeting and duct tape for windows
- [ ] Hand and foot warmers
- [ ] Food that doesn't need cooking, and water
- [ ] Fire extinguisher
- [ ] If you have a fireplace or stove: dry seasoned wood, a screen, and a metal ash bucket with a lid
- [ ] If you have a fuel-burning heater made for indoor use: only the fuel its manufacturer specifies, stored outside living areas in approved containers
