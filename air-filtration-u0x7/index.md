# Air Filtration for Emergencies

> **You can't fix the air outside, but you can clean one room.** A closed door, a filter, and a fan can cut the smoke you breathe indoors by a large margin. Filters stop particles. They don't stop gases, and no filter stops carbon monoxide.

## Do this now

If smoke, ash, or dust is in the air:

1. **Close windows and outside doors.** Set central air or a window unit to recirculate, or close its fresh-air intake.
2. **Pick one room** big enough for everyone, ideally a bedroom with a bathroom attached, and close it off. Keep a clear way out.
3. **Run a filter in that room on its highest setting:** a portable HEPA air cleaner, or a box fan with a MERV 13 furnace filter strapped to the back.
4. **Stop making your own smoke.** No candles, incense, frying, broiling, smoking, or vacuuming (unless the vacuum has a HEPA filter).
5. **If you have central air,** fit a MERV 13 filter if your system can take it, and switch the fan from "Auto" to "On."
6. **Going outside?** Wear a NIOSH-approved N95 or P100 respirator with two head straps. Keep the trip short.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) for:

- Trouble breathing, or shortness of breath that doesn't ease in clean air
- Chest pain or tightness, or a fast or irregular heartbeat
- An asthma attack that doesn't respond to the person's usual medicine
- Headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, or confusion in a home where anything is burning fuel (a generator, heater, stove, or grill). **That pattern is carbon monoxide, not smoke.** Get everyone outside first, then call. See the Generator Safety guide.

People with heart or lung disease, older adults, children, and pregnant people are affected sooner and should act at lower smoke levels.

## What Filters Do and Don't Remove

A fan-and-filter air cleaner traps **particles**. In wildfire smoke, the fine particles are the main health threat, so filtering works well against smoke. It also works against volcanic ash, dust, pollen, and mold spores.

| In the air | Does a particle filter help? |
|------------|------------------------------|
| Smoke particles, ash, dust, pollen | Yes. This is what MERV 13 and HEPA filters are for. |
| Smoke odor and irritating gases | Barely. Only a thick activated carbon filter helps, and there is no standard rating to compare them. |
| Carbon monoxide (CO) | **No.** No home filter, air cleaner, or N95 removes it. |
| Chemical release outside | No. Shut off ventilation and follow the When to Seal a Room guide. |
| Mold | It catches spores in the air, but the mold keeps growing until the moisture is fixed. |

So an air cleaner never makes it safe to burn anything indoors (a CO alarm is your protection there), and a filter is not ventilation: a closed room still needs fresh air once the outside air improves.

## Reading the Ratings

### MERV (furnace and HVAC filters)

MERV is a 1 to 16 scale for how well a filter catches particles of different sizes. The smallest size range matters most for smoke.

| Filter | Smallest particles (0.3 to 1 micron) | Mid-size (1 to 3 microns) | Largest (3 to 10 microns) |
|--------|--------------------------------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| MERV 8 | Not rated | At least 20% | At least 70% |
| MERV 11 | At least 20% | At least 65% | At least 85% |
| **MERV 13** | **At least 50%** | At least 85% | At least 90% |
| HEPA | At least 99.97% at 0.3 microns | Higher still | Higher still |

**MERV 13 is the number to remember.** It is the lowest rating the EPA recommends for smoke. Look for the MERV number printed on the filter or its packaging.

### HEPA

A HEPA filter captures at least 99.97% of particles 0.3 microns across. That size is the hardest to catch, not the smallest it can catch: both larger and smaller particles are trapped even more efficiently.

### CADR (portable air cleaners)

Clean Air Delivery Rate is how much filtered air a unit delivers, in cubic feet per minute. A great filter with a weak fan cleans very little air, so CADR matters more than the word "HEPA" on the box.

**The two-thirds rule:** choose a smoke CADR of at least two-thirds of the room's floor area in square feet. A 12 by 15 foot bedroom is 180 square feet (17 m²), so it needs a smoke CADR of 120 or more.

| Room size | Minimum CADR |
|-----------|--------------|
| 100 sq ft (9 m²) | 65 |
| 200 sq ft (19 m²) | 130 |
| 300 sq ft (28 m²) | 195 |
| 400 sq ft (37 m²) | 260 |
| 500 sq ft (46 m²) | 325 |
| 600 sq ft (56 m²) | 390 |

These figures assume an 8-foot (2.4 m) ceiling. For higher ceilings or an open floor plan, size up or use two units. CADR is measured on the highest fan speed, so a unit run on low delivers much less.

**Avoid ozone.** Skip ozone generators, and be wary of ionizers, "plasma," and UV add-ons, which can produce ozone, a lung irritant. In the US, look for California Air Resources Board (CARB) certification.

## Three Ways to Filter a Home

### 1. Central heating and cooling

- Use a **MERV 13 filter, or the highest rating your system can handle.** Most systems can take one if it is changed often. Ask an HVAC technician if you are unsure.
- The filter must fit snugly. Air that leaks around the edge is not filtered.
- **Set the fan to "On."** On "Auto" the system only moves air when heating or cooling, usually less than a quarter of the time.
- Close the fresh-air intake or set the system to recirculate.
- In heavy smoke, check the filter often and change it when it looks dirty or smells of smoke. Keep spares before fire season, because stores sell out.

### 2. Portable HEPA air cleaner

- Size it with the two-thirds rule above.
- Put it in the room where you spend the most time, usually the bedroom. Keep it clear of curtains and furniture so air can flow.
- Run it continuously on the highest speed you can tolerate, with windows and doors closed.
- Replace or clean filters when they look dirty or smell of smoke.

### 3. Do-it-yourself box fan filter

When you can't buy or afford an air cleaner, a box fan and a furnace filter work. The EPA tested them and considers them a stopgap, not a permanent replacement.

**You need:** a 20-inch box fan, a 20 by 20 inch MERV 13 filter (1 to 4 inches thick), and tape, clamps, or a bungee cord.

1. Find the airflow arrow on the edge of the filter.
2. Attach the filter to the **back** (intake side) of the fan, arrow pointing **toward** the fan.
3. Seal the edges so air can't slip around the filter.
4. Optional but worth it: tape a cardboard shroud over the corners of the fan's front, leaving a circle open the size of the blades.
5. Run it on the highest speed, in a closed room.

**How well each design works** (EPA lab tests, smoke CADR):

| Design | CADR | Room it covers by the two-thirds rule |
|--------|------|---------------------------------------|
| One 1-inch filter | 111 | About 165 sq ft (15 m²) |
| One 1-inch filter plus shroud | 156 | About 235 sq ft (22 m²) |
| One 4-inch filter plus shroud | 248 | About 370 sq ft (34 m²) |
| Two 1-inch filters plus shroud | 263 | About 395 sq ft (37 m²) |
| Four-filter cube ("Corsi-Rosenthal box") plus shroud | 401 | About 600 sq ft (56 m²) |

A piece of scrap cardboard raises performance by about 40%. A 4-inch filter with the shroud more than doubles it.

**Fan safety:**

- **Use a box fan made in 2012 or later with a UL or ETL mark.** These have a fused plug and a thermal cutoff. In testing of five such fans with dirty filters, and with both sides blocked for seven hours, none overheated to an unsafe level or caught fire. Lab tests can't cover every situation, so keep to the rules below.
- The EPA does not recommend older fans. If one is all you have, don't leave it running unattended or while you sleep.
- No extension cords. Don't use a damaged fan. Don't leave children alone with it.
- Have working smoke alarms.

**Change the filter when it turns dark brown or grey, or smells of smoke.** Check daily in heavy smoke. A clogged filter was almost useless in EPA tests.

## Setting Up a Clean Room

1. **Choose the room.** Big enough for the household, comfortable for long stretches. A bedroom with an attached bathroom is ideal.
2. **Close windows and doors,** but never block your exit.
3. **Stay cool without pulling in smoke.** Use fans and air conditioning on recirculate. A window unit whose outdoor air damper can't be closed should stay off. Use single-hose portable air conditioners and evaporative ("swamp") coolers sparingly, because they draw in outside air.
4. **Filter the air** with one of the three methods above.
5. **Run kitchen and bathroom exhaust fans only briefly.** They pull smoky air in through every gap.
6. **Keep dust down** with a damp cloth or mop instead of sweeping.
7. **Air it out when you can.** When the outside air improves, even for a few hours, open the windows and flush the house.

**Leave for a cleaner-air shelter, a friend's home, or a large air-conditioned building if** you can't keep cool, the power goes out, or smoke keeps getting in. Overheating in a closed-up house is a danger of its own.

## How Bad Is the Air?

The US Air Quality Index (AQI) turns particle and ozone readings into one number. Higher is worse:

- **0 to 100** (Good, Moderate): fine for most people.
- **101 to 150** (Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups): people with heart or lung disease, children, older adults, and pregnant people should move indoors and start filtering.
- **151 to 300** (Unhealthy, Very Unhealthy): healthy people are affected too. Close up and run the clean room.
- **301 and up** (Hazardous): emergency conditions.

In the US, AirNow.gov and its Fire and Smoke Map report the AQI. Other countries use their own indexes with different scales. With no data, trust your senses: if the air looks or smells smoky, treat it as unhealthy. The Wildfire Preparedness guide has the full AQI table.

## Respirators for Going Outside

- Choose one marked **NIOSH** and **N95 or P100**, with **two straps** that go around your head: one above the ears, one below. Outside the US, FFP2 and P2 are the comparable ratings.
- It must seal to your face. Press the nose clip down with both hands and check for leaks at the edges. A good seal isn't possible over a beard.
- **Ear-loop masks, single-strap dust masks, surgical masks, and cloth don't seal** and won't protect your lungs from smoke.
- Children 2 and older can wear a respirator, but approved ones aren't made in young children's sizes. Keeping children indoors works better.
- Respirators make breathing harder and add heat. **If you have heart or lung disease or are pregnant, ask your doctor first.** If you get dizzy or short of breath, go to cleaner air and take it off.
- Replace it when it is dirty or harder to breathe through.
- It filters particles only: no protection from carbon monoxide or other gases.

## When the Power Is Out

Every method above needs electricity. A battery power station can run a box fan filter or small air cleaner indoors. A fuel generator must stay **outdoors, at least 20 feet (6 m) from the house, exhaust pointed away**, because its exhaust carries carbon monoxide that no filter catches (see the Generator Safety guide). If you can't filter and can't stay cool, go somewhere with clean air.

## Supplies to Keep

- [ ] Portable HEPA air cleaner sized for your clean room, and a spare filter
- [ ] Or: 20-inch box fan (2012 or later, UL or ETL mark), two or more 20 by 20 inch MERV 13 filters, tape, and cardboard
- [ ] Spare MERV 13 filters for central air, if your system can use them
- [ ] N95 or P100 respirators that fit each adult
- [ ] Working smoke alarms and CO alarms
- [ ] A way to check the AQI, and a battery radio for when the internet is down

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Measure your clean room. Multiply length by width in feet, then multiply by two-thirds: that is the smoke CADR you need. Now check what you own. Read the CADR on your air cleaner's label, or find the date and UL or ETL mark on your box fan. Last, find your furnace filter slot, read the size printed on the filter's edge, and write it on your shopping list.

**What would you do?**

1. *Smoke has been thick for two days. All you have is a box fan from the early 2000s and a new MERV 13 filter. It's bedtime.*

   **Answer:** Build it and run it while you are awake and in the room, then switch it off before you sleep or leave. Fans made before 2012 lack the fused plug and thermal cutoff, and the EPA says not to run them unattended or overnight. Keep the room closed so the air you cleaned stays clean, and put a newer fan on your list.

2. *The power fails during a smoke event. A neighbor suggests running your generator in the garage with the door cracked so the air cleaner can keep going.*

   **Answer:** No. A generator in a garage can fill the house with carbon monoxide in minutes, and neither the air cleaner nor an N95 removes it. Run the generator outdoors at least 20 feet from the house with the exhaust pointed away, use a battery power station, or go to a clean-air shelter.

3. *The AQI is 180. Your home has no air conditioning, it is 95°F (35°C) inside with the windows shut, and your elderly father is with you.*

   **Answer:** Don't stay sealed in. A closed house at that temperature is dangerous for him, and the EPA's advice is to shelter somewhere else when you can't stay cool. Go to an air-conditioned place with filtered air: a cooling or clean-air center, a library, a mall, or a relative's home. Give him water on the way, and get medical help at the first sign of heat illness.
