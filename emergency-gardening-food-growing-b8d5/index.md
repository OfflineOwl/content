# Emergency Gardening & Food Growing

> **A garden is slow food.** Seed planted today feeds you in one to four months, not this week. That makes a garden a poor answer to a sudden emergency and a very good answer to a long one, as long as you start before you need it.

## Start here

1. **Find your sunniest spot.** Vegetables want full sun: at least 6 hours of direct light a day, and more is better. Put the garden near a water source.
2. **Start small.** One bed of 4 by 8 feet (1.2 by 2.4 m), or a few large containers, is enough to learn on.
3. **Plant something fast today.** Radishes and spinach are ready in 25 to 40 days, leaf lettuce in 45 to 65.
4. **Buy seed now and store it right:** fast greens, plus calorie crops such as potatoes, beans, and winter squash. Keep seed sealed, dry, and cool.
5. **Ask your county extension office** for the local planting calendar, frost dates, and a soil test. In the US every state has an extension service, and most publish free guides. Elsewhere, ask your agriculture ministry or a local gardening society.
6. **Start a compost pile.** It takes months, so begin before you need it.

## The honest math

A garden gives you fresh vitamins quickly and calories slowly. The table uses extension yield figures for a 10-foot (3 m) row and FDA calorie values. Real yields swing widely with soil, weather, and skill.

| Crop | Days to first harvest | Yield per 10 ft (3 m) row | Rough calories in that row |
|------|-----------------------|---------------------------|----------------------------|
| Radishes | 25 to 40 | 3 to 5 lb | 160 to 270 |
| Leaf lettuce | 45 to 65 | 2 to 5 lb | 160 to 400 |
| Summer squash | 45 to 55 | 15 lb or more | 1,400 or more |
| Bush snap beans | 50 to 60 | 3 to 12 lb | 330 to 1,300 |
| Carrots | 54 to 80 | 7 to 10 lb | 1,200 to 1,700 |
| Sweet corn | 68 to 90 | About 12 ears | 1,100 |
| Potatoes | 70 to 90 | 10 to 20 lb | 3,400 to 6,700 |
| Sweet potatoes | 90 to 105 | 8 to 12 lb | 2,800 to 4,200 |
| Tomatoes | 52 to 120, from transplants | 10 lb or more | 770 or more |

(1 lb is about 0.45 kg.)

Read the potato line again. It is the best calorie crop on the list, and a 10-foot row grows about two to three days of food for one adult eating 2,000 calories a day, three months after planting. Kansas State suggests 50 to 100 feet of potato row per person for an ordinary kitchen garden. Even that is only about one to five weeks of one adult's calories.

What this means:

- **A garden supplements stored food. It does not replace it.** Feeding a household from the ground takes a lot of land, several seasons of practice, and a way to store the harvest.
- **Grow greens for health and morale.** Even a 100-square-foot (9 m²) plot can supply a good share of a family's fresh vegetables.
- **Grow potatoes, sweet potatoes, winter squash, dry beans, and corn for calories and storage.** Dry beans are small yielders by weight: extension tables put shelled beans at roughly 2 to 5 lb per 10-foot row.
- **Start now.** The first season is mostly practice.

## What to plant when

Plants differ in how much cold they stand. Your last spring frost and first fall frost dates set the calendar: get them from your extension office.

| Group | Crops | When to plant |
|-------|-------|---------------|
| **Hardy** | Peas, spinach, kale, radishes, onions, turnips, mustard | Early spring, as soon as the soil can be worked; again in late summer for fall |
| **Half-hardy** | Lettuce, beets, carrots, potatoes | A little later in spring; they take light frost |
| **Tender** | Beans, sweet corn, tomatoes, peppers | After the last frost |
| **Very tender** | Squash, cucumbers, sweet potatoes, okra, melons | After the last frost, once the soil is warm |

Cool-season seed sprouts best in soil around 50 to 60°F (10 to 16°C). Warm-season seed wants 70 to 85°F (21 to 29°C) and rots in cold ground.

**Succession planting:** sow a short row of fast crops every 7 to 10 days, not the whole packet at once. You get a steady harvest in place of one glut.

## Soil and site

- **Drainage:** choose ground where water does not stand after rain. Where it does, build raised beds.
- **Bed width:** 3 to 4 feet (about 1 m), so you can reach the middle without stepping on the soil.
- **Feed the soil:** mix in finished compost every fall or spring.
- **Rotate:** avoid growing the same plant family in the same spot for 3 to 4 years. Tomatoes, potatoes, and peppers are one family; squash and cucumbers another; cabbage, kale, and radishes another.

### Check for lead before you dig

Soil near old painted buildings and busy roads often holds lead. You cannot see or smell it.

- Have the soil tested through your extension office or health department before growing food in a yard with an unknown history.
- Until you know, keep children and pets out of the plot. Children and pregnant or breastfeeding women are the most sensitive to lead.
- **Above 400 ppm lead:** do not grow food in that soil. Use containers, or raised beds holding at least 16 inches (40 cm) of clean soil over landscape fabric.
- Everywhere: wash hands and tools, wash produce, discard the outer leaves of leafy crops, and peel root crops. Keep soil near neutral (pH 6.5 to 7.5) and mulched to hold dust down.

### Raised beds

Raised beds drain well and let you bring in clean soil. Lumber sold for home use today is mostly treated with copper compounds (ACQ) that contain no arsenic, and researchers find only very small amounts move into soil. The older arsenic treatment (CCA) was restricted for home use in 2004, so avoid salvaged treated wood of unknown age. Stone, block, and untreated wood also work.

### Containers

No ground at all? Food-safe buckets and tubs work if they drain.

| Crop | Minimum container | Minimum depth |
|------|-------------------|---------------|
| Lettuce, spinach, radishes, scallions, herbs | 1 to 3 gallons (4 to 11 L) | 4 to 6 in (10 to 15 cm) |
| Beans, beets, carrots, chard, summer squash | 4 to 6 gallons (15 to 23 L) | 8 to 12 in (20 to 30 cm) |
| One tomato, pepper, or cucumber plant | 8 to 10 gallons (30 to 38 L) | 12 to 16 in (30 to 40 cm) |
| Potatoes | About 30 gallons (115 L) of potting mix | Deep tub or bag |

- Every container needs drainage holes. Do not put gravel in the bottom: it does not help drainage.
- Dark containers in full sun can overheat roots. Clay and wood dry out faster than plastic.
- Never reuse a container that held chemicals, fuel, or paint.

## Water

- **How much:** a vegetable garden needs about 1 inch (2.5 cm) of water a week from rain and watering combined. That is about 62 gallons per 100 square feet (235 L per 9 m²).
- **How often:** once a week on clay or loam. On sandy soil, half an inch twice a week.
- **Check first:** dig down. Water when the soil is dry 2 inches (5 cm) below the surface.
- **When:** early morning, low and slow at the base of the plants. Wet leaves in the evening invite disease. If plants are wilting, water now, whatever the hour.
- **Mulch** with straw, leaves, or untreated grass clippings to hold moisture.
- **Too much water** drowns roots. Soil should be moist, not soggy.

In a dry week a 100-square-foot bed drinks about 9 gallons (34 L) a day. That cannot come out of your stored drinking water. Plan a separate source: see the Rainwater Collection guide. To learn your hose's flow, time how long it takes to fill a 5-gallon bucket. Twelve buckets' worth of time puts an inch on 100 square feet.

## Seeds

### Storing seed

Dryness matters most, then cold. Keep seed in a moisture-proof container (a sealed jar or can, not a plastic bag) in a cool place. A refrigerator at 32 to 41°F (0 to 5°C) is ideal. Add a packet of silica gel, or 1 to 2 tablespoons of dry milk powder wrapped in tissue, to absorb moisture, and label every packet with variety and year.

On a dry shelf at room temperature, most seed keeps for a year without much loss. In good storage, expect roughly:

| Years | Crops |
|-------|-------|
| 1 | Onion, parsley, parsnip |
| 2 | Sweet corn, pepper, okra |
| 3 | Beans, peas, carrots, spinach, broccoli |
| 4 | Beets, kale, radish, squash, tomato, turnip, Swiss chard |
| 5 | Cucumber |

Thoroughly dried seed in sealed containers below 40°F (4°C) can last 10 years or more. Old seed is not dangerous. It just sprouts poorly, so test it (see Practice) and sow thicker.

### Saving seed

- **Save only from open-pollinated varieties** (the packet says "open-pollinated" or "heirloom"). Seed saved from hybrids, often marked F1, grows into an unpredictable mix.
- **Easiest for beginners:** beans, peas, tomatoes, and peppers. They mostly pollinate themselves.
- **Beans and peas:** leave pods on the plant until brown and rattling, up to a month past eating stage. Dry indoors at least two more weeks before shelling.
- **Tomatoes:** scoop seeds and gel from a fully ripe fruit into a jar of water and stir twice a day. Within about five days the good seeds sink. Rinse them and dry on paper.
- **Harder:** corn and the squash family cross with other varieties nearby. Carrots and beets need two years to make seed.
- **Save from your healthiest plants only.** Disease can travel on seed.

### Potatoes are planted from potatoes

Use certified seed potatoes if you can get them. Extension services advise against planting grocery-store potatoes, which may carry disease and are often treated to stop sprouting.

1. Cut large seed potatoes into pieces about 1.25 inches (3 cm) across, each with at least one eye. Let the cut faces dry for a few days.
2. Plant 3 to 5 inches (8 to 13 cm) deep, 10 to 12 inches (25 to 30 cm) apart, in rows 30 to 36 inches (75 to 90 cm) apart.
3. When stems are a foot (30 cm) tall, pull soil up around them. Repeat until the ridge is 6 to 8 inches (15 to 20 cm) high. Tubers exposed to light turn green.
4. Dig small "new" potatoes after 7 to 8 weeks. For storage, wait until the vines die back.
5. Cure for 10 days or more at 60 to 65°F (16 to 18°C) in the dark, then store cold, dark, and humid at 32 to 40°F (0 to 4°C).

**Green potatoes and sprouts:** cut away green areas, throw out potatoes with extensive greening, and remove all sprouts before cooking. Sprouts are toxic.

## Compost and manure

**Compost** turns scraps into the fertilizer you can no longer buy.

- Mix two to three parts "browns" (dry leaves, straw, shredded plain paper and cardboard) to one part "greens" (fruit and vegetable scraps, grass clippings, coffee grounds) by volume.
- Keep it as damp as a wrung-out sponge and turn it now and then. A working pile heats to 130 to 160°F (54 to 71°C) in the middle.
- **Time:** 3 to 5 months when well tended, up to a year if left alone. Finished compost is dark, crumbly, smells like soil, and no longer heats up.
- **Leave out:** meat, fish, bones, dairy, fats, pet waste and cat litter, diseased plants, weeds with seeds, herbicide-treated clippings, and treated or painted wood.

**Manure** can carry E. coli and other germs onto food.

- Raw manure must go on at least **120 days before harvest** for any crop that touches or sits near the soil (lettuce, carrots, potatoes, radishes), and 90 days for crops held well above it, such as sweet corn. The simple rule: spread raw manure in fall, not on a growing garden.
- A heap that just sits is not composted. Only a pile that heats through is.
- **Never use dog, cat, or human waste** on food crops.
- Washing lowers the risk. Cooking does more.

## Pests and disease without a store

- Walk the garden daily and pick off beetles, caterpillars, and egg clusters by hand.
- Cover young plants with light fabric or netting to keep insects, birds, and rabbits off.
- Pull out and discard diseased plants. Do not compost them.
- Rotate plant families, water at the base, and give plants room for air.

## Garden kit

- [ ] Seed: fast greens, plus potatoes, beans, squash, and corn suited to your region
- [ ] Open-pollinated varieties if you plan to save seed
- [ ] Sealed jar with silica gel for seed storage, in the refrigerator
- [ ] Spade or digging fork, hoe, trowel, gloves
- [ ] Watering can and buckets; rain barrel if allowed where you live
- [ ] Containers with drainage holes, and potting mix, if you have no ground
- [ ] Mulch material: straw or saved leaves
- [ ] Light row-cover fabric or netting
- [ ] Soil test results, including lead
- [ ] Your extension service's planting calendar, printed

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Test your oldest seed packet. Count 10 seeds onto a damp paper towel, fold it, slide it into a plastic bag, and write the date on the bag. Keep it somewhere warm. In 7 to 10 days count the sprouts: 8 of 10 is 80% germination. Under about half, sow that packet extra thick or replace it. While you wait, phone or look up your extension office and write down your last spring frost date.

**What would you do?**

1. *It is July. Supply trucks have stopped and your household has two weeks of food. Your partner wants to dig up the lawn and plant potatoes to cover the gap.*

   **Answer:** Plant, but do not count on it. Potatoes need 70 to 90 days, and even then a 10-foot row is two or three days of food for one adult. The gap has to be covered by stretching stored food and finding other supplies (see the Rationing Calorie Planning guide). Sow fast greens now for vitamins in a month, and potatoes or beans for the fall.

2. *The only sunny ground you have runs along a 1920s garage with peeling paint. The soil has never been tested. The kids want to help dig.*

   **Answer:** Treat it as lead-contaminated until a test says otherwise. Keep the children out, and do not grow food in that soil. Use containers, or a raised bed with at least 16 inches of clean soil over fabric, set as far from the wall as you can. Wash hands and produce, and peel root crops.

3. *In June a neighbor offers a trailer of fresh chicken manure for your half-grown lettuce and carrots.*

   **Answer:** Take it, but not for those beds. Raw manure needs 120 days before harvest for crops that touch the soil, and your lettuce will be picked long before that. Pile it well away from the garden to compost, or spread it in fall after harvest. Feed this year's crop with finished compost.
