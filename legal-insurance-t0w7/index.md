# Legal & Insurance Readiness

> **The policy you have on the day is the policy you have.** Insurance and legal papers cannot be fixed after the water rises. An hour with your policy now decides whether a disaster is a hard month or a lost decade.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Policies, laws and aid programs differ by state and country, so check your own policy and local rules, and ask a lawyer or your state insurance department when it matters.

## Start here

1. **Find your declarations page.** It is the one- or two-page summary at the front of your home or renters policy. Read the coverage limits and the deductible.
2. **Ask the flood question.** Most home and renters policies do not cover flood. Unless you hold a separate flood policy, assume you have no flood coverage.
3. **Check "replacement cost" or "actual cash value".** One buys a new roof. The other buys a used one.
4. **Save the claims phone number** in your phone and in your documents kit. The number, the policy number and your name are enough to start a claim.
5. **Video your home.** A 10-minute walk-through with your phone is the best claim evidence you will ever have. See the Important Documents Offline Kit guide.
6. **Name the people who can act for you:** a will, a power of attorney and an advance directive. Details below.

## What a home or renters policy usually covers

| Part of the policy | What it pays for |
|--------------------|------------------|
| **Dwelling** | Repairing or rebuilding the house, up to the limit on the declarations page |
| **Other structures** | Detached garage, shed, fence |
| **Personal property** | Your belongings. Jewelry, firearms, computers and similar items often have low caps unless you add coverage for them. |
| **Loss of use (additional living expenses)** | Necessary extra costs of living elsewhere while a covered loss makes the home unlivable, up to a stated limit |
| **Liability** | Claims from people injured on your property or by your actions |

Renters insurance works the same way without the dwelling part: it covers your belongings and liability. Do not assume your landlord's insurance covers your things. Ask, and read your own policy.

**Replacement cost** pays what it takes to repair or replace with similar materials. **Actual cash value** subtracts depreciation first, so a 15-year-old roof pays out as a 15-year-old roof. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) suggests insuring a home for at least 80 percent of its replacement value and reviewing the policy with your agent every year, because renovations and building costs change the number.

**Deductibles:** the amount you pay on each claim before insurance does. Some policies carry a special deductible for named storms. Find out before the storm has a name.

## What it usually does not cover

| Hazard | The usual answer |
|--------|------------------|
| **Flood** (rising water from rain, runoff or snowmelt) | Not covered. Needs a flood policy from the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private insurer. |
| **Earthquake** | Not covered. Needs an added endorsement or a separate policy. |
| **Mold and pest infestations** | Typically excluded |
| **Business property kept at home** | Typically excluded or tightly capped |
| **Your car** | Not a home policy matter. Flood, fire, theft and storm damage to a vehicle fall under the comprehensive part of an auto policy, if you bought it. |

### Flood insurance has a waiting period

An NFIP policy generally takes effect **30 days after you buy it**. You cannot buy it when the storm is on the forecast map and expect it to pay.

The exceptions are narrow: there is no wait when you buy it with a new, increased or renewed mortgage, and a one-day wait applies if your property was newly mapped into a high-risk flood zone or the flood is caused by wildfire on federal land. Confirm current rules at FloodSmart.gov or through the NFIP help line, 1-800-427-4661.

NFIP limits for a home are **up to $250,000 for the building and up to $100,000 for contents**, bought as separate coverages. Renters can buy contents coverage alone. FEMA calls flooding the nation's most common natural disaster.

## Filing a claim that gets paid

Safety first: do not go into a damaged building to take photos until it is safe to enter.

1. **Call your insurer or agent as soon as you can.** Most companies set a time limit for reporting.
2. **Document before you clean.** Photograph and video everything before moving debris. For appliances and electronics, record make, model and serial number.
3. **Stop further damage.** Cover the hole in the roof, board the broken window. Insurers advise this, and the cost is typically reimbursed if the loss is covered. Keep it temporary.
4. **Keep every receipt:** tarps, hotel, meals, storage, laundry. Loss-of-use coverage runs on receipts.
5. **Hold off on permanent repairs** until the adjuster has seen the damage or approved the estimate.
6. **Keep samples and damaged items** where you safely can: a square of carpet, a piece of flooring. Throw out what is a health hazard after photographing it.
7. **Keep a claim diary:** date, who you spoke to, what was said. Follow phone calls with a short email.
8. **Read the adjuster's estimate** and say so if something is missing.

**Flood claims have a hard deadline.** For an NFIP policy you must send a signed Proof of Loss, with supporting documents, within **60 days of the flood** unless FEMA announces an extension. Send it even if you disagree with the adjuster's figure. You can add missed items in a supplemental claim later.

**Adjusters:** the insurer's adjuster costs you nothing. A public adjuster offers the same service on your behalf, and you pay the fee. If your insurer is not responding or not investigating reasonably, your state insurance department takes complaints and helps for free.

**Your mortgage does not pause.** You still owe payments on a home you cannot live in, and missing them can put the loan in default. Call your lender early, explain, and ask what relief they offer. Renters: read your lease, check local law, and talk to the landlord early.

## FEMA help is not insurance

After a presidential disaster declaration that includes Individual Assistance, FEMA can help with temporary housing, basic home repairs and other serious needs. FEMA's own words: this help "is not a substitute for insurance and cannot compensate for all losses."

What that means in practice:

- **Apply to both.** File your insurance claim and apply to FEMA. You must tell FEMA what insurance you have.
- **FEMA cannot pay for something insurance already paid for.** It looks at needs that are uninsured or under-insured, and will ask for your settlement letter or a denial letter.
- **Applying is free.** Use DisasterAssistance.gov, call 1-800-621-3362, or ask at a Disaster Recovery Center. Have ready: Social Security number, addresses, phone, insurance details, household income, a description of the damage, and bank details for direct deposit.
- **Write down your FEMA application number.**
- **SBA disaster loans** are the larger pot of federal money for households. The Small Business Administration lends to homeowners and renters, not only businesses: up to $500,000 to repair or replace a primary home and up to $100,000 for personal property. They are loans, to be repaid. SBA: 1-800-659-2955.
- **Read every letter and note the deadlines.** Application and appeal windows are limited.

Outside the US, disaster aid and insurance work differently. Your national or regional emergency agency is the place to start.

## Legal documents to have in place

Disasters injure and separate people. These documents decide who can sign, pay and speak for you. Requirements vary by state and country, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau notes that a lawyer's help with a power of attorney is usually inexpensive and worth it.

| Document | What it does |
|----------|--------------|
| **Will** | Says who receives your property after your death. It is also usually where parents name a guardian for minor children. |
| **Power of attorney (financial)** | Lets a person you choose act for you: pay bills, deal with the insurer, sell property. A "durable" one stays in force if you become incapacitated. |
| **Advance directive** | Covers medical decisions if you cannot speak for yourself. Usually two parts: a **living will** (which treatments you want) and a **durable power of attorney for health care**, which names your health care proxy. |
| **Guardianship or custody papers** | Show who may make decisions for a child or dependent adult |

- [ ] Each adult in the household has these, signed
- [ ] The people named know they are named and where the papers are
- [ ] The advance directive is on file with your doctor and hospital
- [ ] Copies are in the documents kit
- [ ] Reviewed after a marriage, divorce, birth, death or move to another state

## Fraud after a disaster

Scammers arrive with the cleanup crews. The Federal Trade Commission's warning signs for repair scams:

- They say they do not need a license.
- They want everything paid up front, or cash, wire transfer, gift cards, payment apps or cryptocurrency.
- They ask you to sign over your insurance check, or to sign a contract with blank spaces.
- They pressure you to decide today.

**Do instead:** get more than one written estimate, confirm license and insurance, get a written contract, negotiate a modest down payment, pay by check or credit card, and make the final payment only when the work is done.

**Fake officials:** FEMA never charges for applications or inspections. Real inspectors carry a government photo ID badge; a jacket or shirt proves nothing. Not sure? Call FEMA at 1-800-621-3362.

**Fake charities:** look the charity up through an independent source such as Give.org or Charity Navigator before giving, and never donate by gift card, wire or cryptocurrency.

**Report it:** to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to your state attorney general. FEMA's Disaster Fraud page lists where to report fraud that involves FEMA aid.

## Free legal help: Disaster Legal Services

After a major disaster declaration, FEMA can switch on Disaster Legal Services: free, confidential help from volunteer lawyers for survivors who cannot afford one. They are not FEMA employees and do not share what you tell them with FEMA.

Typical help: insurance claims, home repair contracts and contractor disputes, landlord and tenant problems, replacing wills and other legal papers lost in the disaster, powers of attorney, consumer protection, and FEMA appeals. There is no application. Call the hotline announced for your disaster, or ask at a Disaster Recovery Center. Cases that would earn a lawyer a fee are referred on.

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Get your policy out and answer five questions on an index card: What is my deductible? Do I have flood coverage? Replacement cost or actual cash value? What is my limit for living expenses if I cannot stay home? What is the claims phone number? Put the card in your documents kit. Any question you cannot answer is a call to your agent this week.

**What would you do?**

1. *A hurricane is forecast to reach your coast in five days. You have a homeowners policy but no flood insurance, and your neighbor says you can still buy it online today.*

   **Answer:** You can buy it, but it will almost certainly not cover this storm: NFIP policies generally take effect 30 days after purchase. Buying it is still sensible, because the season is not over. For this storm, protect what you can: move valuables and documents up high, video the house as it is now, and follow evacuation instructions. Your homeowners policy may still cover wind damage, so know your named-storm deductible.

2. *Wind has torn shingles off and rain is coming through the ceiling. The adjuster cannot come for ten days. You worry that touching anything will hurt your claim.*

   **Answer:** Doing nothing is the mistake. Photograph and video the damage first, then make temporary repairs to stop it getting worse: a tarp on the roof, buckets, wet items moved out. Keep the receipts and the damaged materials. What you should not do before the adjuster sees it is the permanent repair.

3. *A contractor going door to door offers to fix your roof tomorrow. He needs you to sign your insurance check over to him and pay the balance in cash today.*

   **Answer:** Walk away. Signing over the check, cash up front and same-day pressure are three of the FTC's warning signs. Get written estimates from more than one licensed, insured contractor, pay a reasonable deposit by check or card, and hold the final payment until the job is finished. If he will not leave or becomes threatening, call the police.
