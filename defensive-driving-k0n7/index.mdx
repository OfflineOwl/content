# Emergency Defensive Driving

> **The safest emergency drive is the early one.** The road hazards that kill in a disaster are mostly avoidable ones: floodwater, ice, smoke and downed power lines. Leaving sooner, slowing down and turning around matter more than any driving trick.

## Do this now

If you have to drive during an emergency:

1. **Decide whether to drive at all.** If officials tell you to evacuate, go now. If you have not been told to leave and the roads are hazardous, staying put is usually safer than driving.
2. **Buckle everyone in.** Children under 13 ride in the back, in the right car seat, booster or belt for their size.
3. **Lights on, speed down, gap up.** Turn on low-beam headlights and leave far more room than usual behind the vehicle ahead.
4. **Take the official route.** Shortcuts may be blocked. Never drive around a barricade or into water covering the road.
5. **Tell someone your route and when you expect to arrive.**
6. **If you can't see or can't control the car, get off the road.** Pull over as far as you can, stop, and set the parking brake.

## Get emergency help if

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) if:

- **Anyone is hurt,** or the vehicle is smoking or on fire. Get everyone out and well away from the car and from traffic.
- **Your vehicle is caught in moving water** or water is rising around it.
- **A power line is on or near your vehicle.** Stay inside and warn others to keep away.
- **You are stopped in a traffic lane** and can't move the vehicle.
- **Another driver is following or threatening you.** Keep driving toward a police station or a busy public place while you call.
- **You are stranded in extreme cold or heat** and can't keep people safe.

Outside the US and Canada the number differs: 112 works across the European Union and in many other countries, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia. Know yours before you travel.

## Before you turn the key

Ten minutes of preparation matters more than any driving technique.

- **Fuel.** Keep at least half a tank at all times, and fill up when an evacuation seems likely. Gas stations often can't pump in a power outage, and evacuation traffic burns fuel while going nowhere. Keep an electric vehicle charged the same way.
- **Tires.** Check pressure once a month and before long trips, when the tires are cold (the car has sat for at least three hours). The right pressure is on the placard on the driver's door frame and in the owner's manual. It is not the number molded into the tire. Tread must be at least 2/32 inch (1.6 mm). AAA suggests replacing earlier: push a quarter into the groove with Washington's head down, and if you can see the top of his head you have less than 4/32 inch (3.2 mm) left. Check the spare too.
- **See and be seen.** Working headlights, brake lights, turn signals and hazard flashers, good wiper blades, and a full washer reservoir.
- **Clear the whole car** of snow, ice or ash before moving, including lights, cameras and the roof.
- **Route.** Know two ways out of your area and carry a paper map. Plan on one car per household to cut congestion.
- **Kit.** See the Vehicle Emergency Kit guide for what to keep in the trunk.

## What you see and what you do

| What you see | Do this | Never |
|--------------|---------|-------|
| Water over the road | Stop, turn around, find another route | Drive in to "test" the depth, or go around a barricade |
| Snow or ice | Slow down, leave 5 to 6 seconds of gap, brake and steer gently | Use cruise control |
| Thick smoke, fog or dust | Low beams, slow down, pull fully off the road if you can't see | Use high beams, or stop in a travel lane |
| Power line down | Stay back at least 35 feet (10 m), take another route | Drive over a line or through water that touches one |
| Traffic signals dark | Stop, then take turns as at an all-way stop | Assume the other driver will stop |
| Angry or following driver | No eye contact, no gestures, drive to a public place, call 911 | Drive home or get out of the car |

## Floodwater

Driving into water is the most common way people die in floods. The National Weather Service says more than half of all flood drownings happen when a vehicle is driven into hazardous water.

Water is heavier and stronger than it looks:

- **6 inches (15 cm)** of fast-moving water can knock an adult off their feet, and is enough to make a car lose control or stall.
- **12 inches (30 cm)** of rushing water can carry away most cars.
- **2 feet (60 cm)** can carry away SUVs and pickup trucks.

You also can't see what is under it. The road may have washed out or collapsed, which is exactly why the barricade is there.

- **Turn Around, Don't Drown.** If water covers the road, stop and find another way. It does not matter that the vehicle ahead of you made it.
- **Stay off bridges over fast-moving water.** Floodwater can wash a bridge out without warning.
- **Be extra careful at night,** when water on a dark road is nearly invisible until you are in it.
- **If your car is trapped in rapidly moving water, stay inside.** If water rises inside the car, get onto the roof and call or signal for help.

The Flood Response and Water Safety guide covers flooding in full.

## Ice and snow

On a slick road everything takes longer: starting, turning, and above all stopping.

- **Slow down,** even if the road only looks wet.
- **Stretch your following distance to 5 or 6 seconds.** Pick a sign the car ahead passes and count until you reach it.
- **Do everything gently.** Press the gas slowly to keep traction. Start slowing well before you need to.
- **No cruise control** on any slippery surface.
- **Braking with antilock brakes (ABS):** press firmly and keep the pressure on. The pedal may pulse or vibrate; that is the system working. Do not pump. You can still steer while you brake.
- **Braking without ABS:** if you feel the wheels start to lock, ease off and reapply.
- **If you skid:** stay calm, ease off the gas, and steer where you want the front of the car to go. Don't slam the brakes.
- **Hills:** build a little momentum on the flat before the climb, since extra gas on the slope just spins the wheels. Slow down at the crest and go down slowly.
- **Snow plows:** stay behind them, where the road is better. Don't drive beside one or into the snow cloud it throws up.

**If you get stuck or stranded, stay with the car.** You get lost quickly on foot in blowing snow. Run the engine about 10 minutes each hour for heat, clear snow from the exhaust pipe first, and open a window slightly while it runs so carbon monoxide can't build up. Tie a bright cloth to the antenna or door, and turn on the dome light at night while the engine is running. The Extreme Cold Survival guide has more.

## Smoke, fog and dust

Low visibility causes chain-reaction crashes: one driver slows, and the next one does not see them in time.

- **Use low beams.** High beams bounce off fog, smoke and dust and blind you. Low beams also switch your tail lights on.
- **Slow down and leave a long gap.** Follow the painted lines with your eyes.
- **If you can barely see:** turn on your hazard lights and pull into a parking lot or driveway if there is one. If not, pull as far off the pavement as you can. Never stop in a travel lane.
- **Once you are stopped off the road,** set the parking brake and take your foot off the brake pedal so drivers behind don't follow your tail lights into you. In fog the National Weather Service says to leave only your hazard flashers on. In a dust storm it says to turn all lights off.
- **Dust storms:** don't drive into one if you can avoid it. The rule is "Pull Aside, Stay Alive."
- **Wildfire smoke:** leave early, before smoke and traffic close the road. Drive with headlights on, windows up, and the air set to recirculate. Watch for fire crews and people on foot. See the Wildfire Preparedness guide for what to do if fire traps you in a vehicle.

## Downed power lines

You cannot tell whether a line is live by looking at it. A line that isn't sparking or humming can still kill, and the ground around it can be energized up to 35 feet (10 m) away.

- **Never drive over a downed line,** and don't drive through standing water that has a line in it.
- **If a line falls across your car while you are driving, stay inside and keep driving away from the line.**
- **If you can't drive clear, stay in the car.** Call 911, honk to get attention, and warn people not to touch the car or come near. Wait until a utility or emergency crew tells you the line is dead.
- **Get out only if the car is on fire.** Jump clear so that you never touch the car and the ground at the same time, land with both feet together, and shuffle away in small steps without lifting your feet.

## Dark signals, damaged roads and storms

- **Signals out.** When a power outage leaves a traffic signal dark or flashing red, California's emergency agency and many US states tell drivers to treat it as an all-way stop: come to a full stop, then take turns. The exact rule varies by state and country, so check your local driver handbook. Directions from police or emergency workers always override the signal. Watch for pedestrians and cyclists, who are hard to see at an unlit intersection.
- **Earthquake while driving.** Pull over, stop, and set the parking brake. Stay away from overpasses, bridges, power lines, signs and trees. Stay in the car until the shaking stops, then go on carefully, watching for cracked pavement and debris.
- **Tornado.** A vehicle is not safe shelter. Drive to the closest sturdy shelter if you can. If you can't reach one, either get down in the car and cover your head, or leave the car for a low spot such as a ditch. See the Tornado Safety guide.
- **Heavy rain.** Slow down to avoid hydroplaning, which is when the tires ride up on a film of water. Don't use cruise control. If the car starts to slide, look and steer where you want to go, and don't slam the brakes.

## Evacuation traffic

- **Leave early.** That is the whole strategy. Leaving early keeps you out of both the hazard and the worst traffic.
- **Follow the recommended evacuation routes.** Officials may reverse lanes or close ramps, so follow signs and directions even when your map app disagrees.
- **Expect to crawl.** Bring water, food, medications and a phone charger within reach, not buried in the trunk.
- **Watch for** washed-out roads and bridges, downed lines, debris, and emergency vehicles.
- **Stay calm and predictable.** Don't block intersections, use the shoulder as a lane, or tailgate. A fender-bender in an evacuation can strand hundreds of cars behind you.
- **Stop to rest or change drivers** if you get drowsy on a long evacuation.

**If the car breaks down on a highway:** move fully onto the right shoulder if you can, turn on the hazard lights, call 911, and stay inside with your seat belt on. Standing beside a disabled car on a highway is more dangerous than sitting in it. The exception is smoke or fire: then everyone gets out and moves well away, upwind and clear of traffic.

## If someone threatens you on the road

Your car's best safety feature in a confrontation is that it can leave.

- **Don't answer anger with anger.** Avoid eye contact with an angry driver, make no gestures, and give them room to go.
- **Keep doors locked and windows up.**
- **Leave space.** In traffic and when parking, leave enough room to pull out if someone approaches.
- **If you feel at risk, don't drive home.** Drive to a public place such as a police station, fire station or hospital, and call 911. Stay in the locked car and use the horn to attract attention.
- **If a crowd or disturbance is blocking the road ahead, turn around early** and take another route. Don't drive into or through a crowd.

Keep moving away from danger, and let the police handle the rest. This guide does not cover any use of a vehicle against people or other vehicles. That is dangerous, and the law on it varies by place.

## People who need extra thought

- **Children:** correct car seat or booster, in the back seat. Never leave a child alone in a parked car, in any weather.
- **Older adults and people with disabilities or medical equipment:** leave at the first warning, and bring medications and devices in the cabin.
- **Pets:** bring them, in a carrier or harness. Public shelters may accept only service animals, so plan where you will go.

- [ ] Windows, lights and roof cleared of snow, ice or ash

- [ ] Someone told your route and expected arrival time

## Practice

**10-minute drill:** Do a parked walk-around today. Find the tire pressure placard on the driver's door frame and read the numbers. Check all four tires and the spare with a gauge. Sit in the driver's seat and find the hazard light switch, low beams and rear defroster without looking. Then open a paper map and trace two different routes out of your neighborhood with a finger. Nothing in this drill involves moving the car.

**What would you do?**

1. *It is raining hard at night and you are ten minutes from home. Brown water is flowing across a dip in the road. It looks only a few inches deep, and the pickup ahead of you just drove through.*

   **Answer:** Turn around. You can't judge depth or current in the dark, and you can't see whether the road under the water is still there. Twelve inches of moving water carries away most cars, and the pickup sits higher than you do. A detour costs minutes. More than half of flood drownings happen in vehicles.

2. *A storm brings a pole down and a wire lands across your hood. The engine has stalled. A passer-by runs toward you to help you out.*

   **Answer:** Stay in the car and shout or signal for them to stay back, at least 35 feet. Inside, you are protected; stepping out makes your body the path between the car and the ground, and the ground near the car may be energized too. Call 911, honk to keep people away, and wait for the utility crew. Only fire changes this: then jump clear with feet together and shuffle away.

3. *You honked at a car that cut you off. Now it is behind you, lights flashing, following every turn. Your street is next.*

   **Answer:** Don't turn into your street, and don't stop to talk. Keep the doors locked, avoid eye contact, and drive at a normal speed toward a police station, fire station, hospital or other busy, well-lit place. Call 911 (hands-free, or have a passenger call) and tell the dispatcher where you are and where you are heading. Stay in the locked car when you get there and use the horn if they approach.
