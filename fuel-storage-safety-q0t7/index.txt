FUEL STORAGE SAFETY
===================

Stored fuel is the most dangerous thing most households keep.
Gasoline gives off ignitable vapor at any temperature you will
ever store it at, and that vapor is heavier than air: it flows
along the floor until it finds a pilot light. Store less than you
think you need, keep it out of the house, and use the right
container.


START HERE
----------

1. FIND EVERY FUEL CONTAINER YOU OWN. Anything kept inside the
   house or basement moves out today: liquid fuel to a detached
   shed or garage, propane cylinders outdoors.
2. CHECK EACH CONTAINER. It must be made and labeled for that
   fuel, with the cap and vent closed tight and no leaks. Replace
   anything improvised: milk jugs, drink bottles, glass, unmarked
   cans.
3. LOOK AT WHAT IS NEAR IT. No water heater, furnace, dryer,
   pilot light, electric motor, or anything else that makes heat
   or sparks.
4. ADD UP THE GALLONS and find your local limit. Fire departments
   commonly cite a limit of about 25 to 30 gallons (95 to 115
   liters) of gasoline at a home. Your fire department's
   non-emergency line can tell you the rule where you live.
5. DATE EVERY CAN with tape and a marker, and put a fire
   extinguisher (rated at least 2-A:10-B:C) where you can reach
   it on the way out, not beside the fuel.


GET EMERGENCY HELP IF
---------------------

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) from a safe distance
if:

- Fuel is burning and the fire is not out within seconds. Get
  everyone out and away. Never put water on burning gasoline,
  diesel, or kerosene.
- A propane cylinder is in or next to a fire. Do not go near it.
  Move everyone far away.
- You smell propane and closing the cylinder valve does not stop
  it, or you cannot reach the valve safely.
- Liquid fuel has spilled indoors, into a basement, or into a
  drain, or more has spilled than you can soak up with a bag of
  cat litter.
- Someone's clothing is on fire. Stop, drop, and roll; cool the
  burn with cool water; call.
- Someone has swallowed fuel and is coughing, choking, drowsy, or
  short of breath. Do NOT make them vomit.
- A carbon monoxide (CO) alarm sounds, or people near a running
  engine or heater get a headache, dizziness, or nausea. Get to
  fresh air first.

Swallowed fuel with no symptoms yet: call Poison Help at
1-800-222-1222 (US, free, 24 hours). Outside the US, use your
local emergency number or poison center.


KNOW YOUR FUELS
---------------

The FLASH POINT is the lowest temperature at which a liquid gives
off enough vapor to ignite. It is the number that explains why
gasoline is in a class of its own.

FUEL        FLASH POINT             WHAT IT MEANS
----------  ----------------------  ----------------------
Gasoline    about -45°F (-43°C)     Ignitable vapor even
                                    in deep cold. Class I
                                    flammable liquid.
Kerosene    100 to 162°F            Must be warmed before
            (38 to 72°C)            it flashes. Class II
                                    combustible liquid.
Diesel      at or above 100°F       Class II combustible
            (38°C)                  liquid, like kerosene.
Propane     a gas at normal         Stored as a liquid
            temperatures            under pressure. Leaks
                                    as a heavy gas.

WHAT ACTUALLY BURNS IS THE VAPOR, NOT THE LIQUID.
- Gasoline vapor and propane are both heavier than air. They
  sink, spread along the ground, and collect in basements, pits,
  and low corners.
- The ignition source does not have to be close. A water heater
  pilot, a furnace, a dryer, or a motor switching on can light
  vapor that has drifted to it.
- An "empty" can is full of vapor. Some fire codes count empty
  containers as full ones.

DIESEL AND KEROSENE ARE SAFER, NOT SAFE. They are harder to
light, but once burning they are every bit as serious, and even a
small amount of gasoline mixed into kerosene sharply raises the
risk of fire or explosion.


CONTAINERS
----------

- USE ONLY CONTAINERS LISTED FOR THAT FUEL. Look for a UL or ASTM
  mark. Listed cans are built to hold vapor and to vent safely as
  the fuel warms.
- 5 GALLONS (19 LITERS) OR SMALLER. That is the largest portable
  can fire codes typically approve for home storage.
- COLOR IS THE CONVENTION, THE LABEL IS THE RULE. In the US, red
  means gasoline and blue means kerosene; diesel cans are usually
  yellow. Never put kerosene in a red can or gasoline in a blue
  one, and write the fuel and the date on every can.
- NEVER glass, drink bottles, milk jugs, buckets, or bags.
  Children drink from drink bottles, and kerosene and lamp oil
  look like water or juice.
- LEAVE THE SAFETY PARTS ALONE. US cans made since 2023 must have
  a flame mitigation device, usually a small screen in the
  opening. It stops a flame outside from flashing back into the
  can. Do not pull it out. Buy cans with child-resistant caps.
- FILL TO THE FILL LINE, NOT THE BRIM. Fuel expands as it warms.
- CLOSE THE CAP AND THE VENT every time, and replace any can that
  is cracked, swollen, rusted, or weeping at the seams.


WHERE TO STORE IT
-----------------

NEVER:
- In the house, the basement, or a crawl space. Not even a small
  amount.
- Near a water heater, furnace, dryer, or any appliance with a
  flame, a hot surface, or a motor.
- In a vehicle.

BEST:
- A detached shed or detached garage that is cool and well
  ventilated.
- Out of direct sun, where a can cannot fall or be hit by a car,
  and away from the door you would escape through.
- Locked, or out of children's reach.

ATTACHED GARAGE: many codes allow a small amount (10 gallons or
38 liters is a common cap), kept well away from the water heater
and furnace. A detached building is safer.

KEEP FUELS APART. Store kerosene away from gasoline so the two
can never be confused. Keep propane cylinders at least 10 feet (3
meters) from liquid fuels.

Apartments, condos, and rented storage units usually have their
own, tighter rules. Ask before you store any fuel there.


HOW MUCH YOU MAY KEEP
---------------------

YOUR LOCAL FIRE CODE DECIDES, and it varies. Examples published
by US fire departments that apply the International Fire Code:

- Gasoline and other flammable liquids: 25 to 30 gallons (95 to
  115 liters) in total, in containers of 5 gallons or less, none
  in the basement, and often no more than 10 gallons in an
  attached garage.
- Diesel, kerosene, and lamp oil: 60 gallons (227 liters) or
  more, outside the dwelling.
- Portable propane cylinders: one department allows about 25
  gallons of total capacity (about five grill-size cylinders),
  stored outside the home, and no more than two 1-pound camping
  cylinders indoors.
- Fuel in the tanks of vehicles and equipment does not count.

These are ceilings, not targets. Every extra gallon is more to
burn. Outside the US, limits, container standards, and can colors
differ: follow your national or local fire service.

WORK OUT WHAT YOU NEED. Example: the generator manual says 8
hours on a 4-gallon tank at half load. Eight hours a day is 4
gallons a day, so 20 gallons lasts 5 days. That is already near
the limit for many homes, which is why it pays to run a generator
in shifts, not around the clock. See the Generator Safety guide.

YOUR CAR IS YOUR BEST GAS CAN. Its tank is sealed, crash-tested,
and does not count toward the limit. Keep it at least half full,
and full when a storm or evacuation is likely. Gas stations often
cannot pump during a power outage.


SHELF LIFE AND ROTATION
-----------------------

GASOLINE GOES STALE. The lightest parts evaporate and the rest
slowly forms varnish that clogs carburetors. Old gasoline can
make an engine hard or impossible to start, which is the last
thing you want from a generator.

- Plan to use stored gasoline within about 6 months.
- A fuel stabilizer, added when the gasoline is fresh, slows the
  decay. Follow the label for the dose and the storage time it
  claims. Stabilizer cannot rescue fuel that has already gone
  stale.
- Rotate. Pour the oldest can into your car's tank and refill the
  can with fresh fuel. A can dated March is due by September.

DIESEL AND KEROSENE: apply the same 6-month rotation unless your
supplier tells you otherwise, and keep containers sealed so water
and dirt stay out. For heaters, buy only water-clear 1-K kerosene
from a dealer who can certify the grade.

PROPANE DOES NOT GO STALE, which makes it the easiest fuel to
keep for years. The cylinder is what ages. In the US a cylinder
must be requalified 12 years after the manufacture date stamped
on its collar, and every 5, 7, or 12 years after that depending
on the test. Refillers will refuse an out-of-date cylinder.
Exchange services handle this for you.

OLD OR CONTAMINATED FUEL goes to a household hazardous waste
collection site. Never pour it down a drain, onto the ground,
into a storm sewer, or put it in the trash.


PROPANE CYLINDERS
-----------------

- OUTDOORS ONLY. Never in the house, basement, crawl space, or
  garage, and that includes small camping cylinders whenever you
  have the choice. Leaking propane sinks and pools indoors until
  something lights it.
- UPRIGHT, on its foot ring, on a firm, level surface. Upright
  keeps the pressure relief valve in vapor, where it is designed
  to work.
- VALVE CLOSED when not in use, with the plug or cap in place.
- AWAY FROM HEAT: out of strong sun where you can, away from
  flames and ignition sources, and not under or beside the grill.
- AWAY FROM DOORS, WINDOWS, VENTS, AND LOW SPOTS where leaking
  gas could drift inside or collect.
- CHECK FOR LEAKS WITH SOAPY WATER (1 part dish soap to 2 parts
  water) brushed on the valve, hose, and connections. Bubbles
  mean a leak. Never test with a flame. If you find a leak, close
  the cylinder valve. If the leak does not stop, move away and
  call the fire department.
- DO NOT USE A DAMAGED CYLINDER: dents, bulges, cuts, heavy rust
  or pitting (look at the bottom), a missing or bent collar or
  foot ring, or a leaking valve.
- TRANSPORT IT UPRIGHT AND SECURED, valve closed and capped, in
  the trunk or truck bed with the vehicle ventilated. Go straight
  home. Never leave a cylinder in a parked car.

A grill-size cylinder is for outdoor appliances. Never bring one
indoors to run a heater or stove. See the Emergency Heating
guide.


FILLING, CARRYING, AND REFUELING
--------------------------------

AT THE PUMP:
1. Engine off. No smoking.
2. Take the can OUT of the vehicle and set it on the ground, away
   from the vehicle. A can in a truck bed, on a bed liner, or on
   carpet is insulated, so static charge builds up as fuel flows
   and can spark at the nozzle.
3. Touch the nozzle to the can before you open it.
4. Keep the nozzle in contact with the rim of the can the whole
   time you fill. Fill slowly, to the fill line.
5. Cap it tight and wipe off any drips before loading.

IN THE VEHICLE:
- Carry only a small amount, in a listed can, closed and sealed.
- Upright and tied down so it cannot tip or slide.
- In the trunk or truck bed, never in the passenger compartment,
  with windows open for ventilation.
- Unload it as soon as you arrive. Touch the metal of the vehicle
  before you lift the can out, to discharge static.

REFUELING A GENERATOR, MOWER, OR HEATER:
- Outdoors only.
- TURN IT OFF AND LET IT COOL FIRST. Fuel spilled on a hot engine
  or muffler can ignite, and engines stay hot long after they
  stop. Never refuel a running or hot generator, however much you
  want the lights back.
- Use a funnel or the can's spout. Do not overfill.
- Wipe up spills and move the can well away before you restart.
- At night use a battery light, never a candle, lighter, or fuel
  lantern.
- Kerosene heaters: refuel outside the home, off and cool, and
  never above the "full" mark.

NEVER:
- Use gasoline to start or revive a fire, a grill, or a fire pit.
  The flame can flash back into the can and shoot burning fuel
  out of the opening like a blowtorch.
- Use gasoline as a cleaner, solvent, or degreaser.
- Siphon by mouth. Fuel that reaches the lungs can cause
  pneumonia and can kill.
- Wear fuel-soaked clothes. Take them off, let them dry outdoors
  away from heat, then wash them.


SPILLS
------

1. Stop the source if it is safe, and keep people, pets, flames,
   and running engines away.
2. Outdoors, cover a small spill with cat litter, sand, or
   another absorbent. Sweep it up and take it to a household
   hazardous waste site.
3. Do not hose fuel away. That spreads it into soil, drains, and
   waterways.
4. Indoors, or any spill you cannot handle with a bag of cat
   litter: leave, and call the fire department.


FIREWOOD
--------

- Season split wood for at least 6 months before burning. It
  burns best below 20 percent moisture.
- Store it outdoors, off the ground, with only the top covered.
  Covering the sides traps moisture.
- Keep the main stack away from the house, not against a wall or
  under a deck.
- Never start a wood fire with gasoline or kerosene.


FUEL STORAGE CHECKLIST
----------------------

[ ] No fuel inside the house, basement, or crawl space
[ ] Liquid fuel in a detached shed or garage, cool and ventilated
[ ] Listed containers, 5 gallons or less, labeled and dated
[ ] Caps and vents closed; no cracked or leaking cans
[ ] Nothing that makes heat or sparks nearby
[ ] Total quantity within the local fire code
[ ] Kerosene apart from gasoline; propane 10 feet from both
[ ] Propane cylinders outdoors, upright, valves closed, in date
[ ] Gasoline rotated within about 6 months
[ ] Fire extinguisher (2-A:10-B:C or larger) on the way out
[ ] Cat litter or other absorbent on hand for spills
[ ] Fuel locked away from children


PRACTICE
--------

10-MINUTE DRILL:
Walk your property with a notepad and do a fuel census. For every
container write down the fuel, the size, the date, and the
nearest thing that could light it. Add up the gallons. Then find
the date stamped on the collar of your propane cylinder (month
and year, such as 04 19) and add 12 years. Finish by timing how
long it takes to put your hand on the fire extinguisher from
where the fuel is kept. Do not open or move any containers for
this drill.

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

1. A hurricane is three days out. At the gas station your
   neighbor is filling six 5-gallon cans lined up in the back of
   his SUV, and says he will just leave them in the car in the
   garage until the storm passes.

   ANSWER: Three problems. Cans must come out and sit on the
   ground to be filled; inside a carpeted vehicle, static can
   spark at the nozzle. Fuel should never ride in the passenger
   compartment or be stored in a vehicle. And 30 gallons parked
   in an attached garage is at or over the limit in many places,
   often a few feet from a water heater. The better plan: fill
   the car's own tank, fill fewer cans on the ground, and store
   them in a detached shed.

2. It is 10 p.m. on the second night of an outage, raining, and
   the generator has just run dry. The family wants the power
   back. The engine is ticking hot and you have a full can and a
   flashlight.

   ANSWER: Wait. Refueling a hot generator is how outage fires
   start: fuel hits the muffler and ignites in your hands. Leave
   it off until it has cooled down, use the flashlight and never
   a flame, refuel outdoors, wipe any spill, and move the can
   away before restarting. Next time, top it up at a set time in
   daylight and shut it down before bed.

3. A hard freeze is coming. You plan to carry the spare grill
   cylinder down to the basement so it stays warm and does not
   get stolen.

   ANSWER: Leave it outside. Cold does not harm a propane
   cylinder, but a slow leak indoors does harm you: propane is
   heavier than air, so it pools on the basement floor until the
   furnace or water heater lights it. Keep it upright outdoors,
   away from doors and windows, and chain it to something if
   theft worries you.


QUICK REFERENCE
---------------

STORE:
- Never in the house or basement
- Detached shed or garage, away from heat and sparks
- Listed cans, 5 gal or less, labeled and dated
- Red = gasoline, blue = kerosene, yellow = diesel
- Check your local limit (often 25-30 gal gasoline)
- Use gasoline within about 6 months

PROPANE:
- Outdoors only, upright, valve closed
- Leak check with soapy water, never a flame

FILLING AND REFUELING:
- Can on the ground, nozzle touching the rim
- Trunk or truck bed, tied down, unload at once
- Engine OFF and COOL before refueling
- Never gasoline on a fire or as a cleaner

EMERGENCY:
- Fuel fire: get out, call 911, never water
- Propane smell you can't stop: get away, call 911
- Swallowed fuel: do NOT induce vomiting.
  Poison Help (US) 1-800-222-1222
